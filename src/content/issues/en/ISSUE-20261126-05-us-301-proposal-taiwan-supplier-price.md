# Can a proposed U.S. tariff change the price in your Taiwan supply contract?

On November 25, the [Office of the U.S. Trade Representative (USTR)](https://ustr.gov/about/policy-offices/press-office/press-releases/2026/november/ustr-makes-determinations-and-proposes-actions-14-section-301-investigations-relating-structural) determined that certain manufacturing-related practices in 14 economies, including Taiwan, are actionable. It proposed responsive tariffs and opened a comment process. No final duty has been set under that announcement.

Consider a U.S. buyer whose Taiwan supplier asks to revise next year's price because of the proposal. The announcement gives neither party a new contract price by itself. A determination about trade practices and a duty payable on a shipment are different stages.

## What the Taiwan notice actually proposes

The [Taiwan Federal Register notice](https://ustr.gov/sites/default/files/files/Press/Releases/2026/Taiwan%20FRN.pdf) considers tariffs of up to 15 percent on goods of Taiwan, net of most-favored-nation rates and existing Section 301 tariffs, with specified exclusions. It also describes a possible alternative of up to 25 percent if ongoing consultations and commitments prove unsatisfactory. Neither figure is a tariff to add mechanically to every invoice today.

Written comments are due January 5, 2027; hearings are scheduled to begin January 25. Product coverage and the final action remain open. The notice does not answer whether a particular shipment is a good of Taiwan or falls within an exclusion. That requires its production and U.S. customs records.

## The customs bill and the sales price

[U.S. Customs and Border Protection](https://www.help.cbp.gov/s/article/Article-1894?language=en_US) says the importer of record remains responsible for duties, taxes and fees on an entry, even when a broker files it. Who ultimately absorbs that cost between a U.S. buyer and a Taiwan supplier is a contract question. The supplier's address, by itself, does not answer either question.

Which law governs the sale? [Article 20 of Taiwan's choice-of-law statute](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=B0000007&flno=20) gives effect to the parties' choice of law for contractual obligations. Without a valid express choice, it points to the law most closely connected with the transaction. Taiwan law does not apply automatically just because the supplier operates there.

Where Taiwan law does govern, [Civil Code Article 153](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=B0000001&flno=153) starts with the parties' agreement. Read the accepted purchase order and any clause on duties, taxes or price revision. If the parties negotiate a change, they can identify the shipments and effective date it covers. The proposal itself supplies no agreed new price.

## An existing contract has another, narrower route

[Civil Code Article 227-2](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=B0000001&flno=227-2) permits a party to ask a court to increase or decrease performance, or change another contractual effect, where circumstances change after formation in a way that could not then have been anticipated and the original effect becomes manifestly unfair. It is a request to a court, subject to those conditions. A press announcement cannot silently rewrite an order.

The contract date matters. So do the risks the parties allocated and the actual effect of any final U.S. measure. For now, a buyer and supplier can keep the proposed tariff separate from charges already paid and record any conditional price agreement against identifiable orders.
