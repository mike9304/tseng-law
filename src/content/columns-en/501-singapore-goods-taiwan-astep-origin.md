Goods shipped from Singapore do not automatically qualify for ASTEP tariff treatment in Taiwan. A Singapore invoice or port of departure alone does not establish origin. For a regional trader, importing finished goods and reselling them through Singapore can therefore leave a gap between the price promised to the Taiwan customer and the duty payable. [Singapore Customs warns against declaring unqualified re-exports as Singapore-origin goods.](https://www.customs.gov.sg/doing-business/rules-of-origin/origin-documentation/origin-documentation-overview/)

ASTEP is the economic partnership agreement between Singapore and the Separate Customs Territory of Taiwan, Penghu, Kinmen and Matsu. It entered into force on 19 April 2014. Before pricing an order on the basis of a tariff saving, establish whether the particular goods qualify and which rate applies. The agreement's tariff schedule includes different categories; it is not a promise that every shipment enters duty-free. [Enterprise Singapore publishes the agreement and tariff information.](https://www.enterprisesg.gov.sg/industries/wholesale-trade/astep)

## Match the rule to the product and its production

For goods made using non-originating materials, the applicable product-specific rule may concern a change in tariff classification, regional value content or a specified process. Check the rule for the actual product in Annex 4B, together with the other applicable conditions. Goods wholly obtained in a party, or made entirely from qualifying originating materials, have different routes under [Article 4.2 of ASTEP](https://www.enterprisesg.gov.sg/-/media/esg/files/industries/wholesale-trade/ASTEP/astepchapter204rules20of20origin.pdf).

Start with the product specification, proposed Harmonised System classification, manufacturing locations and production steps. Ask the producer for the input and cost records needed for the relevant rule. A supplier's description of a product as “Singapore-made” is not a substitute for that assessment.

Repacking, changing packaging or attaching labels, alone or in combination, does not confer origin under Article 4.4. A trader should therefore establish what actually happened to the goods in Singapore before giving an origin commitment. If materials or production sites change between orders, arrange a fresh check rather than carrying the earlier conclusion into the next shipment.

Confirm the transport route as well. Where goods pass through another country, have the logistics team check ASTEP's transit conditions and the supporting documents before dispatch.

## Agree who can support the declaration

ASTEP uses self-certification. Singapore Customs does not issue a preferential Certificate of Origin under this regime. The Singapore exporter or producer completes and signs the required Declaration of Origin in English, using the prescribed template. Where a declaration is required, an ordinary invoice without it is insufficient. [Singapore Customs' self-certification guidance](https://www.customs.gov.sg/doing-business/rules-of-origin/origin-documentation/self-certification/) links to the [ASTEP circular and declaration template](https://isomer-user-content.by.gov.sg/174/617bdb46-7560-482c-8c2e-46e07cb3f11e/2014-04-03-Circular062014.pdf).

A non-manufacturing exporter can sign on the bases allowed by Article 4.15, including reasonable reliance on the producer's written representation of qualifying origin. That makes the upstream supply agreement relevant: obtain a product-specific commitment, identify who holds the manufacturing records and agree how those records will be made available for verification.

Do not promise the Taiwan buyer unrestricted access to a manufacturer's confidential cost data without arranging that access upstream. Agree a lawful disclosure process that enables responses to customs while protecting confidential information where permitted. Name a contact who can retrieve the records after the sales team has moved on to other orders.

## The Taiwan importer has a separate filing task

Taiwan's [ASTEP import-clearance directions](https://law-out.mof.gov.tw/LawContent.aspx?id=GL009764) require the importer claiming preference to enter “PT” in the specified classification-code suffix field and provide the Declaration of Origin, subject to the low-value exception. The declaration applies to a single importation and is valid for one year from signing. Agree a document-delivery date with the importer and its customs broker before shipment.

The importer must retain the relevant origin and import documents for five years from the day after release. Singapore Customs' [current origin-documentation guidance](https://www.customs.gov.sg/doing-business/rules-of-origin/origin-documentation/origin-documentation-overview/) also calls for supporting records to be kept for at least five years. Do not treat the treaty's shorter minimum as permission to discard records required under domestic rules.

Taiwan customs may seek information from the importer, exporter or producer. The clearance directions prescribe a 30-day response period from receipt of the request, with one written extension request within that period for no more than 30 additional days. Failure to respond in time can lead to refusal of preference. Build prompt forwarding of customs correspondence into the commercial arrangements.

## Price the risk of a refused claim

A clause saying “ASTEP eligible” leaves several questions unanswered. Does the seller warrant origin for each shipment, or promise only to supply specified supporting documents? Is the price conditional on the buyer obtaining preference? Who bears additional duty if the manufacturer's information is wrong, and who bears it if the buyer misses the claiming procedure?

Address those events separately. The agreement can allocate responsibility for accurate origin information, document delivery, notification of changes and cooperation with verification. A reimbursement clause should identify the triggering failure, covered costs, evidence required and payment date. State how it interacts with any liability cap and who decides whether to challenge an adverse customs decision. These are terms to negotiate, not automatic entitlements under ASTEP.

Contractual reimbursement does not determine whom customs may require to pay. [Article 6 of Taiwan's Customs Act](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawAll.aspx?pcode=G0350001) identifies the duty-payer by statutory categories, including the consignee and holders of the bill of lading or goods. A private allocation of costs cannot make non-qualifying goods eligible or bind customs to the parties' preferred tariff treatment.
