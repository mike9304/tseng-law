# Running a US SaaS or online business with Taiwan users: personal data rules and the 2025 PDPA transition

Hosting every server in Virginia or Oregon does not place a US SaaS vendor outside Taiwan's Personal Data Protection Act (個人資料保護法, PDPA). [Article 51, paragraph 2](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=I0050021&flno=51) applies the statute to non-government agencies collecting, processing, or using Republic of China (Taiwan) nationals' personal data outside ROC territory. Any US platform, e-commerce brand, or enterprise vendor handling Taiwan user or employee records needs its notices, opt-outs, and security controls aligned with Taiwan law.

As of November 24, 2026, Taiwan's Laws and Regulations Database marks the [November 11, 2025 PDPA amendments](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawAll.aspx?pcode=I0050021) as not yet in force. Those amendments restructure security duties, expand breach reporting, and shift enforcement to a centralized regulator, awaiting an Executive Yuan effective date under Article 56, paragraph 1. Until then, the [May 31, 2023 text](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawOldVer.aspx?pcode=I0050021) governs security maintenance (Article 27) and breach notice (Article 12). Its Article 48 fines, up to NT$15 million, have applied since promulgation in May 2023. US privacy rules require separate US review.

## Overseas servers still fall under Taiwan's Personal Data Protection Act

[Article 2](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=I0050021&flno=2) defines covered records broadly: names, birth dates, National ID and passport numbers, physical traits, fingerprints, marital status, family, education, occupation, medical records, healthcare, genetic, sex-life and health-check data, criminal history, contact details, financial status, social activities, and any other data directly or indirectly identifying a natural person. Every private business qualifies as a non-government agency.

Cloud operations engage three statutory terms immediately. Collection means obtaining personal data by any means; processing covers storing, editing, copying, retrieving, deleting, linking, or internally transmitting data for a personal data file; international transmission means cross-border processing or use. Under Article 4 of the [May 31, 2023 text](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawOldVer.aspx?pcode=I0050021), a vendor entrusted by a Taiwan client to process personal data is treated as the commissioning organization within the Act's scope. Only household activities and audio-visual recordings made in public that are not combined with other personal data sit outside the Act (Article 51, paragraph 1). Platforms billing Taiwan consumers also face separate tax registration rules, covered in [Taiwan VAT registration for foreign digital services](/en/columns/taiwan-vat-foreign-digital-services-registration).

## Notice before collection, lawful grounds, and marketing opt-outs

A privacy policy written only for US law may not cover Taiwan's six upfront disclosures. Collecting directly from an individual requires clear notice under [Article 8, paragraph 1](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=I0050021&flno=8):

1. The company's name.
2. Collection purposes.
3. Personal data categories.
4. Period, territory, recipients, and method of use.
5. Article 3 rights (querying or viewing records, requesting copies, supplementing or correcting data, stopping collection, processing, or use, and requesting deletion) plus how to exercise them.
6. Where provision is optional, how declining affects the individual's rights.

Watch territory under item 4. Routing Taipei checkout profiles or staff rosters to US cloud regions belongs in that notice; narrow Article 8, paragraph 2 exemptions rarely fit commercial software sign-ups.

Indirect collection adds its own step. A US data platform buys enriched B2B leads from a broker, or reuses a Taipei client's staff data for its own analytics (an invented example). [Article 9](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=I0050021&flno=9) requires notifying data subjects of the source plus items 1 through 5 of Article 8, paragraph 1 before processing or use (or upon first use toward the individual under paragraph 3), absent an exemption such as data lawfully made public. A vendor that only processes on the client's instructions is treated as the client under Article 4, so the contract should settle who gives which notice.

Notice alone is never enough. For ordinary data, [Article 19](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=I0050021&flno=19) requires a specific purpose plus one of eight statutory grounds. Commercial software typically relies on a contractual or quasi-contractual relationship paired with appropriate security measures (item 2), or consent (item 5). Under Article 7 of the [May 31, 2023 text](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawOldVer.aspx?pcode=I0050021), initial consent is presumed when users submit data without objecting after clear Article 8 notice. Even then, the collector bears the burden of proof.

Secondary uses and marketing carry tighter limits. [Article 20, paragraph 1](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=I0050021&flno=20) confines use to the collection purpose unless an exception applies, such as separate consent under item 6 after disclosing the new purpose, scope, and impact (Article 7, paragraph 2). Under Article 20, paragraphs 2 and 3, marketing must stop immediately upon refusal, and the first marketing outreach must provide an opt-out at the company's expense.

## Six sensitive data categories and cross-border transfers

Can a US health app or screening platform collect Taiwan medical or criminal records through standard clickwrap terms? Not on generic terms of service alone. [Article 6](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=I0050021&flno=6) bans collecting, processing, or using six exact categories (medical records, healthcare, genetics, sex life, health checkups, and criminal records) unless an exception applies. Apart from the statute-based exceptions in items 1, 2, and 5 (an express legal provision, or what is necessary to perform or assist a statutory duty with appropriate security measures) and data the individual has made public or that is otherwise lawfully public (item 3), a private operator needs written consent under item 6 (within the necessary scope of the specific purpose, not barred by another law, and not against the individual's will) plus Article 8 or 9 notices. The research exception in item 4 is limited to public agencies and academic research institutions doing medical, health, or crime-prevention statistics or research with de-identified data.

Cross-border transfers stay open by default, subject to four statutory brakes. Under Article 21 of the [May 31, 2023 text](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawOldVer.aspx?pcode=I0050021), sending Taiwan data to US servers needs no blanket pre-approval, though the central industry authority may restrict transfers for major national interests, special treaty rules, inadequate recipient-country privacy laws threatening data subjects' rights, or indirect routing to evade the Act. The pending [November 11, 2025 amendment to Article 21](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=I0050021&flno=21) keeps those four grounds while shifting authority to the centralized regulator once effective.

## Currently in force vs. the pending November 2025 amendments

Product and legal teams must track both live rules today and the November 11, 2025 amendments ahead. As of November 24, 2026, three duties define that shift:

| Compliance area | Currently in force (May 31, 2023 text) | Promulgated Nov. 11, 2025 (effective date pending) |
|---|---|---|
| Security maintenance | Art. 27: appropriate security measures; sector authorities may mandate security plans and post-termination disposal methods | Art. 27 deleted; replaced by Art. 20-1 unified rules and Art. 51-1 six-year transitional sector oversight (reviewed every two years) |
| Breach notification | Art. 12: notify data subjects appropriately after clarifying facts when a statutory violation causes theft, leakage, alteration, or infringement | Amended Art. 12: notify data subjects upon awareness; report covered incidents to the competent authority; take immediate containment measures and preserve records |
| Administrative fines | Art. 47: NT$50,000–NT$500,000; Art. 48(1): cure order first; NT$20,000–NT$200,000 per violation only if not corrected by the deadline (notice, data-subject rights, marketing opt-out); Art. 48(2)–(3): NT$20,000–NT$2,000,000 for Art. 27 failures, and NT$150,000–NT$15,000,000 for uncured or serious Art. 27 breaches | Amended Arts. 47–48: centralized enforcement, immediate NT$20,000–NT$200,000 fines for Art. 12 reporting/containment/record failures, and NT$2M/NT$15M tiers under Arts. 20-1 and 51-1 |

Under the [May 31, 2023 text](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawOldVer.aspx?pcode=I0050021), Article 48's heightened security penalties are active now under Article 56, paragraph 2. Breaching Article 27's security duty or omitting a sector-mandated security plan carries an immediate fine of NT$20,000 to NT$2,000,000 plus a cure order. If the violation goes uncured past the deadline, successive fines rise to NT$150,000 to NT$15,000,000; serious breaches draw that higher range immediately.

Once the amendments take effect on the date the Executive Yuan sets—deleting [Article 27](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=I0050021&flno=27) and updating [Article 47](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=I0050021&flno=47) and [Article 48](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=I0050021&flno=48)—breach duties widen sharply. Amended Article 12 triggers upon awareness of theft, alteration, damage, loss, or leakage without establishing a prior statutory violation, adding regulatory reporting for covered incidents, immediate containment, and record preservation. Under new Article 51-1, for a range of businesses announced by the Executive Yuan, sector regulators keep oversight for up to six years after the Personal Data Protection Commission is established, may require security plans from them, and may set stricter standards. See [cyber incidents and personal data scope in Taiwan](/en/columns/taiwan-foreign-company-cyber-incident-personal-data-scope) for local incident scoping.

## Civil damages, executive exposure, and building compliance now

Regulatory fines sit alongside civil and personal liability. Under [Article 29](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=I0050021&flno=29), a non-government agency whose violation leads to unlawful collection, processing, or use of personal data, or otherwise infringes the individual's rights, owes civil damages unless it proves it acted without intent or negligence. Incorporating Article 28, paragraphs 2 through 6 of the [May 31, 2023 text](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawOldVer.aspx?pcode=I0050021), courts may award non-pecuniary damages, assess NT$500 to NT$20,000 per person per event when actual loss is hard to prove, and cap mass claims at NT$200 million unless the interests involved exceed that cap.

Executives carry personal risk too. Article 50 imposes a fine in the same amount on the representative, manager, or authorized officer whenever the company is fined under Articles 47 through 49, unless that officer proves fulfillment of prevention duties. Article 41 adds imprisonment of up to five years, with an optional fine of up to NT$1,000,000, for intentional violations of Articles 6(1), 19, 20(1), or an Article 21 transfer order committed for unlawful gain or harm where damage is likely.
