# Who Is Liable If an Accident Happens While Overtaking???

![Featured image](../images/012-taiwan-overtaking-accident-liability/featured-01.jpg)

When the vehicle ahead is moving too slowly,

many people choose to overtake.

But overtaking is dangerous,

so there are traffic rules that govern it.

For example, it is common knowledge that you cannot overtake on the right and cannot overtake across a double yellow line.

But Taiwan has one overtaking rule that

even Taiwanese drivers who have been driving for decades may not know.

That rule is: when you overtake,

you must 「warn the vehicle ahead」 and

you may overtake only after obtaining the vehicle ahead's consent.

The following vehicle must first 「**sound the horn twice**」 or 「**flash the headlights once**」

to signal its intention to overtake to the vehicle ahead.

Only when the vehicle ahead slows down or moves aside to yield

may the following vehicle overtake.

That is the act of warning the vehicle ahead.

But today, most people have no habit of warning the vehicle ahead when overtaking.

If they simply think the vehicle in front is too slow,

they immediately move into the left lane, accelerate hard,

and after passing the vehicle in front,

return to the original lane.

Not long ago, I handled a case in which

A was riding a motorcycle with friend B as a passenger on a mountain road.

There were two passenger cars ahead of A's motorcycle, and

the first vehicle in front (Vehicle 1) was traveling slowly.

As a result, the second vehicle (Vehicle 2) and the motorcycle were also moving very slowly.

![](../images/012-taiwan-overtaking-accident-liability/img-01.jpg)

A tried to overtake the two vehicles ahead and

entered the oncoming lane and accelerated.

However,

Vehicle 2 also could not tolerate Vehicle 1's slow speed and

turned on its turn signal and, in less than one second, entered the oncoming lane to prepare to overtake.

At that moment, the motorcycle was going too fast to have time to brake and

collided directly with Vehicle 2.

B, who was riding on the back of the motorcycle, suffered a severe head injury and died at the scene, and

motorcycle rider A lost consciousness at the scene and was taken to the hospital.

Even after treatment, lasting physical aftereffects remained.

The families of A and B believed that Vehicle 2's sudden lane change was the main cause of the collision.

​

As a result, the matter went to litigation, and

after multiple traffic-accident appraisals, the liability ratios were determined, and

motorcycle rider A was found to be the main cause of the accident.

​

The main reason was that

although Vehicle 2 suddenly entered the oncoming lane,

A had not notified the vehicles ahead of the intention to overtake,

so Vehicle 2 could not anticipate that someone was overtaking from the rear.

Viewed overall, therefore, A's percentage of fault was higher, and A was judged to be the main cause of the accident.

A had high insurance coverage and did not suffer major financial harm, but

lived with physical aftereffects and the pain of losing a friend, and

had to bear responsibility as the main cause of the accident—

a weight that will likely torment A forever.

Even if most people do not follow the rule,

when overtaking it is better to comply with the traffic regulations.

First warn the vehicle ahead, and

overtake only when the vehicle ahead shows an intention to yield.

That way, if an unfortunate accident does occur,

you can avoid bearing an excessive share of accident liability.
