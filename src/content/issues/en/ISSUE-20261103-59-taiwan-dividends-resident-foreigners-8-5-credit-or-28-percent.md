# Living in Taiwan and collecting Taiwan dividends: the 8.5% credit or the 28% separate rate?

NT$80,000 is the most a Taiwan household can take off its income tax each year through the dividend credit. For a foreign engineer in Hsinchu who has built up Taiwan blue chips and an ETF or two, that cap and a flat 28% alternative are the two numbers that matter when the return comes due in May.

Two things decide the outcome: whether you are a Taiwan tax resident for the year, and which bracket the rest of your household income falls into.

## First, resident or not

[Article 7 of the Income Tax Act](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=G0340003&flno=7) treats a person without a domicile in Taiwan as a resident for a tax year once they have stayed in Taiwan for 183 days in total during that year. Everyone else in that position is a nonresident for the year.

The difference shows up at the payment stage. [Article 2 of the Standards of Withholding Rates for Various Incomes](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=G0340028&flno=2), which lists withholding for residents, has no item for company dividends. That is why a resident's dividend usually lands in full, apart from any National Health Insurance supplementary premium. A nonresident's dividend is withheld at 21% under [Article 3, subparagraph 1](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=G0340028&flno=3), and that generally settles the Taiwan tax. A tax treaty can lower the rate for a resident of the treaty country who provides proof of residence, so check the agreement with your home country.

## The two routes for a resident household

[Article 15 of the Income Tax Act](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=G0340003&flno=15) sets them out.

Under paragraph 4, the household adds its dividends to consolidated income and computes tax at the ordinary progressive rates. It then takes a credit equal to 8.5% of the total dividends against the tax payable, capped at NT$80,000 per household per year. The Ministry of Finance's [tax Q&A](https://www.etax.nat.gov.tw/etwmain/tax-info/understanding/tax-q-and-a/national/individual-income-tax/taxation-scope/which-income/YmDoYM2) adds two points. Any credit left over after the tax is covered is refunded, and dividends of up to about NT$940,000 a year get the full 8.5%.

Under paragraph 5, the household can instead tax all of its dividends separately at 28%. That amount is paid with the same return, and the 8.5% credit no longer applies. The choice covers the household's dividends as a whole, so within one return the two spouses cannot split routes. Spouses who live apart and file separately under paragraph 1 have separate returns, and each return makes its own choice.

## A rough way to compare

For dividends within the credit cap, the first route costs your top marginal rate minus 8.5 percentage points. The second costs a flat 28%.

The Ministry of Finance published the 2026 brackets on [November 27, 2025](https://gazette.nat.gov.tw/EG_FileManager/eguploadpub/eg031224/ch04/type3/gov30/num12/Eg.htm). Net consolidated income up to NT$610,000 is taxed at 5%, and the rate steps up through 12%, 20% and 30% to 40% on the part above NT$5,190,000. So the arithmetic looks like this:

- In the 5% bracket, the 8.5% credit is larger than the tax on the dividends, and the excess offsets other tax or is refunded.
- In the 12%, 20% and 30% brackets, the first route costs roughly 3.5%, 11.5% or 21.5%, all under 28%.
- In the 40% bracket, the first route costs about 31.5%, and the 28% route becomes cheaper.

A worked case shows the gap. Take a household whose net income without dividends is NT$6 million, already in the 40% bracket, with NT$500,000 of dividends. Adding the dividends costs NT$200,000 of tax, less a NT$42,500 credit, for NT$157,500. Taxing them separately costs NT$140,000.

Move the same dividends to a household with NT$2 million of other net income, where the extra NT$500,000 stays inside the 20% bracket. Now the first route costs NT$100,000 less NT$42,500, or NT$57,500, against NT$140,000 at 28%.

A real filing is messier. Dividends can push income across a bracket line, and above about NT$940,000 the credit stops growing, so a very large dividend year can tip even a 30% household toward the 28% route. Running the return both ways before filing is the reliable test.

## Arrival and departure years

Every January, the 183-day count starts again. If you arrive in August, you cannot reach it that year, and the dividends paid that year are withheld at 21% as a nonresident.

Leaving is the other trap. Under [Article 71-1, paragraph 2](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=G0340003&flno=71-1), a resident who gives up a Taiwan domicile or residence and leaves during the year must file and pay for that year before departing. If the spouse is a resident and stays on in Taiwan, the spouse files the joint return under Article 71 instead. Dividends received up to that point are part of that return, and the 8.5% or 28% choice is made there. Our column on [departure filing and tax agents](/en/columns/taiwan-departure-income-tax-agent-article-72) covers the process.

This applies only if you are a resident for that year. Without a domicile, a stay of under 183 days makes you a nonresident, and the withholding on each dividend becomes that year's Taiwan tax, with no 8.5% or 28% choice. The exit date matters.
