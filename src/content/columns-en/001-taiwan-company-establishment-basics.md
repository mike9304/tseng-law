# Setting Up a Company in Taiwan: Subsidiaries, Branches, Representative Offices, Procedures, and Work Permits

![Taiwan company formation overview](../images/001-taiwan-company-establishment-basics/featured-01.jpg)

![Documents for a Taiwan investment and company formation](../images/001-taiwan-company-establishment-basics/img-01.jpg)

Entering Taiwan is not a single filing exercise. The appropriate legal structure depends on how the business will earn revenue, which entity will contract with customers and suppliers, where liability should sit, how funds will enter and leave Taiwan, who will perform the work, and what premises the operation will use. A structure that works for a sales company may be unsuitable for a liaison team, a regulated service, or a project delivered by personnel from abroad.

Company formation, foreign-investment review, banking, tax registration, compliance requirements for business premises, work authorization, and residence are related, but each serves a different legal purpose. Company registration does not substitute for any required foreign-investment review or industry licensing. Investment approval does not obligate any bank to open an account. Share ownership does not authorize a foreign national to work, and a work permit does not itself grant residence status.

Planning should therefore begin with the operating facts: the products or services, expected counterparties, contract and payment flows, staffing, regulated activities, and intended address. Those facts help determine the entity, the documents, the responsible agencies, and a realistic sequence. The overview below explains the principal choices and procedures, but the requirements for a particular investment must be confirmed based on the investor’s nationality, ownership structure, industry, location, and proposed operations.

## 1. Choosing a Taiwan Presence: Subsidiary, Branch, or Representative Office

A Taiwan subsidiary (limited company or company limited by shares) is a separate legal entity under Taiwan law. A Taiwan branch of a foreign company is not a separate legal entity; it operates in Taiwan as part of the foreign company. A representative office may not conduct profit-making business in Taiwan; its activities are limited to liaison work and legal acts on behalf of the foreign company. Liability, tax treatment, licensing requirements, and eligibility for government procurement must be evaluated based on the chosen structure and the circumstances.

A Taiwan subsidiary contracts, owns property, employs personnel, and assumes obligations in its own name. A limited company and a company limited by shares differ in their ownership instruments, governance, decision-making procedures, and fundraising options. Separate-entity status is important, but it does not answer every liability question. Parent guarantees, security arrangements, related-party contracts, director duties, and the way the enterprise is actually operated can still affect risk allocation.

A branch extends the foreign head office’s business into Taiwan. The branch has no shareholders of its own, and the foreign head office bears the branch’s obligations because the branch is not a separate legal entity. Its Taiwan working capital, accounting, tax treatment, and remittance of earnings must be analyzed as a branch arrangement, rather than assumed to follow the dividend mechanics of a subsidiary.

A representative office is suitable only for a limited non-revenue role. It may conduct liaison activities, gather information, support negotiations, and perform legal acts for the foreign company within the permitted scope. It cannot make ordinary sales or provide paid services in Taiwan. If the actual activity includes accepting orders, receiving customer payments, regularly delivering services, or otherwise operating for profit, a subsidiary or branch may be required regardless of the name used for the office.

The structure analysis must go beyond labels. Counsel and the business team should compare liability, capital and funding, profit distribution and remittance, accounting and tax, employment, licensing, and eligibility for public procurement. A tender or regulated activity may require a Taiwan legal entity, specified capital, local experience, licensed professionals, or particular registrations. None of those questions is resolved merely by choosing the word “subsidiary,” “branch,” or “representative office.”

Cross-border arrangements may also engage the Taiwan–Korea Income Tax Agreement. The agreement entered into force on December 27, 2023, and applies from January 1, 2024. For income that satisfies its conditions, the maximum source-country rate for dividends, interest, and royalties is 10%. Business profits generally require a separate permanent establishment (PE) analysis and an attribution of profit to any qualifying PE.

That analysis includes four potentially independent PE categories: fixed facilities such as a place of management, branch, or office; a building site, construction, assembly, or installation project lasting more than six months; services performed for more than 183 aggregate days in any 12-month period; and an agent who repeatedly exercises authority to conclude contracts for the enterprise. Each category has its own conditions. A business may have a PE through a fixed place, project, or dependent agent even when the service-day threshold is not met, so neither PE status nor source-country taxation of business profits can be reduced to a count of service days.

## 2. Key Steps in Forming a Taiwan Subsidiary

The following is a qualified overview of a common sequence for forming a foreign-invested company. It is not a universal checklist, and the order, necessity, and timing of individual steps may change depending on the entity form; the amount and structure of the investment; the industry; the scope of agency review; the bank’s procedures; any requested corrections; and whether the investor is an individual or an entity and, correspondingly, the individual’s nationality or the entity’s jurisdiction of organization.

1. Preliminary review and reservation of the company’s Chinese name and registered business activities

2. Notarization or authentication of foreign documents, including powers of attorney, and Taiwan overseas-office authentication where required

3. Foreign-investment application to the Department of Investment Review, Ministry of Economic Affairs (MOEA), where applicable

4. Opening a preparatory bank account

5. Remittance of foreign investment funds

6. Investment amount verification (投資額審定)

7. Company registration

8. Tax registration

9. Conversion of the preparatory account into a regular company account

10. Additional import-export, industry-license, work-permit, or residence procedures, where applicable

Review of the proposed company name and registered business activities is an early step in the registration process. Approval at that stage does not mean that the business has obtained a separate operating license or that its proposed premises are lawful for the intended activity. When foreign-investment review applies, the identities of the investors, ownership percentages, investment amount and method, funding purpose, and business plan should remain consistent across the application and supporting documents.

Documents issued abroad may include powers of attorney, certificates evidencing a legal entity’s existence, evidence of incumbency or a representative’s authority, and shareholder materials. Depending on the issuing jurisdiction, the document, and its intended use, the required formalities may include notarization, authentication in the issuing jurisdiction, and further authentication by a Taiwan overseas office. Names, signatures, authority, and translations should be consistent across the Taiwan filings. The investor’s nationality and status as an individual or entity can materially affect the required evidence and authentication route.

Opening the preparatory bank account is also a compliance process, not a ministerial formality. A bank may examine the investor, beneficial ownership, source of funds, intended transactions, and business model as part of its customer due diligence. Differences among the approved investor, remitter, remittance purpose, and receiving account can result in questions or correction requests. After the funds are remitted, investment amount verification confirms the amount of qualifying investment. Once that verification is complete, the process can proceed to company and tax registration and, subject to the bank’s requirements, conversion of the preparatory account into a regular company account.

Some steps occur in parallel, and some applications may be needed before or after company registration. Import-export registration, product or factory approvals, professional staffing, sector licenses, work permits, and residence applications all have their own conditions. A proposed opening date should account for these downstream requirements rather than treating the company-registration date as the end of the project.

## 3. Checking Business Activities and Premises Before Formation

Many activities are open to foreign investment, but prohibited or restricted industries must be screened separately. The proposed operation may also require professional qualifications, an industry license, product approval, facility approval, or designated personnel. Medical devices, alcohol, travel, construction, imports, and professional services illustrate areas where the governing rules may depend on the actual product, service, contract, and distribution model.

Registered business activities describe what appears in the company-registration record; they do not independently grant permission to operate. Name and activity reservation, foreign-investment approval, company registration, tax registration, and an industry license have different functions. An online marketplace may be regulated differently from a seller of its own inventory, and importing a product may create requirements distinct from domestic distribution. The business should map each revenue-producing activity to the responsible regulator before signing operational commitments.

The proposed address requires the same early attention. It is more than a place to receive mail: it supports company and tax registration and may affect the ability to obtain operating approvals. Before entering a lease, the business should evaluate land-use zoning, building and occupancy rules, lease restrictions, the landlord’s consent, documents needed for tax registration, and the premises’ practical suitability for the intended activity. A building-use restriction or failure to obtain a required document from the landlord can force a later move even after the company has been registered.

Taipei City offers an advance inquiry for business premises (營業場所預先查詢) for certain company and business registration matters. This procedure is specific to Taipei City. It does not replace fire, health, environmental, building, professional, or industry-specific permits. A business outside Taipei should confirm the applicable process with the local government and the authority responsible for the proposed activity.

Premises risk is especially significant when the company will undertake a long lease, construction, fit-out, or equipment installation. The lease can allocate the consequences if registration or operating approval is unavailable, but contractual protection should be designed before deposits and capital expenditures are committed. Company-registration approval should never be treated as proof that all uses of the property are lawful.

## 4. Work Permits, Residence, and Capital

An investor, shareholder, registered company officer, day-to-day manager, employee, and residence applicant may be the same person, but those roles are legally distinct. Foreign-investment review examines the capital and investment structure; registration establishes the relevant entity or business presence; a work permit authorizes specified work; and immigration authorities determine eligibility for residence. No one approval substitutes for another.

### Company Formation, Work Authorization, and Residence

No. Forming a company does not by itself confer work authorization or residence status. A foreign national who will manage or operate a business in Taiwan must meet the applicable work-permit requirements, including requirements relating to the proposed role, the investment relationship, and the employer’s business performance. After obtaining the work permit, the individual must separately apply for an Alien Resident Certificate (ARC) appropriate to the purpose of residence.

A student may invest in or form a company in Taiwan, but investment and shareholder status do not authorize work or management under the student’s current immigration status. Negotiating and signing contracts for the business, supervising employees, delivering services, or carrying out routine management may constitute work. The person should identify and obtain the appropriate work authorization before beginning those activities.

Work-permit review may address the applicant’s actual duties and qualifications, the relationship between the investment and the proposed position, the employer’s business performance, and the supporting evidence. An approved work permit and an ARC are separate documents issued for separate purposes. Their terms, expiration dates, renewal requirements, and conditions of continuing eligibility must be determined from the applicable approval documents and the rules in effect at the relevant time.

### Company Capital and Work Permits for Foreign Managers

Taiwan does not impose a generally applicable statutory minimum capital requirement for company formation. Industry-specific capital requirements, the reasonableness of the business plan, bank review, and employer-qualification rules for work permits must be assessed separately. The foreign-manager work-permit category for a foreign-invested business covers, among others, the manager (經理人) of a company in which overseas Chinese or foreign investors collectively hold more than one-third of the issued shares or total capital, the manager of a Taiwan branch of a foreign company, and the representative of a representative office. For a company or branch established for less than one year, the employer generally must satisfy at least one of the following: paid-in capital or Taiwan working capital of at least NT$500,000; revenue of at least NT$3 million; import-export performance of at least US$500,000; or agency commissions of at least US$200,000. For a company or branch established for at least one year, the employer generally must satisfy at least one of the following, measured by the most recent year in Taiwan or the average of the preceding three years: revenue of at least NT$3 million; import-export performance of at least US$500,000; or agency commissions of at least US$200,000. A representative office established for at least one year must have a record of activities in Taiwan; this requirement is waived if it has been established for less than one year. Special approval may be available where the business makes a substantial contribution to Taiwan’s economic development or special circumstances exist.

Here, foreign manager (經理人) describes the relevant work-permit category and does not mean that every foreign shareholder, director, or person called a manager qualifies. The thresholds above are employer qualifications for that work permit, not general minimum capital for forming a company. A regulated industry can impose separate capital or security requirements, the planned business must be adequately funded, and a bank conducts its own review. Meeting one financial threshold does not guarantee approval; the proposed job, the applicant’s qualifications, the investment relationship, and the supporting records remain subject to review.

A qualifying spouse and minor children do not receive residence automatically. Each family member must separately apply for dependent residence and provide the required evidence of the family relationship, financial support, and basis for residence. The principal applicant’s work permit or ARC does not itself confer residence status on family members.

For an ordinary foreign national, permanent residence generally requires five consecutive years of lawful residence in Taiwan and at least 183 days of residence in each year. The applicant must also satisfy requirements concerning conduct, assets or skills, and other statutory conditions, and some periods of stay may be excluded from the calculation. Certain foreign professionals may be subject to different calculation rules. Five years of holding a work permit or ARC does not automatically confer permanent residence; eligibility and evidence must be assessed when the application is made.

## 5. Taxes and the Taiwan–Korea Income Tax Agreement

Taiwan’s general business tax rate is 5%, and returns are generally filed every two months. The general profit-seeking enterprise income tax rate is 20%, although actual liability depends on taxable income and the applicable rules. Under Taiwan domestic law, dividends paid to a nonresident are generally subject to withholding at 21%. Dividends that qualify for the Taiwan–Korea Income Tax Agreement are subject to a maximum source-country rate of 10%. The applicable filing and withholding treatment depends on the taxpayer’s residence status, beneficial ownership, the character of the income, and the documents required to claim treaty benefits.

Business tax and profit-seeking enterprise income tax apply to different tax bases and follow different filing rules. Before transactions begin, the company should establish invoicing, recordkeeping, and accounting processes that match its contracts and cash flows. Cross-border dividends, interest, royalties, and service fees also require analysis of the recipient, income classification, domestic withholding rules, and any available treaty benefits.

As noted above, the Taiwan–Korea agreement entered into force on December 27, 2023, and applies from January 1, 2024; its 10% maximum source-country rate covers qualifying interest and royalties as well as dividends. Benefits under the agreement are not automatic merely because one party is in Taiwan and the other is in Korea. A claimant may need to establish both residence for purposes of the agreement and beneficial ownership of the income, and to submit a certificate of residence and any forms or supporting documents required for the particular type of income.

Likewise, the four PE categories discussed above—fixed facilities, projects lasting more than six months, services performed for more than 183 aggregate days in any 12-month period, and agents who repeatedly exercise authority to conclude contracts—must be analyzed independently. If a PE exists, the next step is to determine the profits attributable to that PE. Contracts, invoices, personnel records, deliverables, and payment records should reflect the actual activity; neither the service-day total nor the formal name of the Taiwan presence controls the result.
