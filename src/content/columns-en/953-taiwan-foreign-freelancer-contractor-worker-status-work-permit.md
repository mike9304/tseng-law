# Teaching or Freelancing in Taiwan on a Contractor Agreement: When You Are Really an Employee, and What That Means for Your Work Permit

"Independent contractor" on page one of a Taiwan agreement settles less than it seems. Taiwan's Ministry of Labor says so in writing: its [Guidelines for Determining Labor Contracts](https://www.mol.gov.tw/media/seljrmqo/%E5%8B%9E%E5%8B%95%E5%A5%91%E7%B4%84%E8%AA%8D%E5%AE%9A%E6%8C%87%E5%B0%8E%E5%8E%9F%E5%89%87.pdf?mediaDL=true), issued November 19, 2019, direct that a working relationship be judged by its degree of subordination in fact, "not bound by the form or name of the contract."

For an American teaching English at a cram school or doing design work for a Taipei startup, that cuts two ways. It can unlock rights you didn't know you had. It can also put your work permit on the table.

## How Taiwan draws the line

The anchor is [Article 2(6) of the Labor Standards Act](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=N0030001&flno=2), which defines a labor contract as one that establishes an employment relationship and "carries subordination" (從屬性). Taiwan's Council of Grand Justices, in [Interpretation No. 740](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/ExContent.aspx?ty=C&CC=D&CNO=740) of October 21, 2016, framed the core question in an insurance-agent case: can the worker freely decide how to perform the work, including working hours, and does the worker bear the business risk?

Concrete markers come from the Ministry's guidelines. On the personal side: does the business control your hours, methods and place of work, can you turn down assignments, are you evaluated or disciplined, may you send a substitute? On the economic side: are you paid for your labor rather than for a finished result, who pays for the equipment, are you paid only on terms the business sets or changes unilaterally, are you barred from dealing with its clients directly? Integration into the business's team counts too. The guidelines add supporting signs: whether the business enrolled you in labor insurance, contributed to your pension, or withheld tax on your pay as salary, and whether others doing the same work are on labor contracts.

Picture a buxiban schedule. The school assigns your classes and the room, sets start times to the minute, expects you at Saturday staff meetings, and pays an hourly rate it can change with a notice on the staff board. Those facts lean toward employment. A freelance designer who quotes per project, sets deadlines with the client and works for three other companies sits much further toward the contractor end.

## Which permit you hold changes the answer

Without a permit obtained through an employer, a foreigner may not work in Taiwan unless the law provides otherwise ([Article 43 of the Employment Service Act](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=N0090001&flno=43)). That is the catch. Evidence that you were an employee doubles as evidence that you worked.

Sponsored buxiban teachers have hour limits built into the permit. Teaching a foreign language at a short-term cram school is "professional work" under [Article 4(4)(iii) of the Act for the Recruitment and Employment of Foreign Professionals](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=A0030295&flno=4) (the Foreign Professionals Act). Under the cram-school teacher standard, that means a 專任 (dedicated) language-teaching post ([Article 3](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=N0090054&flno=3)) with at least 14 teaching-related hours a week at the sponsoring school ([Article 5-1](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=N0090054&flno=5-1)). The language taught must be an official language of your passport country. A second school may use you only if it obtains its own permit under [Article 53(1) of the Employment Service Act](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=N0090001&flno=53), for at least 6 hours a week. Total hours may not exceed 32. Teaching at a second school with no permit of its own, or freelancing for a startup on a permit tied to one employer, can lead to revocation under [Article 73](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=N0090001&flno=73); we covered that in [One Show for Another Employer](/en/columns/taiwan-work-permit-side-gig-other-employer).

Some people need no permit at all. A foreigner married to a Taiwanese citizen with household registration and holding residence is covered by [Article 48(1)(2)](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=N0090001&flno=48). So is a foreign professional, or a spouse or minor child of one, who holds permanent residence, under [Article 7(2) of the Foreign Professionals Act](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=A0030295&flno=7). Other permanent residents apply for their own permit, without an employer ([Article 51(2) of the Employment Service Act](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=N0090001&flno=51)). The Gold Card combines a work permit, resident visa, resident certificate and re-entry permit in one ([Article 9 of the Foreign Professionals Act](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=A0030295&flno=9)). Students at a university can be hired, with a permit, under [Article 50 of the Employment Service Act](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=N0090001&flno=50), capped at 20 hours a week outside winter and summer breaks.

Penalties land on both sides. A foreigner who works without the required permit faces a fine of NT$30,000 to NT$150,000, an order to leave, and a bar on working in Taiwan again ([Article 68](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=N0090001&flno=68)). The business that used the work risks NT$150,000 to NT$750,000 under [Article 63](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=N0090001&flno=63) for violating [Article 44](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=N0090001&flno=44) or [Article 57](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=N0090001&flno=57), with criminal penalties for a repeat violation within five years.

## What you can recover

Notice pay comes first. An employer can end the contract with notice only on one of five grounds in [Article 11 of the Labor Standards Act](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=N0030001&flno=11), among them closure or transfer, losses or downsizing, and clear inability to do the job. Notice applies once you have three months of service and runs 10, 20 or 30 days by length of service; an employer who skips it owes wages for the notice period ([Article 16](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=N0030001&flno=16)).

Severance is usually the larger sum, and the formula changed on January 1, 2026. From that date, [Article 24 of the Foreign Professionals Act](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=A0030295&flno=24) puts foreign professionals doing professional work, a sponsored buxiban language teacher included, under the Labor Pension Act's system. That system pays half a month's average wage per year of service, capped at six months ([Labor Pension Act, Article 12](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=N0030020&flno=12)). Spouses of Taiwanese citizens with household registration who hold approved residence, those allowed to stay and work after divorce or the spouse's death, and permanent residents were already inside it ([Labor Pension Act, Article 7](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=N0030020&flno=7)).

Two groups still use the older formula. Professionals already employed by the same business before the change could, within six months of it, choose in writing to stay under the Labor Standards Act; foreign employees outside every category above never moved. For both, [Labor Standards Act Article 17](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=N0030001&flno=17) sets one month per year. Years served before a switch are preserved and paid the old way ([Labor Pension Act Article 11](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=N0030020&flno=11)). Does someone who worked with no permit at all count as a "foreign professional doing professional work" under Article 24? The text does not say. Our [severance overview](/en/columns/taiwan-labor-severance-law) walks through the arithmetic.

Labor insurance is the third item. [Article 6 of the Labor Insurance Act](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=N0050001&flno=6) makes coverage mandatory for, among others, employees of companies and firms with five or more workers, and states that "workers" includes foreign employees in service. A buxiban is not one of the units required to enroll, according to the Bureau of Labor Insurance ([Ministry of Labor press release, September 24, 2021](https://www.mol.gov.tw/1607/1632/1640/38498/post)); the school may enroll its teachers voluntarily under [Article 8 of the same act](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=N0050001&flno=8).

## Where the fight happens

Point 5 of the guidelines sends a worker who considers the business to be the employer to the civil courts. Mediation at the local labor authority usually comes first; our piece on [labor dispute mediation for foreign employees](/en/columns/taiwan-labor-mediation-foreign-employee) walks through it, and [Told your Taiwan job is ending?](/en/columns/foreign-professional-dismissed-taiwan) covers the first week after notice. In these disputes the paper trail of control tends to matter: who sent the schedule, who approved a day off, how the pay was worked out each month.

A Taiwan lawyer can start from the school's own paperwork: the withholding statement, any labor insurance enrollment, and the contracts of teachers who take the same classes, which the guidelines treat as supporting signs. From there the lawyer can put a figure on notice pay and severance under whichever formula covers you and represent you at the local labor authority's mediation or in court. If a second school or a startup client is in the picture, the lawyer can also check whether each one held its own permit, since that answer shapes whether to file at all.
