# Taiwan wind-project payments: who owes the subcontractor when the owner steps in?

Suppose your company installs equipment on a Taiwan wind project under a subcontract. Payments fall behind, and the project owner offers to send the next installment directly to you. Before relying on that offer to keep the team working, establish whether it covers one payment or creates an obligation to pay the outstanding balance.

In its [September 29, 2026 statement](https://mnscdn.moea.gov.tw/Mns/Populace/news/News.aspx?kind=1&menu_id=40&news_id=124091), Taipower said it was following supervised-payment arrangements after Public Construction Commission coordination, while some subcontractors reported nonpayment. [CNA’s October 7 report](https://www.cna.com.tw/news/afe/202610070324.aspx) described continuing disagreement between the project parties. Those public statements do not establish what any particular subcontractor can recover. That requires the project’s contracts and payment arrangements.

## The sender of the money and the debtor

Taiwan project documents may use the term “supervised payment” (監督付款) for an arrangement in which money reaches a subcontractor directly instead of passing through its contracting party. The label does not tell you how much the payer has undertaken to owe.

Where Taiwan law governs the relevant obligation, [Civil Code Article 199](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=B0000001&flno=199) allows a creditor to demand performance from its debtor. For an overseas subcontractor, that means identifying the company bound to pay for each part of the work. An owner, main contractor and subcontractor may appear at the same coordination meeting without becoming parties to the same payment obligation.

A [Taiwan High Court judgment of May 21, 2024, 111年度建上字第13號](https://judgment.judicial.gov.tw/FJUD/printData.aspx?id=TPHV%2C111%2C%E5%BB%BA%E4%B8%8A%2C13%2C20240521%2C1), illustrates the problem. In that building-project case, a subcontractor sought its unpaid balance from an upper-tier contractor that had already made direct payments. The court examined the meeting record, invoices and payment documents. It found that the arrangement changed the payment mechanism but did not establish the alleged new construction contract or guarantee. The defendant was an upper-tier contractor, not the project owner, and the decision did not concern the wind project now in the news.

Check whether the proposed document merely authorizes someone to transmit money or commits that company to paying you a defined debt.

## A direct promise needs a defined scope

If the owner or an upper-tier contractor proposes a new agreement with your company, the document should identify its payment obligation. Is it taking responsibility for work already completed, a specified installment, future work, or some combination? Which acceptance conditions apply? Does the arrangement leave your existing claim against the original contractor intact? These are matters to negotiate and document, rather than assumptions to draw from the first remittance.

A different route may exist where two other parties make a contract for your company’s benefit. [Civil Code Article 269](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=B0000001&flno=269) can give the beneficiary a direct right to demand the agreed performance. But the terms must support that arrangement; naming your bank account in a payment instruction does not settle the issue. Before the beneficiary expresses its intention to enjoy the benefit, the contracting parties may change or revoke the agreement. If the beneficiary rejects the benefit, it is treated as never having acquired the right. Under [Article 270](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=B0000001&flno=270), the debtor can also raise defenses arising from that contract against the beneficiary.

## An assigned receivable can still be disputed

Your contractor might instead assign to you part of the payment it is owed by the owner. That transfers a receivable; it does not necessarily create a fresh, unconditional promise for the same amount as your subcontract invoice.

[Article 294](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=B0000001&flno=294) permits assignment subject to exclusions, including the nature of the claim, a contractual restriction and legal protection from attachment. A contractual restriction cannot be asserted against a good-faith third party. The relevant contract and circumstances therefore need to be checked before treating an assignment as available.

Under [Article 297](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=B0000001&flno=297), an assignment generally has no effect against the debtor until the assignor or assignee notifies it, unless another law provides otherwise. Presenting the assignor’s assignment document has the same effect as notice. A signed assignment kept only between the contractor and subcontractor may leave this step unfinished.

The owner’s defenses also matter. [Article 299](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=B0000001&flno=299) preserves defenses available against the assignor when notice is received. It also permits set-off for a claim against the assignor existing at that time if that claim falls due before, or at the same time as, the assigned receivable. Before accepting an upstream receivable, examine its due date, acceptance requirements, retention conditions and any asserted deductions. Its face value may differ from what can actually be collected.

## Before the next payment meeting

Prepare a short schedule showing each unpaid amount, the work it covers, the company said to owe it, and the document supporting that position. Keep the original subcontract beside the proposed payment agreement, meeting minutes, approvals, invoices and remittance records. If an assignment is proposed, obtain the relevant upstream payment terms and evidence of notice as well.

Ask for unclear commitments to be resolved in the payment document before deciding whether to continue work or sign a release. A reference to “supervised payment” leaves too much unanswered if the real concern is who will owe the final account.
