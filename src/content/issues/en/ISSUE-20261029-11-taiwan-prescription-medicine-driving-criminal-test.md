# A prescription in the car: when Taiwan treats medicine as drug driving

Some cold medicines can leave a driver drowsy. On October 29, 2026, the [Taipei Times reported](https://www.taipeitimes.com/News/taiwan/archives/2026/10/29/2003866819) a Food and Drug Administration warning about medicines that may slow reactions, blur vision or affect coordination. Some painkillers, cold medicines, antihistamines and blood pressure medicines were among the examples. The report said the Criminal Investigation Bureau warned that driving after taking prescribed medication that impairs driving ability can still bring legal responsibility.

For an American resident driving in Taiwan, the legal question is more precise than the warning: what was taken, and which part of Taiwan's driving law could apply? The safety advice covers a wider range of medicines than the traffic statute's named controlled substances. A prescription is evidence. It does not establish that the driver was fit to drive.

## The criminal provision has two drug-related routes

The relevant criminal provision is [Criminal Code Article 185-3](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=C0000001&flno=185-3). It applies to a person driving a powered vehicle. Item 3 covers a specified concentration of narcotics, anesthetics or similar substances, or their metabolites, in urine or blood, at or above the levels announced by the Executive Yuan. Item 4 addresses other circumstances showing use of those substances and an inability to drive safely. The distinction matters. Item 4 calls for proof of impaired ability; item 3 has a specified-substance and concentration test.

A laboratory result needs context. For item 3, the record must identify the substance or metabolite found in urine or blood, its concentration and the corresponding Executive Yuan announcement. Detection below the stated threshold would not establish that item by itself. Item 4 calls for evidence of both use of the listed kind of substance and inability to drive safely. The manner of driving, observations at the stop and medical information can bear on that inquiry; the court still has to assess what they prove about this driver. A pharmacy receipt alone answers neither route.

Neither item says that every medicine on a pharmacy shelf triggers the offense. The FDA warning is a public safety warning, and the Bureau's statement is its enforcement view. In an individual case, the alleged substance, the test method, the driver's condition and the statutory item must all be identified. Being sleepy after a medicine is a reason to stop driving; it is not, by itself, a completed legal finding under either item.

Article 185-3 sets a maximum of three years' imprisonment for the first-paragraph offense, with a possible fine. Where the prohibited driving causes death or serious injury, separate higher ranges apply. The causal link is part of that aggravated provision. A reported crash or a police investigation does not establish guilt; a criminal charge must be proved, and an accused person remains presumed innocent.

## The traffic penalty changes on October 31

For car and motorcycle drivers, [Article 35 of the Road Traffic Management and Penalty Act](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=K0040012&flno=35) has its own drug item: driving after using narcotics, hallucinogens, anesthetics or similar controlled drugs. Testing, fines, license measures and vehicle impoundment are set out in the same article. The FDA's mention of an ordinary cold remedy does not automatically place that product inside it.

On October 31 that article gets much harsher. An [Executive Yuan order dated October 28](https://gazette.nat.gov.tw/EG_FileManager/eguploadpub/eg032199/ch01/type1/gov01/num1/Eg.pdf), published in the Executive Yuan Gazette the next day, brings the August 17 amendment into force on October 31, 2026. Paragraphs 6 and 15 wait until December 31. Under the amended text, a driver who falls under the drug item is fined at the top of the range and loses the license outright. On a motorcycle that fine is NT$120,000; in a car, NT$150,000. Paragraph 9 adds confiscation of the vehicle, both for the drug item and for refusing that check or test. Refusal alone is fined NT$270,000. Adult passengers can be fined too, with listed exceptions.

None of this settles whether a particular prescription sedative is a "similar controlled drug." It raises the cost of the answer.

Which text applies depends on the date of the stop. Under [Article 5 of the Administrative Penalty Act](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=A0030210&flno=5), the law in force when the penalty is decided governs, unless an earlier law is more favorable to the person penalized; then the most favorable version applies. For a stop on or before October 30, that points to the [January 14 compilation](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawOldVer.aspx?pcode=K0040012&lnndate=20260114&lser=001), with its lower fines and a one-to-two-year license suspension. Even that compilation carries blood-testing paragraphs 6 and 13 from a November 2025 amendment that have not been brought into force. On October 29 the database's [legislative history](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawHistory.aspx?pcode=K0040012) still showed the commencement date as one for the Executive Yuan to set, so the Gazette is the source for October 31.

Traffic authorities may issue a notice under Article 35 while criminal investigators consider Article 185-3 for the same stop. The notice should identify the administrative provision used, while any allegation under Article 185-3 needs its own statutory elements proved. One document is not a verdict on the other. A driver comparing the two records needs the precise medicine or substance, the test result, the observations and the date of the stop; those details show which legal questions remain open.

## What the medicine record can answer

A driver takes a prescribed sedative with dinner, feels dizzy on the drive home and is stopped by police (an invented example). The prescription and pharmacy record would show the product and prescribed use; they would not establish the degree of impairment at the wheel. The package warning, timing of the dose, observed driving, any test result and the police record each answer a different part of the question. A foreign prescription may also help identify the product without deciding its classification under Taiwan law.

The same separation applies to nonprescription medicine. A warning label can explain why driving was unsafe, yet criminal and administrative liability still depend on the words of the applicable provision and the evidence. A person facing a drug-driving investigation should keep the medicine packaging and prescription alongside any test or stop record, because the exact substance and the date of the stop determine which law is being applied.
