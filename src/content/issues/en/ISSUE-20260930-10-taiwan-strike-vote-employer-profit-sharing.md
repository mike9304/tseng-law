# A Taiwan Union Plans a Strike Vote: What Should Foreign Employers Check?

If your foreign-owned company has a Taiwan plant or office, a strike vote in the news may prompt a practical question: what would we do if our own employees and managers disagreed over pay? The useful starting point is a clear record of the dispute and the company's existing compensation rules. A vote announcement alone does not settle either the legal right to strike or the underlying pay proposal.

[Taiwan News reported on September 22, 2026](https://www.taiwannews.com.tw/news/6444345) that Micron Taiwan's Taoyuan union planned a strike vote after mediation over a profit-sharing proposal ended without agreement. The report described the unions' proposal as an allocation of 15% of operating profit to quarterly bonuses, and included the company's statement that it had participated in mediation in good faith and sought further talks. A [September 24 CNA report](https://www.cna.com.tw/news/afe/202609240377.aspx) said the Taoyuan union would hold a briefing on September 29 and start voting on October 1, with voting running through October 6. These are reports of a planned process, not a finding that a strike has been authorized or has begun. This article does not assess either party's bargaining position.

## What has to happen before a union can declare a strike?

Under [Article 53 of the Act for Settlement of Labor-Management Disputes](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=N0020007&flno=53), a dispute action generally requires mediation to have failed. The same article says a **rights dispute** cannot be the basis for a strike. Whether a particular pay demand is an adjustment dispute or a dispute about an existing right should be decided from the actual collective agreement, employment terms and stated demand. The news description alone is not a legal classification of another employer's dispute.

[Article 54](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=N0020007&flno=54) adds a separate union vote rule: the union may not declare a strike or set up a picket line without a **direct, secret ballot** and the consent of **more than half of all its members**. That denominator is the full membership, not merely the people who cast ballots. The article also specifies workers who may not strike and industries with necessary-service conditions. Those details should be checked if the employer's activity falls in a regulated category. A union's public timetable, the taking of a vote and a valid declaration are distinct events.

For a foreign employer, the immediate record to request is the notice defining the dispute, the mediation result, and any communications explaining the vote. Do not infer the vote result before ballots are counted. A management team should designate someone in Taiwan to receive formal notices and keep a dated file of responses and proposals, so later explanations do not depend on memory.

## What may the employer do while the dispute is being handled?

During **mediation, arbitration or adjudication**, [Article 8](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=N0020007&flno=8) says an employer may not, **because of that dispute**, close the business, stop work, end employment contracts or take other action adverse to workers. It also restricts dispute action by the worker side because of the dispute during those procedures. The words “because of that dispute” matter. The rule does not turn every management decision at the same time into a prohibited act, but it does require the employer to explain a legitimate, independent reason for a decision that affects workers.

Article 8 applies only while mediation, arbitration or adjudication is pending. Once mediation has ended without agreement and no other procedure is pending, a separate rule still applies. [Labor Union Act Article 35](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=N0020001&flno=35) bars an employer, or anyone exercising management authority for it, from dismissing, demoting, cutting the pay of, or otherwise treating a worker adversely because the worker participates in union activities or collective bargaining, or participates in or supports dispute action. A union strike ballot must also be considered under the protection for union activities in Article 35(1)(1), not only the separate protection for dispute action in Article 35(1)(4). Article 35(2) makes a dismissal, demotion or pay cut on those prohibited grounds void.

Managers can keep communicating and negotiating. They should use consistent channels, avoid threats tied to union participation, and preserve the written reasons for any staffing or scheduling change. If the business must make an unrelated operational decision, get Taiwan advice on timing and evidence before announcing it. This is especially important when a planned dismissal, transfer or shutdown overlaps with an active dispute. The separate [guide for a foreign professional facing dismissal in Taiwan](/en/columns/foreign-professional-dismissed-taiwan) explains the employee-side consequences of an actual termination; it does not decide whether a particular action during a collective dispute complies with Article 8.

If a strike is lawfully declared, [Article 55](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=N0020007&flno=55) requires dispute action to follow good faith and avoid abuse of rights. It also says an employer may not claim damages from a union or its members for harm caused by dispute action taken under the Act. A company should distinguish lawful dispute activity from a specific act that falls outside legal protection rather than announcing a blanket damages claim. Safety and access problems should be documented as concrete events, with prompt local advice about the permitted response.

## Does Taiwan company law dictate the union's profit-sharing formula?

[Company Act Article 235-1](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=J0080001&flno=235-1) requires a company to specify in its articles of incorporation a **fixed amount or ratio** of employee compensation based on the year's profit, while first covering accumulated losses. For a Taiwan company limited by shares, it permits stock or cash compensation and requires a board resolution followed by a report to the shareholders' meeting. [Article 110](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=J0080001&flno=110) applies Article 235-1 to a Taiwan limited company, but its decision-making structure must be checked under [Article 108](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=J0080001&flno=108) rather than assuming the same board and share-based procedure. A foreign company's Taiwan branch is part of the foreign company, not a separately incorporated Taiwan company ([Article 3](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=J0080001&flno=3)). Identify the employer's legal form before applying these corporate procedures. Article 235-1 does **not** itself set the 15% of operating profit, quarterly payment, or other terms reported in the Micron negotiations. Nor does an articles clause answer every question under an employment agreement or collective agreement.

An employer should retrieve the current registered articles, board resolutions and any collective or individual pay commitments. Ask what accounting measure each instrument uses: “annual profit” in the statute, an operating-profit proposal in negotiations, and a contractual bonus formula may mean different things. Check the relevant group entity too. An overseas parent policy does not automatically become a binding Taiwan subsidiary commitment, while a promise made by the Taiwan employer cannot be dismissed simply because headquarters described it differently.

The Article 235-1 review also helps prevent an avoidable communications error. A statement that “the law already mandates this exact bonus” may overstate what the statute says. A statement that “bonuses are entirely discretionary” may overlook the articles or existing agreements. The right explanation starts with the actual legal and company documents, then separates statutory employee compensation from negotiated additional bonuses.

## What should the Taiwan team prepare this week?

First, put the dispute documents, mediation outcome and union notices into a single dated record. Identify who is authorized to negotiate and who can approve a pay proposal for the Taiwan employer. Second, compare the registered articles, bonus policies, employment contracts and collective agreements word by word. If there are different subsidiaries, list which workers each entity employs. Third, brief local managers on the Article 8 restriction while mediation, arbitration or adjudication is pending, and on Labor Union Act Article 35, which applies at every stage, and route proposed staffing changes through a documented review. Finally, prepare a neutral employee communication that accurately states what has been proposed and what remains undecided.

These steps do not predict a vote, a strike or an eventual agreement. They let the employer respond to workers and regulators from its own records rather than from a headline. They also help locate the issue that may need negotiation: the payment measure, eligibility, frequency, company entity or approval process.
