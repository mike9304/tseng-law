# Taiwan wants platforms to label synthetic images. Paid ads already have a rule

On October 25, 2026, [CNA reported](https://www.cna.com.tw/news/afe/202610250026.aspx) that Taiwan's Ministry of Digital Affairs is pressing Meta, Google and other platforms to mark images produced with artificial intelligence. The director of the Department of Digital Strategy described three layers.

Pictures made that way, and deepfake images, are already common on social platforms, the report said. The ministry's stated aim is a digital setting people can trust, with the first attention on high-risk deepfakes that leave the public unsure what they are looking at. For an American in Taipei whose face turns up in an ad they never agreed to, the question is what a platform already has to do.

## Three layers the director described

Since the start of this year, the director told [CNA](https://www.cna.com.tw/news/afe/202610250026.aspx) on October 25, the ministry has been meeting Meta, Google, LINE, industry associations and specialists, and wants the mark where a person will actually see it.

Layer one belongs to the person who uploads. The platform should offer a box to tick when the image was made with artificial intelligence, so the disclosure is made at the start.

Many of these images carry a hidden mark. A viewer never sees it. A system can. Layer two is that scan: if the file shows those features and the uploader left the box empty, the ministry wants the platform to add the label on its own.

Misses will remain. Someone can strip the hidden mark on purpose. A platform can also fail to notice one file in a very large pile. The third layer is the report that follows, through a government notice, an ordinary user's report, and a complaint from the person whose privacy is involved, so that a post breaking community rules or harming someone's rights can be handled.

Fraud changes the next step. Where a synthetic image is tied to fraud or to some other harm to a person's rights, the director said the ministry will work with the platform and, case by case, follow the law that actually applies and the facts of that file. Adding a label is one option the director named. Taking the image down is another. The director's wording leaves room for more.

## Paid ads already carry a rule

Advertising is not waiting on the talks. [Article 31 of the Fraud Crime Hazard Prevention Act](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=D0080226&flno=31) requires an online advertising platform that runs or pushes an ad to disclose, in the ad itself, several items. One of them is that the ad uses deepfake technology or a personal image generated by artificial intelligence. The act defines a deepfake as a computer-made or otherwise technical depiction of a person's words or conduct that is false and can lead others to believe it is real. After the platform has verified who commissioned the ad and who funded it, and neither falls under the high-risk business relationship in the preceding article, Article 31 allows a simpler form of those disclosures. The method is left to rules set by the competent authority for digital-economy industries.

Under [Article 40](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=D0080226&flno=40), that authority can fine a platform that runs or pushes an ad without the disclosures Article 31 requires. The ordinary range is NT$200,000 to NT$5,000,000, with an order to correct and a further fine each time the correction is still missing. A serious case of the same violation can draw NT$1 million to NT$25 million, on the same pattern of an order and a repeat fine. [Article 2](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=D0080226&flno=2) limits "online advertising platform" to a paid ad service that is the last platform before the ad reaches the public. The fine is written for that platform. [Article 58](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=D0080226&flno=58) puts the act into effect on promulgation except a listed set of provisions, and Article 31 and item 4 of Article 40, paragraph 1, are outside that list. Article 58 leaves the start date of a listed set of other provisions to the Executive Yuan. CNA's October 25 report did not cite the act, and the three layers it described reach past ads to ordinary posts.

## Obvious on the screen

Position, size and layout are not being standardized at this stage, [CNA reported on October 25](https://www.cna.com.tw/news/afe/202610250026.aspx). The mark still has to be obvious.

YouTube supplied the example. Its note, saying the material had been produced with artificial intelligence, sat below the description, and a viewer had to open a "more" control before the note appeared. The ministry wants a label field under the video itself. Hunting through a collapsed description was the failure the director pointed at.

Does a tiny corner mark count if most people scroll past it? The test CNA reported was whether people can tell what they are seeing. One shared design was not announced.

## Chats, ads, and three different speeds

Google is moving faster. Meta is still adjusting its systems. LINE is different, the director said in the same answer, [CNA reported the same morning](https://www.cna.com.tw/news/afe/202610250026.aspx).

One-to-one chats and group messages start outside the labeling range, the director said, because those messages are point-to-point and private. Ads and other public material come first. A synthetic picture inside a family group is not, on this account, the file LINE is being asked to badge at the outset.

Talks will continue, the ministry said, both with the platforms and with the associations. The ministry also plans to promote public awareness of the marks, so that a person who sees one knows the image was made with artificial intelligence. Neither side of that work was presented as a finished regulation with an effective date.

## When the picture is already doing harm

If the picture is already selling a scam, [Article 32](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=D0080226&flno=32) is the provision that moves. Once an online advertising platform knows an ad is a fraud ad, or that it obviously involves fraud, it has to remove the ad, restrict viewing, stop running it, or take another necessary step, on its own or within the deadline set in a notice from the police, the digital-economy authority, or the authority for that line of business. It also has to hand the police the details of the person who commissioned the ad, the funder, and any messaging account or telephone number in the ad that looks tied to the fraud. Where the article's second item applies, the platform pauses service to that user for a reasonable period. A platform that breaks those duties shares liability with the person who commissioned the ad and the funder, toward anyone who lost money by believing the ad.

[Article 33](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=D0080226&flno=33) adds a step that is not limited to the ad unit. Once the competent authority or the police give notice that content on the platform is suspected of a fraud crime, the platform must first restrict access, restrict browsing, or remove the content related to that crime.

Platform rules, a fraud complaint and a privacy complaint can be in motion together. Which of them fits depends on what the image is doing. An advertisement that impersonates someone, a message the person never sent, and a picture the person never posed for are different files. The October 25 story does not sort those files into offenses.
