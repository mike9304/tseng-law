# Taiwan Inheritance and Parental Rights: A Guide for Surviving Families

![Illustration of inheritance planning in Taiwan and protection of a minor’s property](../images/016-taiwan-inheritance-custody-analysis/featured-generic.webp)

After a death in the family, who inherits is only the first question. Others follow: which assets and debts form part of the estate, whether the surviving spouse has a separate marital-property claim, who will exercise parental rights and assume parental duties for any minor child, whether the child needs a guardian, and how the child’s own property will be protected. Each must be considered separately. They may affect one another. Their legal bases differ, though, and so does the order in which they should be analyzed.

Three pairs in particular need to be kept apart: an inheritance share and a residual-property distribution claim, parental rights and guardianship of a minor, legal representation and ownership of property. Carrying the conclusion on one issue straight over to another can cause misunderstandings about who holds a right, which property enters a calculation, or which court procedure is required.

## 1. Statutory Heirs and Intestate Shares

Article 1138 of Taiwan’s Civil Code ranks the statutory heirs other than the spouse: descendants first, then parents, siblings, and grandparents. If an heir exists in a higher rank, an heir in a lower rank generally does not inherit ahead of that person. The surviving spouse stands outside that order. Under Article 1144, the spouse inherits concurrently with the heirs in the rank that actually applies. The precise share may vary with that rank.

With a surviving spouse and two children as the only relevant heirs, each would ordinarily receive a one-third share. That assumes no valid will and no waiver, disqualification, succession by representation, or other material fact that changes the result. This is a hypothetical illustration. It is not a conclusion about any actual estate.

Disqualification from inheritance, a legally effective waiver, and succession by representation in place of a descendant who died earlier may also affect the result. Even within the same rank, the timing of deaths, legal parentage, adoption, and succession by representation all have to be checked. So the family-status records should be obtained first.

The opening of succession does not immediately make each heir the sole owner of a particular bank deposit or parcel of real property. In a co-inheritance, the parties may identify the scope of the estate, settle debts and expenses, and decide who receives which asset through a partition agreement or court proceedings. An abstract statutory share is one thing. Who finally receives a particular asset is another.

## 2. Wills and Identifying the Estate

A valid will may distribute the estate differently from intestate succession. Its form, the testator’s testamentary capacity, its interpretation and enforceability, and the restrictions imposed by mandatory rules, including reserved portions, must all be examined. A will’s mere existence does not determine the disposition of every estate asset. If it addresses only some assets, intestate-succession rules may apply to the remainder.

Before inheritance shares are calculated, the inventory and legal character of the estate have to be established. The records should confirm real property, bank deposits, securities, business interests, receivables, and personal property, and equally the decedent’s debts, guarantee obligations, unpaid taxes, and funeral-related expenses. No conclusion should rest on registered or account title alone. Beneficial ownership, shares in jointly titled property, third-party rights, and security interests should also be investigated.

Benefits with separately designated beneficiaries, such as insurance proceeds or retirement benefits, may be treated differently from estate property depending on the contract and applicable law. Trust property requires review of the trust arrangement and beneficial interests. Lifetime gifts or transfers may raise issues of recovery, inclusion, or reserved portions. For foreign accounts or real property, the law of the place where the property is located and Taiwan’s choice-of-law rules must also be considered.

An estate investigation does more than locate rights. It must also identify debts and procedural risks. Bank balance certificates, land records, tax records, insurance policies, trust documents, company books, and loan and guarantee documents should be organized as of the relevant reference date. Calculating a particular inheritance share from incomplete information may produce a figure that differs from the net estate actually available for distribution.

## 3. The Surviving Spouse’s Residual-Property Claim

Residual-property distribution is not the same as an inheritance share. It is a separate claim. A surviving spouse may assert it under Article 1030-1 of Taiwan’s Civil Code if the statutory requirements are met, and it must be analyzed separately from inheritance.

When the statutory marital-property regime ends, the claim compares, under statutory criteria, the increase in each spouse’s property acquired during marriage. Its legal basis, the party against whom it is asserted, and the property included in the calculation all differ from those of the inheritance share the surviving spouse takes as an heir. If the claim is established, its result may need to be reflected before the property remaining with the decedent is identified as the estate.

Not every asset acquired during marriage enters the calculation. Nor does the surviving spouse necessarily receive half of the estate. The calculation requires examining the Civil Code’s rules on what is included and excluded; whether property was acquired during marriage is not enough on its own. Statutory exclusions may include property acquired by inheritance or gift and solatium, and debts incurred during marriage must also be considered. All of this has to be examined on the facts. The evidence must establish the marital-property regime and whether the spouses agreed to a different one, when and why each asset was acquired, and the appropriate valuation date.

Under Article 1030-1, a court may adjust or waive the distribution amount if an equal distribution would be unfair. The factors required by law must be examined specifically, including household work during the marriage, the care and upbringing of children, the overall contribution and cooperation given to the family, how long the spouses lived together and apart, when property was acquired during the marriage, and each party’s economic capacity. Comparing registered title or the length of the marriage therefore cannot predetermine the existence or amount of the claim.

## 4. Inherited Debts and Waiver of Inheritance

From the opening of succession, an heir succeeds comprehensively to the decedent’s property rights and obligations, except for those that are exclusively personal to the decedent (Article 1148 of Taiwan’s Civil Code). Liability for inherited debts is generally limited to the value of the property acquired through inheritance. The relevant statutory procedures and exceptions still have to be examined, including preparation of an estate inventory, public notice and payment to creditors, and preservation of the estate.

Waiving an inheritance has a legally prescribed form. Under Article 1174, an heir who wishes to waive must make a written declaration to the court with jurisdiction within three months after learning of the right to inherit. Merely telling family members that the heir will take nothing must not be treated as completing a waiver in that form. The same goes for refraining from using estate property. How a waiver affects heirs in the next rank, or succession by representation, must also be checked against the family relationships and the stage of the proceedings.

Before estate property is disposed of or debts are paid, both assets and liabilities should be investigated. Creditors, security interests, guarantee obligations, ongoing contracts, and tax-filing duties should be checked. Where necessary, the filing of an inventory and creditor procedures should be considered. Conduct that may affect the statutory limitation of liability, such as concealing property or omitting it from the inventory, should also be avoided.

The Ministry of Finance’s Taiwan Tax Portal guidance on procedures for inheritance matters was updated on June 25, 2026. It describes two general periods. One is three months, for the court procedures concerning submission of an estate inventory and waiver of inheritance; the other is six months, for filing an estate tax return. The starting date, extensions, exceptions, and jurisdiction must be checked in each case, and this guidance must not be used to calculate an individual deadline.

Estate-tax filing, household-registration reporting, transfers of title to real property and vehicles, and payment procedures at financial institutions may involve different authorities and supporting documents. A waiver of inheritance is a court filing. An estate-tax return goes to the tax authority. The two must not be confused. Several periods may run at the same time, so it is safer to track the starting date and supporting evidence for each procedure on a separate schedule.

## 5. The Surviving Parent’s Rights and Duties

Article 1089 of Taiwan’s Civil Code covers the case where one parent cannot exercise rights and assume duties concerning a minor child: the other parent ordinarily does so. A surviving parent who retains parental rights therefore ordinarily continues to exercise them and to assume parental duties, as long as no contrary court order exists. Courts may still become involved. Existing orders, statutory grounds for restriction or suspension, cross-border factors, the child’s best interests, and other specific circumstances may require it.

Parental rights and duties may include the care and upbringing of a minor child, decisions about the child’s residence, legal representation, and management of the child’s property. Each authority must be exercised to protect the child’s personal and property interests. None may be exercised for the parent’s own benefit. Decisions about day-to-day care and legal representation for a significant property disposition may require different analysis.

Existing divorce judgments, rulings on the exercise of parental rights, and any restriction or suspension of those rights should be reviewed along with the family relationships before and after the death. If a foreign court has issued an order, its recognition and effect in Taiwan and any procedures required in another country must also be considered. Where the interests of parent and child conflict in a transaction, whether ordinary legal representation is sufficient has to be determined separately.

Parental rights and inheritance are legally separate issues. What the child inherits belongs to the child. That holds even where the surviving parent exercises parental rights and assumes parental duties, and the property cannot be treated as part of the parent’s own inheritance share. A surviving parent may hold parental rights even when not an heir. If the parent is also an heir, the possibility of a conflict of interest between parent and child requires particular care.

## 6. Guardian Designation and Court Involvement

Guardianship of a minor becomes relevant in two situations under Article 1091 of Taiwan’s Civil Code: when the minor has no parent, or when neither parent can exercise rights and assume duties concerning the child. One parent’s death alone must not be assumed to commence guardianship immediately. The surviving parent’s parental-rights status, any existing court order, and that parent’s actual ability to exercise those rights must be checked first.

Under Article 1093, the parent who is last to exercise rights and assume duties concerning a minor may appoint a guardian for the minor by will. That will must satisfy the statutory formalities. The appointing parent must also have authority to designate a guardian for the minor. Even with a designation, the requirements for commencement of guardianship, the guardian’s eligibility and acceptance, reporting to the court, and other supervisory procedures must be examined.

Without a valid testamentary designation, or if the designated person cannot serve, the statutory order under Article 1094 and the court-appointment rules under Article 1094-1 may apply. The court examines the child’s best interests on the specific facts, including the child’s age and ability to express views, the candidate’s relationship with and ability to care for the child, the suitability of the proposed property management, and the stability of the child’s living arrangements.

Relatives and other applicants authorized by law may, where statutory grounds exist, ask the court to appoint or replace a guardian or to order another necessary measure. A family relationship alone does not make a particular candidate guardian as a matter of course. Nor is a guardian in the same position as a parent exercising parental rights. A guardian may have separate duties concerning the scope of authority, preparation of an inventory, reporting, and court supervision.

For any guardian under consideration, two roles are in play: personal care and property management. Each should be assessed specifically. Safeguards may need to be considered where a candidate lives abroad for an extended period, has an economic interest that conflicts with the child’s, or lacks experience in asset management. The court may determine appropriate methods of supervision or other necessary measures in light of the actual circumstances.

## 7. Protecting a Minor’s Inherited Property

May a surviving parent freely use property that a minor child inherits? No. Under Articles 1087 and 1088 of Taiwan’s Civil Code, what a minor acquires through inheritance is the child’s separate property. Separate property means property that belongs to the minor. A parent or guardian who undertakes its management does not become the beneficial owner and must not use the property for personal living expenses or debt repayment.

Inherited bank deposits, real property, shares, and other rights should be clearly identified as the child’s property and managed separately. The powers of management, use, collection of income, and disposition under Article 1088 are tied to one purpose: serving the child’s interests. Legal representation must be handled in those interests too. A parent must not be understood to have unrestricted authority to use a child’s inherited property unilaterally.

Records should be kept of the type of property, the need for a disposition, the adequacy of the consideration, and the plan for holding and using the proceeds. High-value or high-risk actions, such as a sale of real property, creation of security, or business investment, need one more check: whether permission under another law or a court procedure is required.

Conflicts of interest may arise where a parent and child are co-heirs of the same estate or are counterparties to a contract with each other. Such a conflict, or a significant disposition, may require a special representative or court involvement. The special-representative system under Article 1086 should be considered, including who may represent the child in a partition agreement or litigation. Whether a special representative is needed should be assessed from the form of the transaction and from whether the economic interests actually conflict.

Where a guardian of a minor manages the property, rules on preparing an inventory, retaining supporting documents, separating income and expenditure, reporting to the court, and court supervision may apply. Financial accounts and investments should be managed in a way that identifies them as the child’s property. Each expenditure’s purpose and basis should be recorded. Records should be organized systematically from the outset, so that the property and the management records can be transferred smoothly when the guardianship ends or the minor reaches adulthood.

A trust or insurance arrangement cannot be assumed to be safe on the contract alone. Its trustee and beneficiary, payment conditions, management fees, supervisory safeguards, and amendment and termination provisions should be checked together with reserved-portion and tax issues. The child’s current needs and future life should be balanced. A property manager’s convenience must not take priority over the child’s interests.

## 8. Cross-Border Family Issues

For a family with cross-border elements, the domestic rules of Taiwan’s Civil Code should not be applied immediately without further analysis. Nationality, domicile and habitual residence, the parties’ center of life at the time of death, the location of property, a foreign marriage or divorce, and existing parental-rights orders may all affect the governing law and jurisdiction. Even within one family, succession, the marital-property regime, parental rights, guardianship, and property registration may be subject to different connecting factors.

Taiwan’s Act Governing the Choice of Law in Civil Matters Involving Foreign Elements is the starting point for determining the governing law of a civil relationship with foreign elements. That Act alone may not be sufficient. The court’s international jurisdiction, recognition and enforcement of foreign judgments, treaties, and the law of the other country must also be examined. Whether a determination valid in Taiwan will be accepted for the transfer of property located abroad must be checked separately, in the place where the property is located.

Wills made abroad must be examined for form and substantive effect, translation and authentication, and probate or enforcement procedures. Foreign marriage and divorce certificates and parentage documents may require an apostille or consular legalization and a translation. If the spelling of a name, passport information, and household-registration records differ, additional documents may be required to establish that they refer to the same person.

With a foreign judgment on parental rights or guardianship, three questions have to be answered: whether the judgment is final, whether due process was observed, and whether it can be recognized in Taiwan. If the child is habitually resident in another country, the jurisdiction of the local court and any emergency protective measures may also be important. Which country’s proceedings should be pursued first? The child’s best interests and the practical enforceability of the outcome should both be considered.

Tax filing obligations may also arise separately in more than one country. It is necessary to check whether Taiwan estate-tax filing overlaps with foreign inheritance or gift tax, foreign financial-account reporting, or real-property transfer taxes, and to review the rules for relieving double taxation. The applicable exchange-rate date, valuation method, and scope of taxpayers may differ. A filing result from one country should not simply be reproduced in another.

## 9. Gathering Records and Sequencing the Procedures

Six steps form a basic framework for gathering the facts completely and for keeping different rights and procedures from being confused. The actual order of filing may vary with the agency that has jurisdiction and the urgency of the matter. Each step’s deadline should be checked separately.

1. Verify death certificates and death-registration records, family relationships and Taiwan household-registration records, marriage, divorce, and adoption records, and existing court orders. Foreign documents also need a check of authentication, translation, and consistency in the spelling of names.
2. Investigate real property, bank deposits, investment assets, business interests, personal property, and receivables, and organize loans, guarantees, taxes, and contractual liabilities alongside them. Registered and beneficial ownership, insurance beneficiaries, trusts, jointly owned property, and records of lifetime transfers should each be identified separately.
3. Review the original will: its method of execution, testamentary capacity, witness or notarization requirements, the executor, and any legacy. Consider both how a valid will changes the distribution and the restrictions imposed by mandatory rules, including reserved portions.
4. Calculate statutory inheritance shares separately from the residual-property distribution claim. Each system has its own calculation base, debts, exclusions, valuation date, and supporting evidence, and the result under one must not be counted twice in the other.
5. Identify property belonging to a minor, then determine the scope of legal representation and of parental or guardian management, any conflict of interest, and whether a special representative is needed. Arrange a method for keeping accounts, books, and disposition proceeds separate from an adult’s personal property.
6. Sort the procedures by agency: the court procedures for waiver of inheritance, an estate inventory, guardianship, and a special representative; the tax authority’s estate-tax filing; household-registration reporting; and property-registration procedures. For each one, check the jurisdiction, starting date, required documents, opportunity to supplement, and availability of an extension, and retain receipts and copies.

While collecting materials, record where each original is kept and its issue date or reference date, and organize electronic and paper records under the same classification system. If there are several co-heirs or property managers, record who keeps each document and who approved each act. Access to a minor’s personal and financial information should be limited to the people and institutions that need it.

Property requiring urgent preservation should be distinguished from routine filing procedures. First check for matters requiring immediate attention, such as a risk that access to an account or business operations may be interrupted, damage to real property, or expiration of a claim. Urgency does not authorize a disposition by someone who lacks authority. It helps to track court proceedings and tax, household-registration, and registration procedures together on a single schedule.
