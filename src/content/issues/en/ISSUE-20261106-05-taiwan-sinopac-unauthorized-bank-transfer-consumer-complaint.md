# SinoPac’s transfer incident and Taiwan’s bank-complaint deadlines

If a Taiwan bank rejects a complaint about an unauthorized transfer, when can the customer seek an outside review? [CNA reported on November 4](https://focustaiwan.tw/society/202611040022) that Bank SinoPac said it had reimbursed all customers affected by the previous day’s unauthorized transactions. The bank attributed the incident to a breach involving suppliers of its mobile banking system. It also said it had found no evidence of a customer-data leak.

For a resident whose own disputed debit remains unresolved, the relevant dates are when the bank received the complaint and when its answer reached the customer.

## Which account holders can use the process

Banks are covered financial service providers under [Article 3 of the Financial Consumer Protection Act](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=G0380226&flno=3). [Article 4](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=G0380226&flno=4) defines a financial consumer by the receipt of financial products or services, with exclusions for qualified institutional investors and people or entities meeting prescribed financial-capacity or professional-expertise criteria. Nationality is not an exclusion.

An eligible foreign resident can therefore use the Act’s complaint process for a personal banking dispute, while a company or professional investor cannot assume the same eligibility merely because it also holds a bank account. Classification matters. It determines access to the statutory consumer procedure without deciding whether the disputed transfer was authorized.

## The two periods run from different events

[Article 13](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=G0380226&flno=13) requires the consumer to complain to the bank first; the bank must handle the complaint appropriately and reply within 30 days after receiving it. Receipt starts the clock. A complaint acknowledgment, for example, records the event from which that period runs.

A customer dissatisfied with the response may apply to the Financial Ombudsman Institution within 60 days after receiving that response. If the bank fails to handle the complaint within the 30-day period, the application period instead runs for 60 days from its expiry. An early rejection can start the second clock. There is no requirement to wait for the full 30 days after a response has already arrived.

These periods concern the ombudsman route. They are not a complete account of court-filing deadlines. The institution’s [English consumer FAQ](https://www.foi.org.tw/Article.aspx?Arti=29&Lang=2) explains the same sequence, including both possible starting points for the application period. Keeping the bank’s response together with its delivery date makes the relevant deadline easier to establish.

## What an unresolved claim needs to explain

Under [Article 7](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=G0380226&flno=7), a financial service provider must exercise due care when providing its services. The article also invalidates clearly unfair contract terms and requires ambiguities to be interpreted in the consumer’s favor.

An online banking agreement also has a statutory boundary: [Article 6](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=G0380226&flno=6) prevents advance contractual limits or exclusions of the provider’s liability under this Act and invalidates the offending stipulation. Contract wording is not conclusive. The agreement and the disputed transaction still need to be assessed together to establish the basis of a particular claim.

Account records give the complaint its substance. The transaction entry identifies the debit, while messages and the bank’s explanation show what each side says happened. Where money has been recredited, a claim can distinguish the refunded principal from any specific amount still disputed. The reported SinoPac reimbursement is relevant background. The account-specific comparison remains the debit, the repayment and the bank’s written response.
