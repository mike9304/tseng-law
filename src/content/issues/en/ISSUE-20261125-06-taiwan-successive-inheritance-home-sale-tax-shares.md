# One inherited Taiwan home, two tax histories

An heir who failed to file the required return after selling an inherited Taiwan home was assessed more than NT$400,000 in additional tax and fined, according to a [November 24 notice](https://www.ntbca.gov.tw/singlehtml/3c844e426e9c4b5ebcaaa18694d7e230?cntId=6f7e96bbfefa4cec995d5d2c861dc1a9) from the National Taxation Bureau of the Central Area. The heir had treated the entire sale as outside the newer house-and-land income-tax regime.

After the father, who had acquired the property in 2001, died in 2019, his spouse and child each inherited half. The mother died in 2023. Her half then passed to the child, who sold the home in 2025.

## Each inherited share has its own tax treatment

[Income Tax Act Article 4-4](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=G0340003&flno=4-4) sets the acquisition-date scope of the newer house-and-land transaction income tax, while a [Ministry of Finance interpretation](https://law-out.mof.gov.tw/LawContent.aspx?id=GL009971&media=print) addresses property inherited from a person who acquired it by the end of 2015. In the stated circumstances, the sale follows the older treatment, even though the heir inherited after that cutoff.

Applied to the bureau's example, the half inherited directly from the father followed the older regime. The mother, however, had acquired her half by inheritance in 2019. The child's later inheritance of that share therefore fell within the newer regime. The father's original purchase date did not place both halves outside it.

Older treatment still involves income tax. [Article 14, paragraph 1, category 7](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=G0340003&flno=14) governs property-transaction income, including income from selling inherited property; the interpretation applies that treatment to the house portion. It also permits an election into the newer regime where its specified owner-occupied-home conditions are satisfied. The ordinary classification is not a blanket exemption from tax.

## A longer holding period answers a different question

Once a share falls under the newer regime, [Article 14-4](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=G0340003&flno=14-4) permits adding the deceased owner's holding period to the heir's. Point 5 of the ministry's [filing directions](https://law-out.mof.gov.tw/LawContent.aspx?id=GL009961) extends that calculation across successive inheritances or bequests, meaning two or more consecutive transfers including the current one.

The earlier years still count. That can affect the holding-period bracket for the mother's half, while leaving it within the newer regime. Acquisition history determines the applicable regime; the combined holding period then helps determine the rate.

## An overseas heir also needs the residence test

For a Taiwan resident individual, Article 14-4 provides a 15% bracket for holdings longer than ten years. Its nonresident schedule instead applies 35% to holdings longer than two years and 45% to holdings of two years or less. A US-based heir cannot infer a 15% rate from the family's long ownership alone.

Residence is a separate test. Under [Article 7](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=G0340003&flno=7), an individual qualifies through Taiwan domicile and habitual residence, or, without Taiwan domicile, at least 183 days in Taiwan during the tax year. A passport or the location of inherited property does not by itself resolve that question.

For a sale covered by the newer regime, [Article 14-5](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=G0340003&flno=14-5) requires filing within 30 days counted from the day after ownership-transfer registration, even if no tax is due. A copy of the contract and related documents must accompany the return. Any tax due requires a payment receipt too.

The sale contract, both inheritance records and the transfer-registration date explain the heir's filing position far more precisely than the family's original purchase year alone.
