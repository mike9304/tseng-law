# Signing for a Taiwan Company Before It Exists

A Taiwan company does not come into existence merely because its proposed name has been approved. [Article 6 of the Company Act](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=J0080001&flno=6) makes registration a condition of incorporation. For a US business preparing a Taiwan subsidiary, the signing date matters: was the intended contracting company incorporated when the agreement was made?

## A reserved name is an early step

Under [Article 18](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=J0080001&flno=18), company names and business activities undergo approval before company registration, with the approval reserved for a period. That paperwork establishes progress in the application. It does not establish incorporation.

Taiwan's Ministry of Economic Affairs sets out the separate stages in its [foreign-investment flow chart](https://www.moea.gov.tw/Mns/dir/Investment/DirApply.aspx?kind=A&menu_id=42814&sub_menu_id=42813): name preapproval, investment application, remittance, verification of the investment amount, and incorporation registration. An investment approval letter belongs to that sequence. Treating it as the final registration document can put the wrong party on a contract. The broader process is covered in the [Taiwan company formation guide](/en/columns/taiwan-company-establishment-basics).

## The name on a proposed lease or order

Negotiations for premises or equipment may begin while incorporation is being arranged. Read the party and signature blocks together. Does the draft identify an individual investor, an existing US entity, or the proposed Taiwan subsidiary as the party taking on payment and performance obligations?

Identity matters here. [Article 19](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=J0080001&flno=19) prohibits conducting business or other legal acts in a company's name before incorporation registration. The ministry's [Company Act FAQ](https://serv.gcis.nat.gov.tw/crm/faqAction.do?id=912&method=faqDetl) explains the prohibition by reference to business and equivalent transactions carried out using an unregistered company's name, protecting the other party and the security of transactions.

Does that make every preparatory arrangement a criminal offense? A particular act still needs examination. Its nature and the name used matter. The FAQ is neither a blanket approval of every pre-incorporation contract nor a finding that all preparation is criminal. A discussion of terms and an obligation already undertaken in a company's name should not be treated as interchangeable facts.

For a draft already circulated, a useful review would compare the named party, signing authority, recipient of any deposit, and proposed treatment of an incorporation delay. These are practical questions, not a statutory list of required documents. Keep the surrounding correspondence too. Changing a name in the latest draft does not, by itself, explain what the parties previously agreed or establish who bears an existing obligation.

## Article 19 reaches the actors themselves

Civil liability falls on the actor who violates Article 19. Where there are two or more such actors, the provision makes their civil liability joint and several. Identifying those actors requires attention to conduct; a shareholder list or meeting attendance sheet alone is not a substitute for that assessment. These consequences are stated in [Article 19, paragraph 2](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=J0080001&flno=19).

Criminal penalties are also specified. The provision allows imprisonment for up to one year, short-term detention, or a fine of up to NT$150,000, and permits a fine to be imposed in addition. It also provides for the competent authority to prohibit use of the company name. Whether a particular agreement involves a violation depends on the acts and evidence; this statutory description is not a prediction of a prosecution or sentence.

A plan to finish incorporation later should therefore not replace examination of the earlier signing. Article 19 expressly addresses the actors' own liability. Keep the name-approval documents, registration status at the time, signed agreement, and explanations sent to the other party together. The later registration certificate will not tell the whole story of the earlier signature.
