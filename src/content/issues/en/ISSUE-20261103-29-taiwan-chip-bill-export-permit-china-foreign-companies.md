# Taiwan's 'chip bill' hearing: what a China export permit and a transshipment offence could mean for foreign suppliers

Ten years in prison and a fine of up to NT$10 million. That is the ceiling in a draft amendment to Taiwan's Trade Act discussed at a Legislative Yuan Economics Committee hearing on the afternoon of November 2, 2026. The hearing carried the title "Trade Act partial amendments (Articles 13-2 and 27-3) — chip bill", [CNA reported](https://www.cna.com.tw/news/afe/202611020211.aspx). Nothing has been passed.

For a foreign materials or equipment supplier with a Taiwan subsidiary or distributor, the draft is worth reading now. It would reach not only direct shipments to China but also sales destined for mainland China but routed through a third location.

## What the draft would do

The [Economic Daily News reported](https://udn.com/news/story/7238/9850435) that the bill, proposed by legislators of the ruling Democratic Progressive Party, would add an Article 13-2. Under it, certain integrated circuits, key materials for semiconductor wafers, components and manufacturing equipment could not be exported to mainland China without permission from the competent authority. These items would also be listed as strategic high-tech goods. According to the same Economic Daily News report, the bill also provides for a list, to be published by the Ministry of Economic Affairs, of specific countries, regions, legal persons, groups or other bodies and individuals that are substantially controlled or directed by mainland China, or that act for its benefit to evade controls.

A new Article 27-3 would set the penalty: up to 10 years' imprisonment, and a fine of NT$5 million to NT$10 million may be added. Sending the items to mainland China through a third location, resale or another indirect route would be punished the same way, and proceeds of the crime would be confiscated, according to the same report. [PTS described](https://news.pts.org.tw/article/833939) the draft as adding an anti-circumvention clause and making confiscation of illicit gains mandatory.

Would a sale to a customer in a third country count? The reports describe the offence as an indirect route whose final destination is mainland China. How "final destination" and knowledge would be defined is not set out in the coverage.

## The gap the bill targets

Article 13 of the [current Trade Act](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=J0090004&flno=13) already requires a permit to export strategic high-tech goods and a truthful declaration of use and end user. The competent authority announces which goods and regions are controlled. According to PTS, the current list covers only 12 categories of semiconductor wafer manufacturing equipment and leaves out integrated circuits and key wafer materials.

Criminal liability already exists for part of this ground. Under [Article 27](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=J0090004&flno=27), exporting strategic high-tech goods to a controlled region without a permit carries imprisonment of up to five years or short-term detention, with a fine of up to NT$3 million imposed separately or in addition. Where a company's representative, agent or employee commits that offence in the course of business, the company is also fined. Shipments to other regions without a permit are handled administratively under [Article 27-2](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=J0090004&flno=27-2), with fines of NT$60,000 to NT$3 million and possible suspension of trading. The reports did not say whether the draft Article 27-3 would carry a similar provision for companies.

## What the ministry and the experts said

Liu Wei-lien, director-general of the ministry's International Trade Administration, told the hearing that the ministry was studying an overall package for bringing advanced chips and other high-tech goods under control, drawing on Taiwan's commitments under its reciprocal trade agreement (ART) with the United States and on the authority the Trade Act already provides, [CNA reported](https://www.cna.com.tw/news/afe/202611020211.aspx). The Economic Daily News separately reported that the ministry respects the legislative proposal and does not oppose the new articles.

The four outside speakers quoted by CNA all supported stronger controls in some form. Lin Ching-fu of National Tsing Hua University's Institute of Law for Science and Technology cited national security, closing gaps in current chip controls and Taiwan's ART commitments. Lawyer Lai Chung-chiang said the amendment should be made, while balancing the interests involved. Wang Kuo-chen of the Chung-Hua Institution for Economic Research said it fitted the ART and Taiwan's security interests, but warned, according to CNA, that Taiwanese businesses in China could be caught between US export rules and Chinese penalties. Tseng Chih-chao, deputy secretary-general of an economics and finance association (中華經濟與金融協會), backed sound export controls but asked why other high-risk countries would not be covered and called for evidence and technical thresholds for any China-specific control.

## For foreign companies with a Taiwan entity

Nothing in the draft is in force. Even so, the questions it raises are practical ones. Which products in a Taiwan entity's catalogue might fall under "key wafer materials" or "components" once lists are published? How does the entity confirm the end user and final destination when it sells to a trading company in a third country? And who inside the company signs off?

On November 2 the committee heard the arguments. Reports did not describe a vote or a timetable, and the text of Articles 13-2 and 27-3 may still change.
