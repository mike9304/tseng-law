# Taiwan Inheritance and Parental Rights: A Guide for Surviving Families

![Illustration of inheritance planning in Taiwan and protection of a minor’s property](../images/016-taiwan-inheritance-custody-analysis/featured-generic.webp)

A death in a family can raise issues under several distinct bodies of law, each addressing a different question. Intestate succession identifies heirs and inheritance shares when no effective will controls the distribution. Estate administration identifies what the decedent owned, what debts and expenses must be addressed, and what remains for distribution. A surviving spouse may also have a residual-property distribution claim arising from the marital-property regime; that claim is separate from inheritance and may affect what belongs in the estate.

The family-law analysis is equally distinct. Parental rights and duties concern a minor child’s care, upbringing, residence, education, health, legal representation, and property management. Guardianship of a minor generally becomes relevant only when no parent can exercise those rights and assume those duties. Neither a parent’s exercise of parental rights and duties nor a guardian’s role determines ownership of property inherited by a child. The child owns that property, while a parent, guardian, legal representative, special representative, or court may have a defined role in protecting or administering it. Keeping these questions separate is the starting point for a reliable analysis.

## 1. Statutory Heirs and Intestate Shares

Under Articles 1138 and 1144 of Taiwan’s Civil Code, the surviving spouse inherits concurrently with the heirs in the applicable statutory rank, and descendants are first in the statutory order. If the only relevant heirs are a surviving spouse and two children, there is no valid will, and no waiver, disqualification, succession by representation, or other material fact changes the result, each would ordinarily receive a one-third share. This is a hypothetical illustration, not a conclusion about any actual estate.

Article 1138 places relatives other than the spouse in four ranks: descendants, parents, siblings, and grandparents. The first rank that contains a qualified heir ordinarily excludes the later ranks. Article 1144 then determines the surviving spouse’s concurrent share according to the rank involved. When the spouse inherits with descendants, the spouse’s intestate share is equal to that of each descendant. Different formulas apply when the spouse inherits with parents, siblings, or grandparents, and the spouse takes the entire inheritance if no heir exists in any of those four ranks.

The family record must be verified rather than assumed. Relevant questions include legal parentage, adoption, the degree of relationship, and the order of death. A descendant may take through succession by representation if the statutory conditions are met. An apparent heir may be affected by disqualification or may complete a waiver of inheritance. Simultaneous-death issues, an unborn child’s protected interests, or a dispute over status can also change the heir analysis. Household-registration records are important evidence in Taiwan, but foreign civil-status records may also be necessary.

An inheritance share is an undivided legal proportion, not an automatic assignment of a particular bank account, parcel of land, security, or personal item. Before partition, co-heirs may hold rights in the estate collectively under the applicable rules. Payment of debts, taxes, administration expenses, legacies, and other estate obligations may affect the property available for partition. A distribution agreement, a will’s partition directions, or a court proceeding may ultimately determine which asset is transferred to which heir.

## 2. Wills and Identifying the Estate

A valid will may alter the distribution that would otherwise follow intestate succession. Its legal form, the decedent’s testamentary capacity, revocation or amendment, the meaning of its operative provisions, and the authority of any executor should all be examined. The will may create a legacy, direct a method of partition, or establish another arrangement, but it remains subject to reserved portions and other mandatory rules. A conclusion about validity or effect should be based on the original instrument and the circumstances of execution, not merely on a copy or a summary.

Before calculating any inheritance share, identify the estate. The inquiry should cover real property, accounts, investments, business interests, receivables, valuable personal property, intellectual-property interests, and claims held by the decedent. It should also identify debts, guarantees, taxes, funeral and administration expenses, security interests, and pending proceedings. Legal title and beneficial ownership may differ; co-ownership proportions, nominee arrangements, liens, mortgages, and third-party claims require separate attention.

Some benefits associated with a death may not pass through the estate in the same manner as ordinary assets. Insurance proceeds require review of beneficiary designations and policy terms. Retirement, pension, employment, and death benefits may follow the governing statute, plan, or contract. Trust property requires examination of title, beneficial interests, the trust instrument, and the effect of the settlor’s or beneficiary’s death. Lifetime gifts and transfers may affect ownership, tax, reserved-portion, collation, or avoidance questions. Each asset must therefore be classified before a net distributable estate can be calculated.

The governing law may not be uniform across every issue or asset. The law governing testamentary form can differ from the law governing succession, marital property, a trust, a corporation, or title to land. Foreign wills and probate-related orders may require recognition, authentication, or additional local procedures before an institution in Taiwan can act on them.

## 3. The Surviving Spouse’s Residual-Property Claim

No. A residual-property distribution claim under Article 1030-1 of Taiwan’s Civil Code is a separate claim that a surviving spouse may assert if the statutory requirements are met; it must be analyzed separately from inheritance. Not every asset acquired during marriage enters the calculation, and the surviving spouse does not necessarily receive half of the estate. The marital-property regime, the reason and time of acquisition of each asset, debts, and statutory exclusions must be examined on the facts.

Article 1030-1 concerns dissolution of the statutory marital-property regime. In general terms, it compares the remainder of property acquired by each spouse during marriage after deducting relevant marital debts. The statutory calculation excludes property acquired through inheritance or gift and excludes solatium. Related provisions can require adjustments for the source of funds, repayment of one spouse’s debts with another class of property, or dispositions intended to reduce the other spouse’s claim.

The right, the calculation base, and the parties to the claim differ from the rules of succession. A proper sequence is to determine the marital-property regime, establish the property and debt figures attributable to each spouse under that regime, resolve any qualifying residual-property distribution claim, and only then identify the property that belongs to the decedent’s estate. Treating the claim as an inheritance share, or simply adding it to an assumed estate percentage, produces an unreliable result.

Equal distribution under the statutory formula is not invariably the final result. Article 1030-1 permits a court to adjust or waive the distribution amount when an equal division would be manifestly unfair, taking account of statutorily relevant circumstances such as contributions to family life, household work, care of children, periods of cohabitation or separation, timing of acquisitions, and economic capacity. No entitlement can be inferred from the length of the marriage, registered title, or the date of acquisition alone. Those facts may be relevant, but the manner of acquisition, source of funds, legal classification, treatment of debt, statutory exclusions, and supporting evidence must also be examined.

## 4. Inherited Debts and Waiver of Inheritance

Article 1148 provides that an heir succeeds to the rights and obligations pertaining to the estate, except those that belong exclusively to the decedent. It generally limits the heir’s liability for the decedent’s debts to the value of property acquired from the estate. That protection operates within a statutory system, not as permission to ignore estate procedures. An heir should preserve property and records, avoid preferential or unauthorized distributions, and determine whether an estate inventory, public notice to creditors, proof-of-claim process, or court-supervised step is appropriate.

The Civil Code contains consequences for misconduct. An heir may lose the benefit of limited liability for conduct such as gross concealment of estate property, gross falsification of the inventory, or disposition intended to defraud the decedent’s creditors. Creditor notice, the order and timing of payments, treatment of disputed claims, and delivery of a legacy can require careful coordination. Paying one claimant too early or mixing estate property with personal funds can create factual and accounting problems even when the underlying liability rule is clear.

Waiver is a separate election. Under Article 1174, an heir who chooses waiver of inheritance must make a written declaration to the court within three months after learning of the right to inherit. An oral statement, a private family agreement, failure to collect property, or non-participation in discussions does not complete the statutory court procedure. The effect on the next persons entitled to inherit, the required notice, the proper court, filing evidence, and any representation needed for a minor should be checked promptly.

The Ministry of Finance’s Taiwan Tax Portal page “Inheritance Procedures and Required Documents” was updated on June 25, 2026. It describes general three-month periods for court procedures concerning an estate inventory and waiver of inheritance, and the general six-month period for filing an estate tax return. The starting date, extensions, exceptions, and jurisdiction must still be checked for the matter at hand; this general guidance must not be used to calculate an individual deadline.

Court filings, estate-tax filings, household-registration steps, and property-registration work are separate procedures administered by different institutions. Completing one does not necessarily complete or extend another. Urgent advice may be needed when a limitation period is approaching, property is at risk, a creditor is demanding payment, or an heir is located abroad.

## 5. The Surviving Parent’s Rights and Duties

Under Article 1089 of Taiwan’s Civil Code, when one parent cannot exercise rights and assume duties concerning a minor child, the other parent ordinarily does so. Accordingly, if the surviving parent retains parental rights and no contrary court order exists, that parent ordinarily continues to exercise parental rights and assume parental duties. Existing orders, statutory grounds for restriction or suspension, conflicts of interest, cross-border factors, and the child’s best interests may still require judicial consideration.

The correct legal frame is the exercise of parental rights and assumption of parental duties, not an automatic custody award. Those responsibilities can include daily care and upbringing; decisions about residence, medical care, and education; handling civil-status matters; legal representation; and management of the child’s property. The scope of authority may be affected by an existing judgment, agreement approved or recognized by a court, protective order, statutory restriction, or suspension of some or all parental rights.

Article 1089 also reflects the child’s best interests when judicial resolution is required for serious disagreement between parents. After a death, courts may still need to consider the validity and territorial effect of earlier orders, the surviving parent’s legal authority, an asserted ground for restriction or suspension, or a matter in which the parent cannot represent the child because of a conflict of interest. A foreign residence or order may add questions of international jurisdiction, governing law, recognition and enforcement, or cooperation between authorities.

Parental rights do not determine inheritance. A surviving parent may or may not be an heir of the decedent, depending on the parent’s legal relationship to the decedent and the succession rules. Conversely, a minor’s status as an heir does not transfer ownership of the inherited property to the surviving parent. Status, representation, management, and beneficial ownership must each be analyzed on its own legal basis.

## 6. Guardian Designation and Court Involvement

Article 1091 makes guardianship of a minor relevant when the minor has no parent or when both parents cannot exercise rights and assume duties concerning the child. The death of one parent does not by itself commence guardianship if the other parent can lawfully continue to act. A guardian is therefore not simply another name for a surviving parent exercising parental rights and duties.

Under Article 1093, a parent who is exercising rights and assuming duties concerning a minor may appoint a guardian by will. That appointment operates within the statutory guardianship framework and does not displace a parent who remains legally able to exercise parental rights. The will’s validity, whether the conditions for guardianship have arisen, the appointee’s eligibility, required reporting to the court, and preparation of an inventory of the minor’s property all require review.

If no testamentary guardian validly takes office, Article 1094 provides a statutory order for determining a guardian: grandparents living in the same household as the minor, older siblings living in the same household as the minor, and grandparents not living in that household. When a guardian cannot be determined under that order, the court has authority, on an application by a statutorily authorized person, to appoint or replace a suitable guardian and to prescribe the manner of guardianship. Potential applicants include the minor, qualifying relatives, the public prosecutor, the competent authority, and other interested persons within the statutory framework.

Article 1094-1 requires the court to use the ward’s best interests when selecting or changing a guardian. The court considers matters such as the minor’s age, health, needs, and views; the proposed guardian’s willingness, character, health, finances, and record; and the relationship between the minor, the proposed guardian, and other household members. These criteria make guardianship a fact-sensitive protective process rather than a private allocation of authority among adults.

## 7. Protecting a Minor’s Inherited Property

No. Under Articles 1087 and 1088 of Taiwan’s Civil Code, property acquired by a minor through inheritance is the child’s separate property; the parent or guardian does not become its beneficial owner. Management, use, collection of income, legal representation, and disposition must be handled in the child’s interests. A conflict of interest or a significant disposition may require a special representative or court involvement. Parental management does not permit unrestricted unilateral use of the child’s inherited property.

Article 1087 identifies property acquired by a minor through inheritance, gift, or another gratuitous title as the minor’s separate property. Article 1088 provides for joint parental management and recognizes rights of use and collection of income, while prohibiting disposition except for the child’s interests. These provisions must be read together: management authority is fiduciary in purpose and does not merge the child’s assets with a parent’s property.

Practical protection begins with segregation and records. Accounts, securities, title documents, distributions, income, expenses, tax records, and supporting receipts should identify the child’s ownership. A complete opening inventory and periodic account can show what was received, how it was valued, what income it produced, and why an expense or transaction served the child. Personal and estate funds should not be commingled with the minor’s property.

Parents are ordinarily the legal representatives of their minor child under Article 1086. When a parent’s interest conflicts with the child’s interest, however, the parent cannot provide neutral representation for that transaction or proceeding. The court may appoint a special representative on its own initiative or on an authorized application. A proposed partition, settlement, transfer, debt acknowledgment, release, purchase, or sale involving both the parent and child should be screened for conflict before anyone signs or accepts funds.

If a guardian rather than a parent manages the property, additional guardianship rules may govern preparation of an inventory, reporting, accounts, restrictions on disposition, and court supervision. Significant transactions can also engage registration, tax, company, trust, or financial-institution requirements. A protective plan should identify who may sign, whether prior court authority is needed, what valuation evidence is appropriate, and how proceeds will remain traceable for the child.

## 8. Cross-Border Family Issues

A cross-border family requires a conflicts-of-law analysis before Taiwan rules are applied. Relevant connecting factors can include each person’s nationality, domicile, habitual residence, place of death, and family status; the location and legal character of each asset; a foreign marriage or divorce; an existing parental-rights order; and the place where a child or interested party lives. Different issues in the same matter may be governed by different laws.

The Act Governing the Choice of Law in Civil Matters Involving Foreign Elements is a necessary starting point, together with any other applicable domestic statute, treaty, procedural rule, or foreign legal regime. Counsel may need to determine the governing law for succession, the form and effect of a foreign will, matrimonial property, parent-child status, legal representation, a trust, corporate interests, and rights in immovable property. International jurisdiction must be analyzed separately from governing law.

A foreign judgment or order may require recognition and enforcement before it can be relied upon in Taiwan. Foreign wills, probate grants, death certificates, marriage or divorce records, birth or adoption records, and powers of attorney may require certified copies, authentication, verification, notarization, or translation. Names, dates, and family relationships should be reconciled across passports and civil-status records before filings are made.

Tax and registration obligations can arise in more than one jurisdiction. The review may include estate, inheritance, gift, or income tax; financial-account reporting; household-registration changes; land, vehicle, or securities transfers; and corporate-register updates. Coordination is important because a document sufficient for one institution may not satisfy another, and a foreign deadline may run independently of a Taiwan procedure.

## 9. Practical Planning Checklist

1. Verify death records, family relationships, household-registration records, and existing court orders. Obtain original or certified death and status documents, check names and dates across jurisdictions, and identify any urgent order affecting a parent, minor, or estate asset. Preserve private family records securely and disclose them only for a defined legal or administrative purpose.
2. Identify assets, debts, title, insurance beneficiaries, trusts, and lifetime transfers. Record legal and beneficial ownership, account and policy details, security interests, relevant valuation dates, and the location of originals. Preserve property, transaction histories, receipts, and digital records before access is lost or an asset changes hands.
3. Review the will’s form, validity, operative provisions, and reserved portions. Locate the original, document the chain of custody, identify the executor and witnesses, and compare amendments or later instruments. Foreign documents may need authentication and an accurate translation before their effect can be assessed.
4. Calculate intestate shares separately from any residual-property distribution claim. Prepare distinct schedules for the marital-property calculation, the gross estate, debts and expenses, the net estate, and the proposed partition. State every assumption and valuation date so that changes can be traced without conflating the surviving spouse’s separate claim with succession.
5. Determine ownership and representation of a minor’s property and identify conflicts of interest. Maintain records and use accounts that preserve the child’s separate ownership, retain receipts for every use of funds, and review proposed agreements or dispositions to determine whether a special representative, action by a guardian, or court approval is required.
6. Confirm the applicable court, tax, household-registration, and property-registration procedures and deadlines. Verify jurisdiction and the starting event for each period with current official materials, ask about extensions or exceptions, and plan for originals, authentication, translation, filing fees, and proof of receipt. Seek urgent preservation measures if evidence, funds, or property may be lost.
