# Taiwan Pride and sexual-orientation discrimination at work

Company teams joined Taiwan Pride in Taipei on October 31, as shown in [Focus Taiwan's report](https://focustaiwan.tw/society/202610310009). The report described participants from Taiwan and abroad calling for greater understanding and an end to discrimination. It did not describe a workplace discrimination case involving any of those companies.

For a US company managing a Taiwan office, the legal question concerns the reason for a particular employment decision. A photograph from Pride does not establish anyone's sexual orientation. Nor does participation establish that a later workplace decision was discriminatory.

## Hiring and promotion have an express rule

Taiwan's [Gender Equality in Employment Act, Article 7](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=N0030014&flno=7), prohibits employers from treating applicants or employees differently because of sex or sexual orientation in recruitment, screening, hiring, assignment, placement, performance evaluation and promotion. Sexual orientation is expressly covered. The provision includes an exception for work whose nature is suitable only for a particular sex; that is not a general exception for a manager's preferences about sexual orientation.

Dismissal has a separate consequence. [Article 11](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=N0030014&flno=11) prohibits such differential treatment in retirement, severance, resignation and dismissal. Provisions or agreements that violate the article are void, and a termination in violation has no effect. Describing a departure as a business decision does not resolve whether its actual reason breached that prohibition.

These rules address the decision itself. A global diversity statement may explain company policy, but it cannot show why one applicant was rejected or one employee lost a role. The Taiwan decision needs its own factual account.

## Who has to explain the difference?

An employee or applicant has an initial task under [Article 31](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=N0030014&flno=31): substantiating the facts of differential treatment. The employer then bears the burden of proving that the treatment rested on factors unrelated to sex or sexual orientation, or that the nature of the work makes it suitable only for a particular sex. The burden shifts. It does not disappear.

Recruitment scores, earlier performance reviews, messages explaining a change and the timing of a decision can help identify what differed and why. Their usefulness depends on their contents. A sudden unfavorable review may need explanation, but its timing alone does not settle the employer's motive.

What was the stated criterion, and was it applied consistently? For an HR team comparing a local manager's account with records held at US headquarters, that question keeps the review focused on the actual hiring or employment decision. A later explanation can be checked against documents created when the decision was made.

## A complaint and protection from retaliation

Employees and applicants may complain to the local competent authority about violations of these discrimination provisions under [Article 34](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=N0030014&flno=34). This gives a person a statutory complaint route; it does not make the allegations proved merely because a complaint has been filed.

Protection also extends to an employee who helps another person complain. [Article 36](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=N0030014&flno=36) bars dismissal, transfer or other unfavorable treatment because an employee files a complaint under the Act or assists someone else with one. A witness's involvement can therefore raise a further question about the reason for a subsequent transfer.

For the Taiwan office, the records that explain a contested decision are the selection criteria, contemporaneous evaluations and communications about the person affected. A parade photograph cannot replace them.
