# Sole Custody, Booked Flights, and a Taiwan Court Order That Keeps the Children Home

School ends in late June. The tickets to Manchester are bought, the children have passports, and the divorce agreement says you alone exercise parental rights (an invented example). In Taiwan, that may still not get the children onto the plane.

On August 20, 2026, the New Taipei District Court ordered a parent who held sole custody not to take the two children out of Taiwan without both parents' consent until a custody case was over ([115 Jia-Zan No. 205](https://judgment.judicial.gov.tw/FJUD/printData.aspx?id=PCDU%2C115%2C%E5%AE%B6%E6%9A%AB%2C205%2C20260820%2C1)). Sole custody did not exempt that parent.

## Before the divorce, and the passport question

Which country's law governs parental rights is a question of its own. In a case with a foreign element, [Article 55 of the Act Governing the Choice of Law in Civil Matters Involving Foreign Elements](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=B0000007&flno=55) points to the child's national law, and for a child with more than one nationality, [Article 2](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=B0000007&flno=2) uses the one most closely connected. That is separate from the Taiwan court's power to make the provisional orders described below. The Civil Code rules in this section assume Taiwan law governs.

Under those rules, the general position is that parents exercise their rights over a minor child jointly, unless the law provides otherwise ([Civil Code Article 1089](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=B0000001&flno=1089)). If they disagree on an important matter, either may ask a court to decide. Once they have stopped living together for six months or more, [Article 1089-1](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=B0000001&flno=1089-1) lets a court settle custody under the divorce provisions (Articles 1055 to 1055-2) without a divorce, unless the parents have a legitimate reason for not living together or the law provides otherwise.

The passport rule works differently. [Passport Act Article 16](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=E0030001&flno=16) requires a legal representative's written consent for a minor aged 7 or over, or an application by the legal representative for a younger child, and where there are two or more legal representatives, one of them may act. Meeting that requirement does not guarantee issuance. The brake sits elsewhere: a passport is refused once a judicial authority has notified the passport authority ([Article 23(1)(ii)](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=E0030001&flno=23)). A British or American passport held by the same child is outside the Taiwan Act altogether.

## How a court bars a child from leaving

[Family Act Article 85](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=B0010048&flno=85) lets a family court make provisional orders while a non-contentious case, such as one deciding or changing custody, is pending. The Judicial Yuan's rules list what those orders may do, including forbidding anyone from taking the child away from a particular place or out of Taiwan ([Article 7(1)(iv)](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=B0010056&flno=7)).

How hard is one to get? There must be an urgent situation that an immediate order is needed to meet ([Article 4](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=B0010056&flno=4)). The court weighs the child's best interests and is told to handle such requests quickly and with priority. A parent who files for a provisional order without the main case will be asked whether to file that too, and told what happens if not ([Article 2(2)](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=B0010056&flno=2)). An appeal against the order does not, as a rule, suspend it ([Family Act Article 91](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=B0010048&flno=91)). The airport is where it bites. According to the Judicial Yuan's [published guidance](https://www.judicial.gov.tw/tw/cp-1654-57336-94446-1.html), a court that restricts a child's exit must notify the National Immigration Agency on its own motion, and must notify it again when the order lapses.

## The New Taipei order

Back in October 2024, the parents had divorced, agreeing that one of them would exercise parental rights alone. Visits for the other parent were set in a court mediation in February 2025. In January 2026 the custodial parent took the children to mainland China without consulting the other. The parent left behind said contact had been cut off, and the court found that the agreed visits had not taken place.

The children came back to Taiwan on August 19. The order came the next day. Entry and exit records showed the custodial parent had returned alone three times in between. The court held that stopping the children leaving was needed to protect the pending custody case, any later enforcement, and the children's contact with the other parent. When a Taiwan court decides custody, one factor it must weigh is whether a parent has obstructed the other's exercise of parental rights ([Civil Code Article 1055-1(1)(vi)](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=B0000001&flno=1055-1)).

## Getting a ban lifted

[Family Act Article 88](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=B0010048&flno=88) allows the court handling the main case, once a provisional order is final, to revoke or change it if the order is considered improper or no longer necessary. Events listed in [Article 89](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=B0010048&flno=89), such as a final dismissal or withdrawal of the main petition, end the order on their own. On June 25, 2026, the Taipei District Court, sitting as a three-judge appeal panel, refused to lift a ban on three children ([115 Jia-Sheng-Kang No. 42](https://judgment.judicial.gov.tw/FJUD/printData.aspx?id=TPDU%2C115%2C%E5%AE%B6%E8%81%B2%E6%8A%97%2C42%2C20260625%2C1)).

In that case, the parent seeking to lift the ban argued that the custody ruling was now largely final, so the decision to travel was theirs alone. Who may decide a child's travel, the court answered, is one question; whether a provisional order is still needed is another. The part of the ruling on how the children would be educated was still on appeal. The children held a foreign nationality and long-term residence in that country, and the parent wanted them to study there. If they left, the court thought, they might not return.

Travel had not been impossible, though. The children had earlier gone to Japan under a court settlement, after the other parent accepted security and agreed. Both bans in these cases took the same form: no departure without both parents' consent.

What the two courts looked at was concrete: entry and exit records, residence status abroad, the plans a parent had stated, and whether earlier promises had been kept.
