# A Parcel-Pickup Job in Taiwan: Whose Package Are You Collecting?

Before collecting a parcel for someone offering you work in Taiwan, ask whose goods you will handle and why they need to pass through you. Being given a pickup code does not establish who the employer is or what the job involves.

Changhua police's [June 27, 2025 recruitment-fraud warning](https://www.chpb.gov.tw/jpb/Announcement/C122100?ID=08326175-b0a9-47df-bcb1-a06424020df6&PageType=1) includes parcel collection among offers to check carefully, alongside promises of high pay for easy work. Ordinary courier work is not automatically criminal; a person's knowledge and involvement need their own examination.

## What can the recruiter explain?

Ask for the employer's name, business details and the identity of the person directing you. If a company is named, contact it through a channel you find independently to confirm that the person and vacancy belong to it. A copied company name or registration document does not establish the sender's authority.

Get the job description in writing: what the parcels contain, who sends them, who receives them, why this collection route is needed, and how your fee is calculated and paid. Ask about unexplained changes of recipient or destination before accepting them. Keep the answers with the original advertisement, rather than relying on a recruiter's later explanation.

These are practical checks, not a legal clearance procedure. An employer's reassuring answer is still something to verify. If the explanation remains unclear, decline the collection while you seek advice. You do not need to collect or open a parcel to investigate the offer yourself.

## When the parcels contained other people's bank cards

In a [May 15, 2026 press release](https://www.judicial.gov.tw/tw/cp-1888-1546044-094be-1.html), Taichung District Court described its first-instance judgment in cases 114年度金訴字第3427、4336號. The defendant had collected parcels from convenience stores and station lockers and forwarded them to Tainan. They contained ATM cards that people had been tricked into sending; the accounts were subsequently used by a fraud group.

He was an Investigation Bureau officer at the time. The court considered his specialist training and experience, the unusual collection and forwarding arrangements, and the payment. It found intentional participation and treated him as a joint perpetrator, rejecting his argument that he was at most an accessory. The release expressly says the judgment could be appealed; it does not establish that the decision is final.

His professional background was part of that particular assessment. This case does not establish what another job applicant knew. It does show why moving other people's cards can raise criminal questions even without lending out your own bank account.

## What you understood, and what you did

Under [Criminal Code Article 13](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=C0000001&flno=13), a person acts intentionally if they know the facts that constitute an offence and intend them to occur. Foreseeing their occurrence and accepting that outcome also counts as intent. A warning sign is a reason to investigate a job; it does not, by itself, establish that a particular applicant had that state of mind.

[Article 30](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=C0000001&flno=30) separately addresses helping another person commit an offence. Whether someone was an accessory or a joint perpetrator requires examination of their knowledge and actual role. Calling the work “just delivery” does not answer that question.

For that assessment, the sequence matters. What were you told when you accepted the job? Did later instructions reveal a different task? When did you raise a concern, and what happened next? Keep the contemporaneous messages, including any questions you asked and the replies. Do not rewrite them to fit what you now think happened.

## If you have already collected a parcel

For suspected fraud, the [Changhua warning](https://www.chpb.gov.tw/jpb/Announcement/C122100?ID=08326175-b0a9-47df-bcb1-a06424020df6&PageType=1) directs readers to Taiwan's 165 anti-fraud hotline or the police. Pause further collections while seeking their guidance. Explain whether a parcel is still with you and ask how it should be handled. Keep it intact; do not open, discard or forward it on the recruiter's instructions.

Preserve the job advertisement, account names and contact details, complete message history, pickup codes, collection times and locations, transport receipts, tracking details and records of any fee paid. If the parcel has already been handed over, record where, when and to whom, distinguishing a name you were given from an identity you independently know. Keep original Chinese messages alongside any translation you use.

These records help a Taiwan criminal lawyer assess your position; they do not guarantee immunity or a particular outcome. If police have asked you to attend an interview, show the lawyer the notice and original job instructions. The [separate guide to police questioning](/en/columns/taiwan-police-questioning-foreigner-rights) explains the issues that arise at that stage.
