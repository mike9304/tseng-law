# If you win a civil lawsuit in Taiwan, does the losing side pay your lawyer's fees?

Winning a civil lawsuit in Taiwan rarely brings your attorney's fees back. [Article 78 of the Code of Civil Procedure (民事訴訟法)](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=B0010001&flno=78) puts litigation costs on the losing party, yet Taiwan law draws a sharp line between court costs and private counsel bills. In an ordinary district-court or high-court suit, each side absorbs its own lawyer's fees.

That split catches foreign companies off guard. For an unpaid invoice, compare the amount you expect to collect with the fees your own lawyer would charge through trial and appeal.

## What "litigation costs" actually include

Start with what does shift. When you win outright, the judgment orders the losing side to reimburse litigation costs (訴訟費用). If both sides win in part and lose in part, [Article 79](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=B0010001&flno=79) lets the court split those costs proportionally, assign them to one side, or leave each party carrying its own outlay. Even a winning party can be ordered to bear costs caused by steps that were not necessary to assert or defend its rights ([Article 81](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=B0010001&flno=81)).

Court filing fees (裁判費) are the biggest item on that bill. [Article 77-23](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=B0010001&flno=77-23) adds necessary out-of-pocket court expenses: copying, photography, transcription and translation of court papers, witness and appraiser daily allowances and travel, publication fees, and court-approved appraiser compensation. The court returns any unused portion of an advance deposit after the case ends.

Private counsel fees for first and second instance are missing from that list. Taiwan does not require a lawyer to appear in ordinary first- or second-instance civil litigation, so the Code treats hiring one there as a party's own choice rather than a statutory cost of suit.

## Current Taiwan civil court filing fees

Filing fees scale with the amount or value in dispute, and they went up across lower brackets recently. [Article 77-13](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=B0010001&flno=77-13) sets the base tiers, while [Article 77-27](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=B0010001&flno=77-27) authorizes high courts to add surcharges of up to 50% with Judicial Yuan approval. Under the [Taiwan High Court surcharge standard](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawAll.aspx?pcode=B0010054) amended on December 30, 2024 (in force as of November 23, 2026), surcharges stand at 50% up to NT$100,000, 30% from NT$100,000 to NT$10,000,000, and 10% above NT$10,000,000. Any fraction below NT$10,000 rounds up to a full NT$10,000.

| Claim value (NT$) | First-instance fee (with surcharge) | Appeal fee (2nd or 3rd instance) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Up to NT$100,000 | NT$1,500 | NT$2,250 |
| Over NT$100,000 to NT$1,000,000 | NT$130 per NT$10,000 | NT$195 per NT$10,000 |
| Over NT$1,000,000 to NT$10,000,000 | NT$117 per NT$10,000 | NT$175.5 per NT$10,000 |
| Over NT$10,000,000 to NT$100,000,000 | NT$88 per NT$10,000 | NT$132 per NT$10,000 |
| Over NT$100,000,000 to NT$1,000,000,000 | NT$77 per NT$10,000 | NT$115.5 per NT$10,000 |
| Over NT$1,000,000,000 | NT$66 per NT$10,000 | NT$99 per NT$10,000 |

Put numbers on an ordinary dispute. Filing a NT$1,000,000 invoice claim costs NT$13,200 at first instance. Filing an ordinary NT$10,000,000 civil property claim costs NT$118,500. Non-property claims (such as status declarations) carry a flat base fee of NT$3,000 under [Article 77-14](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=B0010001&flno=77-14), which becomes NT$4,500 with the 50% surcharge.

Appeals cost more. Under [Article 77-16](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=B0010001&flno=77-16) and Article 3 of the surcharge standard, taking a case to the second instance—or again to the third instance—adds 50% on top of the first-instance amount. You prepay your own filing fee when you sue or appeal; only after final judgment do you collect the shifted share back from the losing side.

## When lawyer's fees do become litigation costs

Two mandatory-representation regimes carve out exceptions. Both come with strict ceilings.

First, appeals to the Supreme Court (third instance) require a licensed lawyer under [Article 466-1](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=B0010001&flno=466-1), unless the appellant, a statutory agent, or a qualifying relative or in-house corporate employee holds a lawyer's license and meets the statutory conditions. Because representation is mandatory there, [Article 466-3](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=B0010001&flno=466-3) makes third-instance attorney's fees part of litigation costs, subject to a maximum cap. The same rule covers counsel appointed by the court under [Article 77-25](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=B0010001&flno=77-25), which requires the court to fix the fee amount in its final judgment (or by ruling on application if the case ends without judgment).

How high can that court-fixed fee go? The [Judicial Yuan standard on court-appointed and third-instance lawyer fees](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawAll.aspx?pcode=B0010037) (法院選任律師及第三審律師酬金核定支給標準) sets the ceiling in Article 4. For property claims, the court weighs complexity, outcome and counsel's diligence within 3% of the claim value, capped at NT$500,000. For non-property suits, the ceiling is NT$150,000 (NT$300,000 for joined non-property claims; NT$500,000 when combined with property claims). If your actual retainer agreement set a lower fee, the court cannot award more than that agreed amount. And Article 5 counts by case, regardless of how many lawyers you hired.

Second, qualifying commercial cases heard by the Intellectual Property and Commercial Court follow the [Commercial Case Adjudication Act (商業事件審理法)](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=B0010071&flno=6). Article 6 mandates attorney representation, and [Article 13](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=B0010071&flno=13) makes attorney's fees part of litigation or proceeding costs up to a Judicial Yuan cap. Not every company dispute qualifies for that court; which shareholder and corporate claims cross its thresholds is explained in [our column on Taiwan's Commercial Court jurisdiction](/en/columns/taiwan-commercial-court-jurisdiction-threshold).

## Contract clauses, fee transparency, and planning a budget

What if your supply agreement or loan contract already says "the defaulting party shall pay all reasonable attorney's fees"? Taiwan's Code of Civil Procedure does not turn a private contract clause into court-taxed litigation costs automatically. You have to plead and prove the clause as a contractual claim (or damages item) in the suit itself, and a court still examines its scope and whether the claimed amount is reasonable. Never assume a boilerplate English fee-shifting paragraph guarantees full recovery in Taipei.

Before signing a Taiwan engagement letter, check two guardrails in Taiwan's [Attorney Regulation Act (律師法)](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=I0020006&flno=47). Article 47 obliges a Taiwan lawyer to state clearly to the client both the calculation method and the amount of legal fees. [Article 32](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=I0020006&flno=32) prohibits a lawyer from unilaterally terminating an accepted mandate without justifiable cause and requires advance notice, steps to protect the client's rights, and return of any unearned portion of the fee. Taiwan lawyers commonly quote a flat fee per instance (first, second, third) or hourly billing, while court filing fees, certified translations and notarization are billed separately as disbursements. For what else to check before sending a power of attorney from abroad, see [our guide to instructing a Taiwan lawyer from outside Taiwan](/en/columns/hire-taiwan-lawyer-from-abroad).

A realistic Taiwan litigation budget therefore splits into three lines: court filing fees (generally recoverable from the other side if you win outright and can collect), disbursements such as translations and appraisals, and your own lawyer's fees for each instance. Weigh all three against the debtor's attachable assets in Taiwan before filing.
