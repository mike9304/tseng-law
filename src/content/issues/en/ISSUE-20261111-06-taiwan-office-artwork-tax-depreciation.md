# Collectible art in a Taiwan office: the depreciation limit

NT$500,000 in claimed depreciation was disallowed in the tax audit described by the National Taxation Bureau of Taipei on November 9. The company had bought paintings and sculptures and treated them as depreciable fixed assets. It had booked NT$2 million for the works and used a four-year depreciation schedule. The [bureau's release](https://www.ntbt.gov.tw/singlehtml/41ae3594197f4f69b47753ce08188516?cntId=3d618a979ed240ddb89d3fa486fae45e) says the works had collectible value and did not qualify. Additional tax followed.

For a foreign-owned business furnishing a Taiwan office, the issue begins with the asset's nature. The bureau's explanation covers antiques and artwork displayed as decoration in offices or business premises. It says their value does not necessarily decline with use or time and may increase. An office location does not resolve the tax classification.

## Eligibility comes before the depreciation schedule

[Article 54 of the Income Tax Act](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=G0340003&flno=54) requires annual depreciation for depreciable fixed assets and an accumulated-depreciation account deducted from the corresponding asset. It also requires estimated residual value to be excluded from the amount used to calculate depreciation. These rules concern assets that qualify as depreciable in the first place.

The bureau applies that distinction to collectible art. Business use alone did not make the collectible artworks in the reported audit depreciable. A company may have paid for an item, recorded it in its asset register and displayed it for clients, yet still face the classification question identified in the release.

Would spreading the cost over a different number of years solve the problem? No change of schedule answers the underlying issue. In the reported audit, the bureau rejected depreciation because the artworks lacked the required depreciable character.

## The purchase cost answers a different question

Cost still has a defined meaning. Under [Article 45](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=G0340003&flno=45), an asset acquired for a price has an actual cost that includes the purchase price and necessary expenditure incurred to acquire it and make it suitable for business use. Establishing that amount does not itself establish a right to depreciate the asset.

An invoice supports the purchase and price. Its description identifies the asset. A broad description such as office furnishings may give headquarters too little information to distinguish a collectible painting from other purchases included in the same budget. Keeping the underlying descriptions visible lets the Taiwan tax analysis address each item's nature.

The bureau also cites [Article 59](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=G0340003&flno=59), which governs depletion of wasting assets. That provision contains methods tied to extraction or revenue from the resource. It is not an alternative annual deduction for paintings. The bureau's release expressly rejects depletion and amortization for the collectible antiques and artwork it describes.

## What the reported adjustment shows

In its audit of the company's 2023 return, the bureau disallowed the depreciation and assessed NT$100,000 in additional tax. This was a tax adjustment. The release does not describe that amount as a fine, and the example does not set a fixed charge for other businesses buying art.

For an international group's Taiwan office, the purchase record and asset register can therefore tell only part of the tax story. The particular work's description, the reason it is held and the proposed tax treatment need to remain visible in the local review. A finished depreciation spreadsheet cannot supply a missing basis for classifying a collectible work as depreciable.
