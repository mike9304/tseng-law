# Who controls your overseas company when it opens a Taiwan branch?

A Singapore-registered company had an approved branch in Taiwan. A [November 24 report by Taiwan's Central News Agency](https://www.cna.com.tw/news/asoc/202611240237.aspx) describes a later dispute over whether the mainland side had effective operational control. The Ministry of Economic Affairs examined technical support from mainland companies and the roles of two mainland-national technical directors. CNA reports a NT$2.17 million fine and an order to withdraw or correct the investment within six months.

For another overseas company, the question arises before a Taiwan branch opens: which investment route applies when the register of shareholders tells only part of the story?

## Ownership is one test; control is another

[Article 3 of the Regulation Governing Permission for People of the Mainland Area to Invest in Taiwan](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=Q0040015&flno=3) covers a company formed in a third jurisdiction if a mainland person or entity directly or indirectly holds more than 30% of its shares or capital contribution. It also covers that company if a mainland person or entity has the ability to control it. These are alternative conditions. Exactly 30% does not meet the ownership threshold stated in the rule, although the control condition must still be considered.

The second test stands on its own.

Neither an executive's nationality nor an ordinary technical services contract answers the control question by itself. Those circumstances explain why a company may need to look beyond its ownership chart. They do not establish that every branch employing mainland engineers falls into the same category.

## Classification comes before branch registration

Under [Article 73(1) of the Act Governing Relations between the People of the Taiwan Area and the Mainland Area](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=Q0010001&flno=73), mainland persons and entities, including their companies invested in a third jurisdiction, may not invest in Taiwan without permission from the competent authority. The [Ministry's current branch-registration guidance](https://gcis.nat.gov.tw/mainNew/subclassNAction.do?method=getFile&pk=5) says a foreign company in this category must seek investment permission from the Department of Investment Review before applying to register a Taiwan branch. Its business lines must also include an activity open to mainland investment.

The permit comes first.

For a founder who has already prepared branch-registration papers, the classification may change the filing route. An earlier branch approval does not settle a later question about who controls the overseas company. The present facts need to be compared with those supplied for the original approval.

## What an existing branch can put on the table

Useful documents include the ultimate ownership record, any shareholder or management agreements that affect decision-making, technical-services contracts, and records showing who can direct the branch's products and operations. An overseas head office may have changed its suppliers or managers since the original application. A change alone does not prove a breach; its effect on the company's classification and permitted activities must be assessed.

[Article 93-1(1) of the same Act](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=Q0010001&flno=93-1) allows an administrative fine of NT$120,000 to NT$25 million for investment that violates Article 73(1), together with an order to stop, withdraw or correct the investment. The NT$2.17 million reported in the Singapore-company matter is a case-specific amount, not a standard charge for all third-country branches. A clear account of who made operational decisions is the starting point for testing the branch's approval route.
