# Buying Taiwan Shares Directly: A US Investor's Tax Checklist

You want to buy TSMC shares on the Taiwan exchange, or perhaps the Taiwan-listed 0050 ETF, through an account that gives you direct access to Taiwan securities. The first question is how to open that account. The next is how a sale or dividend is taxed in Taiwan and what your US return may still need to report.

This article concerns **direct Taiwan-listed holdings** by a US person who is not a Taiwan income-tax resident. A US-listed receipt or US-domiciled fund can have a different legal and tax structure. Taiwan residency, the type of security and the legal owner on the account can change the answer; see the separate [Taiwan income-tax residency guide](/en/columns/taiwan-income-tax-residency) if your time in Taiwan makes that uncertain.

## How does a foreign individual get direct market access?

[Article 10 of the Regulations Governing Investment in Securities by Overseas Chinese and Foreign Nationals](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=G0400011&flno=10) requires registration with the Taiwan Stock Exchange under its rules before foreign nationals invest in Taiwan securities, subject to the article's limited exceptions. The [TWSE's foreign-investment guidance](https://www.twse.com.tw/en/products/education/foreign/overview.html) explains that a foreign investor obtains an investor ID before opening a local brokerage trading account. For a foreign individual **in Taiwan**, the [TWSE describes](https://www.twse.com.tw/en/products/education/foreign/regulations/onshore.html) a securities broker submitting the registration and then opening the account. For a person **outside Taiwan**, its [offshore guidance](https://www.twse.com.tw/en/products/education/foreign/regulations/offshore.html) describes a domestic agent or representative and the registration before brokerage account opening.

Ask the broker which path fits your residence and documentation, who will be named as the registered investor, and how cash and custody will be handled. Do not assume that access through a foreign app gives you a Taiwan brokerage account or the same beneficial ownership record. Keep the registration, account agreement and transaction statements; they later help identify who receives dividends and who may claim shares in an estate.

## What happens in Taiwan when I sell shares or an ETF?

Taiwan's [Securities Transaction Tax Act](https://law-out.mof.gov.tw/LawContent.aspx?id=FL006079&media=print) imposes a tax on the **seller's transaction price**, separate from income tax on any gain. The [Ministry of Finance's rate guidance](https://www.etax.nat.gov.tw/etwmain/tax-info/understanding/tax-saving-manual/national/securities-transaction-tax/JNxPwlJ) and [stock FAQ](https://www.etax.nat.gov.tw/etwmain/tax-info/understanding/tax-q-and-a/national/securities-transaction-tax/filing/mM8n39b) give the ordinary stock sale rate as **0.3% of the sale price**. The [MOF's beneficiary-certificate FAQ](https://www.etax.nat.gov.tw/etwmain/tax-info/understanding/tax-q-and-a/national/securities-transaction-tax/filing/KJZD0QV) gives **0.1%** for qualifying investment-trust beneficiary certificates, the category to check for a Taiwan-listed ETF such as 0050. Confirm the security's actual classification and any special transaction rule before applying a rate. The tax is borne by the seller; the [MOF explains](https://www.etax.nat.gov.tw/etwmain/tax-info/understanding/tax-saving-manual/national/securities-transaction-tax/mbo7V96) how it is collected.

At the same time, [Income Tax Act Article 4-1](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=G0340003&flno=4-1) says income tax on securities transaction gains is suspended and the corresponding loss cannot be deducted from taxable income. That is a **Taiwan income-tax rule**, not a declaration that a US person has no US capital-gains tax. Keep the buy and sell confirmations, fees and currency conversion records for the US calculation. A sale in a special instrument or nonstandard structure needs its own classification rather than a blanket assumption based on the stock examples here.

## How are Taiwan dividends paid to me taxed?

For a **nonresident individual**, [Article 3(1) of the Withholding Rates Standards](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=G0340028&flno=3) sets Taiwan withholding on company dividends at **21% of the amount paid**. This is the domestic-law rate relevant to an ordinary Taiwan-company dividend paid to the assumed investor. An ETF distribution can contain different components or have different payer and reporting details; obtain the distribution and withholding statement rather than treating every cash payment as a company dividend. If you are a Taiwan tax resident, the nonresident rule is the wrong starting point.

The [IRS list of US income tax treaties in force](https://www.irs.gov/businesses/international-businesses/united-states-income-tax-treaties-a-to-z) does not include Taiwan, and Taiwan's [Ministry of Finance treaty list](https://www.mof.gov.tw/singlehtml/191?cntId=63930) shows no comprehensive income tax agreement with the United States; the US appears only under a 1988 shipping and air-transport agreement. There is **no US–Taiwan income tax treaty in force as of September 30, 2026** that reduces this Taiwan dividend withholding for a US resident. H.R. 33, the United States–Taiwan Expedited Double-Tax Relief Act, passed the House on January 15, 2025 and was referred to the Senate Finance Committee; as of September 30, 2026 it has **not become law** ([Congress.gov bill status](https://www.congress.gov/bill/119th-congress/house-bill/33/all-actions)). The [House-engrossed text](https://www.govinfo.gov/app/details/BILLS-119hr33eh) is a bill, not an enacted treaty or tax rate. Do not plan a dividend on a hoped-for future rate.

## What may the US return require?

US tax is a separate calculation. The IRS's [Form 1116 instructions](https://www.irs.gov/instructions/i1116) explain that an eligible foreign income tax paid or accrued on foreign-source income may support a **foreign tax credit**, subject to limitations, categories, holding-period rules and documentation. The [IRS foreign-tax-credit page](https://www.irs.gov/individuals/international-taxpayers/foreign-tax-credit) gives the general route; it does not say every amount withheld in Taiwan becomes a dollar-for-dollar US credit. Keep the Taiwan withholding certificate and payment details. Have a US adviser decide creditability, source and category, and whether Form 1116 or an exception applies. Taiwan's transaction tax is not automatically an income tax eligible for that credit.

For a Taiwan fund, ask about **passive foreign investment company (PFIC)** rules before investing. Under IRC section 1297, as summarized in the [IRS Form 8621 instructions](https://www.irs.gov/instructions/i8621), a foreign corporation meets the PFIC definition if at least **75% of gross income is passive** or at least **50% of its average assets produce, or are held to produce, passive income**. The [IRS Form 8621 page](https://www.irs.gov/forms-pubs/about-form-8621) describes reporting by certain US shareholders. These rules involve the fund's legal and US tax classification and the relevant year. **This article does not establish that 0050 is, or is not, a PFIC.** Ask a US tax adviser about that specific holding, possible Form 8621 filing and any elections before a purchase or distribution creates a difficult reporting history. Do not assume direct TSMC shares and a Taiwan fund have the same US treatment.

If the investment uses a Taiwan financial account, the IRS says certain foreign brokerage or bank accounts can trigger an [FBAR on FinCEN Form 114](https://www.irs.gov/businesses/small-businesses-self-employed/report-of-foreign-bank-and-financial-accounts-fbar) when the aggregate foreign-account value passes its rule. [Form 8938](https://www.irs.gov/forms-pubs/about-form-8938) separately concerns specified foreign financial assets above applicable thresholds. The [IRS comparison](https://www.irs.gov/businesses/comparison-of-form-8938-and-fbar-requirements) makes clear that these are distinct reporting regimes. The account structure, assets, filing status and residence determine which rules apply; a US adviser can check both.

## What happens if the investor dies owning Taiwan shares?

Tax planning does not end with annual returns. [Article 9(8) of Taiwan's Estate and Gift Tax Act](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=G0340072&flno=9) locates shares by the head office of the issuing or invested business. Shares of a company headquartered in Taiwan can therefore be Taiwan-situs estate property even if the investor and heirs lived elsewhere. For the nationality and residence rules, filing and transfer documents, read [Taiwan estate tax after a death abroad](/en/columns/taiwan-estate-tax-foreign-decedent). Do not infer that every Taiwan-listed ETF interest is located in the same way without checking its legal form and issuer.
