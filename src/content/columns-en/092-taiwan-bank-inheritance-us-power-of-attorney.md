# Inheriting a Taiwan Bank Account From the U.S.: What to Check Before Signing a Power of Attorney

Before ordering U.S. death certificates or signing a power of attorney, ask the Taiwan bank holding the account for its inheritance requirements. Contact the branch handling the release and request its current forms, supporting-document list, and instructions for someone signing in the United States. Have the branch confirm which documents it expects in your circumstances before you pay for certified copies, translations, or authentication.

Living in the United States describes your residence, not your citizenship. You may be Taiwanese, American, or a dual national. Identify your residence and citizenship separately, along with the deceased account holder’s nationality or nationalities. These facts help frame the legal review; a U.S. mailing address does not determine which inheritance law applies. For that broader question, see the discussion of [which succession law governs a Taiwan inheritance](https://tseng-law.com/en/columns/foreign-heir-taiwan-succession-law-land).

## Four questions behind a bank release

Four questions need separate answers: Who is entitled to inherit? Who has authority to act? How must the documents’ authenticity be established? What tax-clearance evidence is needed for release? An authenticated POA addresses only part of this work. It cannot, by itself, establish the estate’s heirs or satisfy the bank’s entire release requirements.

An executor or personal representative administers an estate under the applicable appointment and law. An heir inherits under the governing succession rules. A beneficiary receives under a will or another applicable arrangement. An agent under a living heir’s POA acts within that heir’s authorization. One person may occupy several roles, but the roles are not interchangeable.

California [Letters, form DE-150](https://selfhelp.courts.ca.gov/jcc-form/DE-150), can evidence a court-appointed personal representative’s appointment to carry out estate duties. Their effect on a particular Taiwan account requires separate assessment. Ask the bank how it would evaluate the letters and accompanying documents. If authority remains unclear, obtain Taiwan legal review before commissioning further paperwork.

Likewise, do not treat a POA signed by the deceased during life as authorization newly granted by the living heirs. Have its continuing effect and the authority needed for the inheritance application reviewed.

## Read the authority to close and collect

Bank of Panhsin offers a useful example of why the POA belongs within a larger document package. Its [deposit-inheritance FAQ (in Chinese)](https://www.bop.com.tw/Footer/QA?tni=93) lists an inheritance application, account documents, evidence of the death and of all lawful heirs, identification, and tax-payment or exemption evidence, subject to its stated conditions. Delegated applications require additional papers. Its overseas-resident instructions call for an authorization certified by a local Taiwan overseas mission or an authorized attesting body, along with specified seal and agent-identification items.

That FAQ is evidence of Panhsin’s procedures, not the law defining inheritance rights. Ask the branch which overseas instructions apply to you. Do not automatically import the domestic seal-certificate instructions printed on a form into a U.S. signing package. Nor should another institution’s instructions substitute for your bank’s: [Chunghwa Post’s inheritance guidance](https://subservices.post.gov.tw/post/internet/U_english2/index.jsp?ID=3509010204) concerns its separate postal savings service.

Panhsin’s [deposit-inheritance POA, CM2010 (in Chinese)](https://www.bop.com.tw/api/ArticleFile/5e4ca812-1920-4854-bc61-7c3e4fff8722), illustrates what deserves attention before signature. It authorizes the agent to apply for inheritance, close the account, and receive the remaining balance. It also states that the agent’s acts bind the signer and that the signer and agent will jointly and severally bear full legal liability if the bank or a third party is harmed as a result. This describes the form’s undertaking, not a conclusion about its enforceability or an assumption of every estate debt.

Before signing, ask whether the authority can be confined to the identified account and necessary transactions, where payment will go, and what supporting papers must accompany the authorization. Ask whether the bank accepts payment directly to the intended recipient rather than receipt by the agent. Any proposed restriction or change needs the bank’s acceptance; handwriting a limitation onto its form does not establish that acceptance.

## Before signing or ordering documents

Use this bank-first document checklist to turn those questions into a package the branch can review:

1. Ask for the branch’s current requirements in writing. Request the inheritance application, POA, and any declarations. Ask who reviews overseas documents and whether the branch will check drafts before signature.

2. Confirm evidence of death and entitlement. Ask which death record, family-status records, will, or other supporting documents fit this estate. If Taiwan household records are unavailable, ask what alternative evidence the branch will evaluate.

3. Identify each signer’s capacity. Explain whether the proposed applicant acts as an heir, personal representative, beneficiary, or authorized agent. Ask whose signatures or separate authorizations are needed and which appointment papers should accompany them.

4. Settle identity, names, and translations. Compare Chinese names, English spellings, married names, and dates across the papers. Ask how discrepancies should be documented, which identification is acceptable, and whether translations require separate certification.

5. Approve the POA’s scope and payment instructions. Confirm the account covered, permitted acts, receipt destination, and supporting papers. Obtain acceptance of any revised wording before executing it.

6. Confirm authentication and tax-clearance documents. Check the route for each U.S. document and ask which Taiwan tax-payment, exemption, or other release evidence applies. Keep these as separate tasks; the [estate-tax discussion for foreign decedents](https://tseng-law.com/en/columns/taiwan-estate-tax-foreign-decedent) provides broader context.

7. Confirm delivery and final review. Ask which originals the bank retains, whether preliminary scans are accepted, and how originals should reach the branch. Keep a complete copy and request a list of unresolved items before shipping.

## Is a California apostille enough?

For U.S. documents, “apostille” can cause confusion. California, for example, issues a single certificate called an Apostille for use outside the United States, regardless of destination, according to the [California Secretary of State’s FAQ](https://www.sos.ca.gov/notary/apostille-faqs). The [U.S. State Department’s Taiwan judicial-assistance page](https://travel.state.gov/content/travel/en/legal/Judicial-Assistance-Country-Information/Taiwan.html), whose substantive guidance is dated November 15, 2013, identifies Taiwan as outside the Hague Apostille Convention. California’s certificate may be a step in the accepted process; its name does not give it Convention effect for Taiwan or confer inheritance authority.

Taiwan’s [Document Legalization Act, including the 2017 amendments](https://law.mofa.gov.tw/EngLawContent.aspx?id=26&lan=E), draws a related distinction between authenticity and content. Article 15 limits authentication to signatures, seals, or a document’s formal existence; it does not validate substantive content. Article 15-1 covers documents from a country that has signed a written agreement with Taiwan abolishing repeat legalization: once that country’s designated authority legalizes them, they have the same effect as legalization by a Taiwan overseas mission. It is not an automatic exemption for U.S. documents.

Confirm the route with the responsible Taiwan overseas mission, commonly called TECO—the Taipei Economic and Cultural Office. Give it the precise document type, issuing state, and proposed signing location. Ask which mission has jurisdiction, whose signature must be authenticated, what preliminary certification is needed, and how translations should be handled. Taiwan’s [Bureau of Consular Affairs recommends checking with the mission where you plan to apply (in Chinese)](https://www.boca.gov.tw/cp-43-334-ef805-1.html) because procedures vary by document and jurisdiction.

Also confirm whether that mission accepts the proposed mailing route. Taiwan’s [implementing rules (in Chinese)](https://law.mofa.gov.tw/LawContent.aspx?id=GL000007) permit overseas mail applications where the mission has announced acceptance. Do not assume that one mailing process works for every document.

Before authorizing receipt, clarify whether the agreed payment method completes only the Taiwan release or also involves a later transfer abroad; [remittance requirements are a separate question](https://tseng-law.com/en/columns/taiwan-fx-remittance-declaration).

Estates involving minors, trusts, or disputes need individualized legal review before this checklist is used.
