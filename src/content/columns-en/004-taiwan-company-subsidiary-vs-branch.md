# Taiwan Company Formation: Subsidiary vs Branch

![Featured image](../images/004-taiwan-company-subsidiary-vs-branch/featured-01.jpg)



Should I establish a subsidiary in Taiwan, or a Taiwan branch office?

![](../images/004-taiwan-company-subsidiary-vs-branch/img-01.jpg)

Hello. I’m Attorney Wei Tseng, a Taiwan lawyer.

I previously gave a brief introduction to Taiwan company formation.

You should already have a basic understanding of company types.

Today I want to take the explanation one step further.

If you already have a company in Korea and want to start doing business in Taiwan,

is it better to establish a subsidiary,

or to establish a branch?

After reading this article, you should have a clear framework!

First, let me restate the biggest difference between a subsidiary and a branch.

A subsidiary has an independent legal personality,

and can enjoy rights and obligations equivalent to those of a Taiwanese company.

A branch has no legal personality; it only has the qualification to conduct business.

When deciding whether to establish a subsidiary or a branch, the main considerations are the following five points.

1. Whether you will jointly invest with other shareholders



If you plan to form a company jointly with others,

you must establish a subsidiary.

In the case of a branch, however,

it must be 100% owned by the Korean company,

and other Taiwanese individuals or Taiwanese legal entities cannot participate as shareholders.



2. Tax considerations

Please see the table below.

|  |  |  |
| --- | --- | --- |
|  | **Subsidiary**  **(limited company, company limited by shares)** | **Branch** |
| **Value-added tax**  **(business tax, 營業稅)** | **5%** | **5%** |
| **Corporate income tax**  **(profit-seeking enterprise income tax,**  **營利事業所得稅)** | **20%** | **20%** |
| **Distribution of dividends/profits**  **(foreigner income tax — withholding on company surplus distributions,**  **外國人所得稅-公司盈餘應分配額)** | **21%** | **Ｘ** |
| **Undistributed earnings tax**  **(未分配盈餘稅)** | **5%** | **Ｘ** |

Both a subsidiary and a branch must file value-added tax,

that is, business tax (營業稅), every two months, at a rate of 5%.

They must also file corporate income tax—profit-seeking enterprise income tax (營利事業所得稅)—once a year, at a rate of 20%.



The differences are as follows.

When a subsidiary distributes profits to its Korean parent (foreign shareholder),

the Korean parent (foreign shareholder) must pay 21% foreigner income tax. If the subsidiary chooses not to distribute profits,

it must additionally pay a 5% undistributed earnings tax.

By contrast, because a branch is an extension of the overseas parent,

when it remits profits to the parent’s account,

it does not need to pay additional tax to the Taiwanese government,

and there is also no undistributed earnings tax.

Therefore, compared with a branch,

a subsidiary generally bears a higher tax burden,

because when a branch remits profits to the Korean parent

it does not need to pay additional tax,

whereas when a subsidiary pays dividends to the Korean parent

additional tax must be paid.



That said, subsidiaries and branches involve many other tax-related factors to consider,

including the company’s industry, projected operating costs, and how profits will be handled.

You should carefully decide which form is most advantageous

based on each enterprise’s individual circumstances.



For example,

if losses are expected in the early stage of the Taiwan business,

establishing a branch can reduce the Korean parent’s tax burden.

A subsidiary, however, is an independent legal person, so even if it incurs early losses, it cannot reduce the Korean parent’s tax burden.

3. Legal liability

If you establish a branch, the branch and the parent share the same legal personality,

so all of the branch’s obligations (debts, taxes, fines, etc.)

must be borne entirely by the parent.

By contrast, if you establish a subsidiary, the parent’s liability is limited to the amount of its capital contribution to the subsidiary,

so the risk is lower.

4. Whether to list publicly in Taiwan

If you plan a public offering in Taiwan,

only a subsidiary organized as a company limited by shares is eligible to list.

A Taiwan branch of an overseas parent cannot list publicly.



5. Tax incentives

Only a subsidiary can receive tax incentives in Taiwan.

For example, if a company established in Taiwan incurs innovative research and development expenditures and obtains approval,

it may deduct up to 30% of that year’s profit-seeking enterprise income tax.

A branch, however, cannot receive these benefits.





Finally, under the arrangement for the avoidance of double taxation and the prevention of fiscal evasion between Korea and Taiwan that took effect on December 2, 2023,

business profits are exempt from tax where there is no permanent establishment,

and the ceiling rate on dividends is 10%.



The above covers several points to consider when a Korean company establishes a Taiwan subsidiary or a Taiwan branch.
