# A Counterfeit Bought Online, Held by Taiwan Customs: Personal Use, Resale and Refunds

Taiwan's [Customs Act art. 15](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=G0350001&flno=15)(2) lists patent-, trademark- or copyright-infringing goods among those that "may not be imported." It ignores why the buyer wanted them.

## Three working days after the customs notice

Customs notifies the trademark owner and importer when imports clearly appear to infringe a trademark ([Trademark Act art. 75](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=J0070001&flno=75)(1)). The Ministry of Finance's [Regulations Governing Customs Measures in Protecting the Rights and Interests of Trademark, art. 7](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=G0350069&flno=7), set the clock. For air imports, the owner has 24 hours from notice to confirm it will assess the goods and three working days for evidence.

Importers get three working days from notice to file documents showing no infringement, in writing or on a customs-approved platform. One three-day extension is allowed for good reason, if sought before the deadline.

What counts as proof? For genuine goods, [Trademark Act art. 36](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=J0070001&flno=36)(2) bars the owner from asserting its trademark against goods that it, or someone with its consent, put on the market in Taiwan or abroad, unless needed to prevent deterioration, damage, unauthorized processing or alteration, or for other good cause. Official-store receipts and sourcing records do that work.

Three outcomes follow (regulations [arts. 8](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=G0350069&flno=8), [9](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=G0350069&flno=9)). If the owner submits evidence, the importer files nothing, and a Trademark Act art. 95 or 97 offence is suspected, customs refers the case to the judicial authorities. If the importer does file, the owner has three working days from notice to apply for detention under [Trademark Act art. 72](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=J0070001&flno=72)(1), posting security equal to the goods' dutiable value; the importer may post double to lift it (para. 2, 4), and detention ends if the owner fails to sue and notify customs within 12 days from the day after acceptance, extendable by 12 ([art. 73](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=J0070001&flno=73)(1)(1), (2)); without one, customs may take samples and process the goods under clearance rules, absent other violations, as it also does if the owner is unreachable, misses deadlines or finds no infringement.

## Bought for personal use

Trademark Act arts. 95–97 were amended in May 2022, but the amendment still awaits a commencement date ("not yet determined" on November 3, 2026) ([full Act](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawAll.aspx?pcode=J0070001)). The November 30, 2016 text applies ([prior version](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawOldVer.aspx?pcode=J0070001)). Its art. 97 punishes anyone who, "knowing" the goods bear marks used by others in breach of arts. 95 or 96, sells them, or "with intent to sell possesses, displays, exports or imports" them. Intent to sell sits in front of "imports."

Arts. 95 and 96(1) likewise require acts "for marketing purposes". No criminal provision of the Act punishes importing for one's own use without intent to sell.

No quantity threshold marks intent. Several identical items, or a sales post before the parcel lands, can put personal use in dispute.

Copyright works differently. [Copyright Act art. 87](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=J0070017&flno=87)(1)(3) deems importing unauthorized copies an infringement, with no intent to sell in the text. The personal-use exception in [art. 87-1](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=J0070017&flno=87-1)(1)(3) only disapplies art. 87(1)(4), on originals and lawful foreign copies. The art. 87(1)(3) method carries up to two years, short-term detention, or a fine up to NT$500,000, or both ([art. 93](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=J0070017&flno=93)(3)), except where art. 91-1(2) or (3) applies (proviso).

## Return, abandonment, confiscation

Under [Customs Act art. 96](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=G0350001&flno=96)(1), customs orders the duty payer to ship barred goods back; if the payer abandons them in writing or misses the deadline, customs may sell them. Unsaleable goods are destroyed under customs supervision at the payer's cost (para. 2).

[Customs Anti-smuggling Act art. 39-1](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=G0350029&flno=39-1) fines declared imports or exports that infringe patents, trademarks or copyrights, other than genuine parallel imports, up to three times their value and confiscates them, unless another law provides otherwise. A written disposition must state its legal basis and how, when and where to challenge it ([Administrative Procedure Act art. 96](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=A0030055&flno=96)(1)(2), (6)).

An art. 39-1 fine or confiscation is an anti-smuggling disposition ([art. 46](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=G0350029&flno=46)), reviewed on written application to the issuing office within 30 days from the day after notice ([art. 47](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=G0350029&flno=47)(1)), then appeal and suit ([art. 48](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=G0350029&flno=48)(1)). A Customs Act return order is not; the Customs Act's review provision ([art. 45](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=G0350001&flno=45)) covers classification, value and duties, so the order goes to administrative appeal, filed through the issuing office within 30 days from the day after it arrives, timed by receipt ([Administrative Appeal Act arts. 14](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=A0030020&flno=14)(1), (3), [58](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=A0030020&flno=58)(1)).

## Resold on social media

Knowingly selling is an art. 97 act, online included, with no floor.

| Conduct | Provision | Penalty |
|---|---|---|
| Knowingly selling counterfeit-marked goods; possessing, displaying, exporting or importing them with intent to sell (incl. online) | Trademark Act art. 97 (2016 text) | Up to 1 year, detention, or fine up to NT$50,000, or both |
| Importing unauthorized copies (unless art. 91-1(2)/(3) applies) | Copyright Act arts. 87(1)(3), 93(3) | Up to 2 years, detention, or fine up to NT$500,000, or both |
| Knowingly distributing infringing copies, or publicly displaying or possessing them to distribute | Copyright Act art. 91-1(2) (2019 text) | Up to 3 years; fine of NT$70,000–750,000 may be added |
| The same, where the copies are optical discs (except discs imported in breach of art. 87(4)) | Copyright Act art. 91-1(3) (2019 text) | 6 months to 3 years; fine of NT$200,000–2,000,000 may be added |

Infringing goods are confiscated whether or not they belong to the offender ([Trademark Act art. 98](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=J0070001&flno=98)). The May 2022 amendment to Copyright Act arts. 91 and 91-1 also lacks a commencement date ([full Act](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawAll.aspx?pcode=J0070017)), so the May 1, 2019 text applies ([prior version](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawOldVer.aspx?pcode=J0070017)); its art. 91-1(4) allows a reduced sentence under paras. 2 or 3 if the offender names the source and it is uncovered.

The owner may also demand removal and prevention of infringement, destruction of goods, and damages for intentional or negligent infringement ([Trademark Act art. 69](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=J0070001&flno=69)), lapsing two years after it learns of the damage and the liable person, or ten years after the infringement. One damages measure is up to 1,500 times the seized goods' retail unit price, or their total price if over 1,500 items were seized, reducible if clearly disproportionate ([art. 71](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=J0070001&flno=71)(1)(3), (2)).

## Getting the money back

Under [Choice of Law Act art. 20](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=B0000007&flno=20), the parties' chosen law governs; otherwise the most closely connected law applies, presumed to be the domicile law of the party owing the obligation characteristic of the contract (para. 3).

Taiwan's Civil Code makes a seller warrant that the goods are free of defects reducing their value or usefulness and have any guaranteed quality ([art. 354](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=B0000001&flno=354)). The buyer may rescind or seek a price reduction, but only a reduction where rescission would be clearly unfair ([art. 359](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=B0000001&flno=359)). Missing guaranteed quality such as "authentic," or a deliberately concealed defect, allows damages instead ([art. 360](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=B0000001&flno=360)).

Buyers must inspect promptly and notify defects at once, or the goods are deemed accepted, except for defects ordinary inspection cannot reveal; those must be notified as soon as found ([art. 356](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=B0000001&flno=356)). Rescission and reduction rights lapse six months after that notice, or five years after delivery; the six-month limit does not apply if the seller deliberately concealed the defect ([art. 365](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=B0000001&flno=365)). If customs took the parcel, whether the seller met its duty to deliver and transfer ownership ([art. 348](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=B0000001&flno=348)(1)) is also in issue.

Deceived by an "authentic" listing, a buyer may revoke the declaration ([art. 92](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=B0000001&flno=92)(1)); if a third party deceived, only where the seller knew or could have known. Revocation must come within one year of discovering the fraud, and never after ten years ([art. 93](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=B0000001&flno=93)).

What about the seven-day return right? [Consumer Protection Act art. 19](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=J0170001&flno=19)(1) lets a consumer in a distance sale with a business ([art. 2](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=J0170001&flno=2)(10)) rescind by returning the goods or by written notice within seven days after receiving them, without reasons or cost, unless a reasonable exception applies. The statute is silent on parcels never received. If the business never gave rescission information, the seven days run from the day after it does, ending four months after the original start (para. 3). Refunds are due within 15 days from the day after the goods are collected or received back ([art. 19-2](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=J0170001&flno=19-2)(2)). Complaints go to the business (15 days to handle), then a local consumer ombudsman, then mediation ([arts. 43](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=J0170001&flno=43), [44](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=J0170001&flno=44)). Enforcing against a foreign seller depends on its country's law and where its assets are. Taiwan-based sellers are covered in [a separate column](/en/columns/taiwan-online-consumer-dispute-foreigners).

Save the "genuine" listing when the notice arrives.
