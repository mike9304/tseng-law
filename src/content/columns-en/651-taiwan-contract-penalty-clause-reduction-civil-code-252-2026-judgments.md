# Penalty Clauses in Taiwan Contracts: How Courts Cut Them Down, and When They Refuse

The equipment shipped on time. The technical manuals did not, and a month later the Taiwan buyer's notice arrived: 1% of the contract price for every day of delay, deducted from the final payment (an invented example). Taiwan's Civil Code lets contracting parties agree on such a penalty, so the clause itself is usually not the weak point. The fight is over how much of it a court will keep.

## If you are the supplier and the payment was cut

What you did deliver comes first. When a debtor has partly performed, [Civil Code Article 251](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=B0000001&flno=251) lets the court reduce the penalty in light of the benefit the creditor received from that performance. The Taiwan High Court treated a similar fact as a reason to cut under [Article 252](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=B0000001&flno=252), which allows an excessive penalty to be reduced to a reasonable amount. Its case, decided on April 29, 2026, concerned equipment built for a U.S. company further up the supply chain ([112 Chong-Shang No. 684](https://judgment.judicial.gov.tw/FJUD/data.aspx?ty=JD&id=TPHV%2c112%2c%e9%87%8d%e4%b8%8a%2c684%2c20260429%2c1)). Only the technical documentation ran late, it was one part of the supplier's obligations, and some of it had been handed over. A claim of NT$440 million at 1% a day came down to 0.1% a day for 55 days: US$1,224,011.25, converted at 30.8 and awarded as NT$37,699,547. The supplier filed a third-instance appeal on June 2, 2026; its outcome does not appear in the published decisions.

The burden then sits with you. On May 15, 2026, the Taipei District Court dismissed a Japanese manufacturer's claim for NT$145,801,500 withheld from an offshore wind contract governed by Taiwan law ([114 Jian No. 141](https://judgment.judicial.gov.tw/FJUD/data.aspx?ty=JD&id=TPDV%2c114%2c%e5%bb%ba%2c141%2c20260515%2c1); [Article 20](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=B0000007&flno=20)(1) of the Act Governing the Choice of Law in Civil Matters Involving Foreign Elements). Citing earlier Supreme Court authority, it held that the debtor must assert and prove that a penalty is excessive, and it rejected the argument that the utility, having lost nothing, should collect nothing, finding that one turbine could not generate normally while a repair ran 225 days late. This is a first-instance judgment; an appeal had to be filed within 20 days of service, and as of October 22, 2026, public records do not show whether one was filed.

How you sue matters as well. A deducted penalty is challenged through a claim for the withheld price, or for return of the money as unjust enrichment ([Article 179](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=B0000001&flno=179)), and reduction is argued inside that claim. The Supreme Court said in passing in September 2026 that Article 252 is not itself a basis for a claim.

## If you are the buyer holding the clause

On the buyer's side, the wording comes first. In the High Court case, the clause made the supplier compensate all losses and added costs on top of the daily rate, and that language is what made the court classify the penalty as punitive. Without it, [Article 250](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=B0000001&flno=250)(2) deems a penalty to be the total amount of damages for non-performance. What a buyer keeps even then, for a penalty tied to late or improper performance, is the right to demand performance and the penalty together.

A contractual cap does not end the inquiry either. On September 23, 2026, the Supreme Court set aside a judgment that had upheld delay penalties at a 20% cap, NT$14 million, and remanded it ([115 Tai-Shang No. 1386](https://judgment.judicial.gov.tw/FJUD/data.aspx?ty=JD&id=TPSV%2c115%2c%e5%8f%b0%e4%b8%8a%2c1386%2c20260923%2c1)). A court, it said, must first classify the penalty: liquidated damages are measured mainly against the creditor's actual loss and lost profit, a punitive penalty against all the circumstances at the time of breach. The lower court had not classified a forfeited NT$4.2 million performance bond, nor examined what the buyer gained from the delivered batches and what it actually lost. The contract was a defense procurement of 100,000 meters of ballistic fabric in four batches; the supplier delivered two batches, 40,000 meters, late, and the buyer rescinded the other two and forfeited the bond. No decision after the remand appears in the published decisions as of October 22, 2026.

Distribution and supply relationships raise other contract issues; see [Terminating a Taiwan Distributor](/en/columns/terminating-taiwan-distributor-us-exporter).
