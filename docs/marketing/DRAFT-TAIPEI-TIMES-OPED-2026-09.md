**Title (working):** What foreign founders get wrong about setting up in Taiwan

Taiwan has become one of the easier places in Asia to start a company on paper, and one of the more confusing places to start one in practice. Over the past few years I have watched the same handful of misunderstandings slow down otherwise well-prepared foreign founders — not because the rules are hidden, but because the rules that matter are not the ones people research first.

**The minimum capital question is the wrong question.** Almost every founder begins by asking how much capital Taiwan requires. The honest answer — there is no statutory minimum, and a company can be formed with NT$1 — is technically true and practically useless. The number that actually shapes a plan is the one tied to immigration: a foreign representative who needs a work permit and a residence card must, as a sole shareholder, invest at least NT$500,000. Founders who plan around "no minimum" and later discover they cannot live in Taiwan to run the business have not been misled by anyone; they have simply answered a question nobody needed to ask.

**The timeline is sequential, not parallel.** Company formation in Taiwan typically takes around three months, followed by roughly another month for the work permit and residence card. What surprises people is the order. Foreign investment must first be approved by the Investment Commission, which checks that the capital is genuinely for the stated investment. Only then do the name reservation, notarized power of attorney, capital remittance, audit, and incorporation registration follow, and tax registration comes after that. Founders who sign a long office lease on day one, expecting to be trading in a month, end up paying rent on an empty room. The practical advice is unglamorous: set the lease start date as late as the process allows.

**Subsidiary or branch is a tax decision, not a branding decision.** Many companies choose a subsidiary because it "sounds more established." Both a subsidiary and a branch pay the same 5 percent business tax and 20 percent corporate income tax. The difference appears when profits move. A subsidiary distributing dividends to a foreign shareholder faces a 21 percent withholding tax, and retained profits attract a 5 percent undistributed-earnings tax; a branch remits profits to its head office without either. On the other hand, only a subsidiary can bring in local partners as shareholders or pursue a listing in Taiwan. The right structure depends on whether the plan is to reinvest locally, to repatriate, or to grow with Taiwanese co-investors — and that conversation should happen before the name is reserved, not after.

**The bank is the real gatekeeper.** Anti-money-laundering rules have made Taiwanese banks noticeably stricter about opening corporate accounts. Most will review the lease and physically visit the registered address. Founders who treat the account as a formality — something to sort out after incorporation — often find it becomes the step that stalls everything. The preparatory account, the capital remittance, and the conversion into a formal account are part of the formation process, not an afterthought, and the paperwork behind each should be assembled with the same care as the investment application.

**Remitting capital is a two-country exercise.** Capital sent from abroad must arrive within a year of Investment Commission approval, and the sending side has its own rules. A Korean investor, for example, must make the transfer in person and file an overseas direct investment report at home before the funds move. Every home jurisdiction has some version of this. Founders who plan only for the Taiwan side of the wire discover the delay on the other end.

None of this is secret. All of it is published, in Chinese, across several agencies. What foreign founders lack is not access to the rules but a sense of which rules bind first. If there is one habit I would recommend, it is to map the sequence — immigration, investment approval, formation, tax, banking — before choosing a structure, a lease, or a launch date. Taiwan rewards founders who arrive with the order of operations already understood.
