# Entering Taiwan’s Cosmetics Market: Importer Selection, Product Registration, PIF Preparation and Retention, and Advertising Rules

![Cosmetics documentation and regulatory review required to enter Taiwan’s market](../images/011-taiwan-cosmetics-market-entry-company-setup-pif-registration-legal-sales-guide/featured-01.jpg)

Selling a cosmetic in Taiwan raises four questions at the start. Who imports the product? When is it registered? Who keeps the Product Information File (PIF), and where? And how are labeling and advertising reviewed? A foreign brand can sell either through a local importer or by operating directly in Taiwan. Forming a company does not finish the preparations for sale on its own.

What applies to a given product depends on its type and manufacturing site, the actual import structure, the way it is distributed and what the advertising says. Before fixing a supply schedule, the brand should recheck the latest laws and the competent authorities’ guidance for each product.

![](../images/011-taiwan-cosmetics-market-entry-company-setup-pif-registration-legal-sales-guide/img-01.jpg)

## 1. Going Through an Importer or Setting Up in Taiwan

Foreign brands do not have to form a Taiwan subsidiary or branch to sell here. A Taiwan importer or distributor can handle importation and sales. A brand that operates directly has a second choice to make, between a subsidiary and a branch of a foreign company. Either way, it should first settle its business model and identify the entity that will assume the legal responsibilities of the cosmetics manufacturer or importer.

### Selling Through a Taiwan Importer

Either the distributor also serves as the importer, or a separate importer takes part. Contract labels such as agent, master distributor or distributor do not by themselves decide where legal responsibility lies.

Dividing the actual work comes next. Who imports the products and completes product registration, and who prepares, updates and retains the PIF? The parties must also designate who reviews labeling, maintains distribution records, receives consumer complaints and safety information, and responds to inspections and information requests from the competent authorities. The statutory duties of the cosmetics manufacturer or importer should be reviewed together with the tasks that the brand and its sales partner split by contract.

The agreement should set the permitted use of intellectual property such as trademarks and images. It should say how the manufacturer’s records for product registration and the PIF will be provided, translated and supplemented. Beyond that, it may cover how updated records are managed and handed over when the agreement ends, who may review and revise advertising in advance, how safety information such as complaints and adverse events is passed on, cooperation in any necessary recall, and who bears the costs of testing, translation and retention. Records should not stay with one party alone. The scope and deadline for returning them or providing copies should be agreed in advance.

### Setting Up a Subsidiary or Branch

Taiwan subsidiaries and Taiwan branches of foreign companies are two different organizational forms. The subsidiary is a separate legal entity incorporated under Taiwan law. The branch is registered as part of the foreign company’s head office. They differ in formation and registration, legal personality, the head office’s liability, accounting and tax treatment, profit remittance, representative authority and internal controls. For that reason the form should not be chosen only by how much control over sales the brand wants.

If foreign-investment procedures are required, the brand should consult the current guidance of the Department of Investment Review, Ministry of Economic Affairs (MOEA). Several steps are involved: obtaining investment approval, remitting funds, registering a company or branch, opening a bank account, completing tax registration and obtaining importer status. Timing varies. It depends on the investor, the industry, the organizational form, the documents submitted and whether supplemental documents are requested. A launch date should not rest on an assumed fixed period. The parties should first confirm which procedures apply and what the latest filing requirements are.

Under either structure, cosmetics regulation puts the central responsibility on the cosmetics manufacturer or importer. An outside expert may take over the product documentation or the safety assessment. Outsourcing alone does not shift that responsibility. Any review of the market-entry structure starts by keeping two things apart: who does the work under the contract, and who is responsible under the law.

## 2. Product Registration and the PIF: Two Different Duties

Product registration runs through TFDA’s cosmetics product registration platform. The PIF is a different obligation: a file of product records that the cosmetics manufacturer or importer must prepare, update and retain. TFDA does not receive the PIF in advance.

### Registration: When It Is Due and How Long It Lasts

The cosmetics manufacturer or importer must complete product registration before supplying, selling, giving away or publicly displaying a product, or providing it to consumers for trial use. Paid sales are not the only trigger. The registration timeline must also account for promotional giveaways and consumer trials.

Registration is valid for three years. To continue supplying the product, an extension must be applied for within the three months before the registration expires. If registered information such as the product name, purpose, dosage form, ingredients or manufacturing site changes, the business should also determine what procedure that particular change requires.

Registration is a procedure for reporting the prescribed registration information through the platform. Finishing it does not mean that all the records the PIF requires are in place. Nor does it establish that the labeling or advertising is lawful. So registration, PIF management, and labeling and advertising review should run as separate compliance workstreams.

### What Goes Into a PIF and Which Products Are Covered

A PIF is a set of records meant to give an ongoing account of a product’s quality and safety. It compiles information on the product’s quality, safety, composition, claimed functions, manufacturing methods, test results and safety assessment. Supporting records such as basic information about the product and the manufacturer, and the label, must also be organized for each product. The Regulations for Cosmetic Product Information File Management sort the required records into 16 categories. The records, signature requirements and qualification requirements for each category must be checked according to the product type.

PIF requirements have been implemented in phases by product category. From July 1, 2026, the remaining cosmetics are covered as well, so the requirements apply in principle to all cosmetics. The exception is narrow. It covers only solid handmade soap manufactured at a site exempt from factory registration. A product is not exempt merely because it is handmade or called soap. Both its solid form and the site’s exemption from factory registration must be confirmed.

Outside parties with the necessary qualifications and capabilities may assist with PIF work, including the safety assessment. Even then, the legal responsibility of the cosmetics manufacturer or importer remains in place, whoever helps prepare or retain the records. The original manufacturer, the testing laboratory, the safety assessor and the Taiwan business should set up a system for sharing changed information and current signed records.

### Updating the PIF and Keeping It on File

If an ingredient or formula, a manufacturing method or site, the labeling including the label, a claimed function or safety information changes, the affected PIF records must be reviewed and updated. Consumer complaints, adverse events and new test results may also affect the existing assessment. A change-control process is therefore required even after the PIF is first prepared.

Article 7 of the Regulations for Cosmetic Product Information File Management sets the retention period: at least five years, starting the day after the product was last supplied to the market. Article 8 of the same Regulations sets the place. The PIF must be kept at the address of the cosmetics manufacturer or importer specified in Article 7(1)(7) of the Cosmetic Hygiene and Safety Act. One rule answers how long, the other where, and practice should keep them apart.

Even if the original manufacturer holds the originals, or a secure electronic or cloud repository is used, the cosmetics manufacturer or importer must be able to access the complete records. That means settling access rights, backups, version control, file formats and responsible personnel in advance. The records can then be retrieved and produced promptly at the competent authority’s request. The agreement with a sales partner or service provider should also say what happens when it ends: which records are handed over, how the handover works, and whether access rights survive, so that the records remain available for the statutory retention period.

### Inspections, Correction Orders and Fines

When the competent authority inspects a PIF, it must in principle notify the cosmetics manufacturer or importer at least seven days beforehand. An inspection may still go ahead without advance notice if a statutory exception under the applicable regulations applies. Notice or no notice, complete and current records should be kept ready to retrieve and produce.

Reporting false information in a product registration, or recording false information in a PIF, may bring an administrative fine of NT$10,000–NT$1,000,000. Incomplete PIF records are treated differently. The competent authority ordinarily orders correction within a specified period and may impose an administrative fine if the deficiency is not corrected within that period. A false entry and a documentation gap that can still be fixed should not be treated alike.

Recall or destruction does not automatically follow every deficiency in PIF documentation. The product’s safety, the nature of the violation, the status of corrective action and the statutory requirements for each measure must be considered separately. A measure taken because a safety issue has been identified differs from a request to supplement documentation. The response should follow the competent authority’s notice and the applicable legal provisions.

## 3. Labeling, Promotion, and Advertising Rules

An advertisement is assessed on its overall presentation, including names, text, images, symbols and sound, not on its wording alone. False or exaggerated claims and claims of medical efficacy are prohibited. The administrative fine is NT$40,000–NT$200,000 for false or exaggerated advertising and NT$600,000–NT$5,000,000 for claims of medical efficacy.

### Looking at the Whole Advertisement

No single word settles it. Whether labeling, promotion or advertising is false, exaggerated or a claim of medical efficacy turns on the product name, sentences, images, symbols, sound, surrounding context and the overall impression on consumers, considered together. A disclaimer in small print does not necessarily cure a dominant advertising message, so both the individual claims and the final advertisement as produced should be reviewed.

Saying that a cosmetic treats acne, has an anti-inflammatory effect or sterilizes may amount to a claim of medical efficacy, so these claims require particular care. The review should also look at claims that associate the product with a disease, before-and-after images, and presentations evoking a medical professional. Contexts that connect an ingredient description to a therapeutic effect of the product belong in the review too. The two fine ranges differ by type of violation. Before publication, the advertisement’s overall presentation should be compared against the supporting records.

### Posts by Influencers, Reviewers and Sales Partners

Does an influencer’s post count as the brand’s advertising? It may. That depends on its content and commercial context, and the same goes for posts by reviewers and sales partners. Payment, free products, sales links, the brand’s posting instructions and repeated collaborations are factors that may be considered. Not every personal post automatically becomes brand advertising, so the poster’s relationship with the brand, the specific content and the degree of the brand’s involvement should be examined. A post that is advertising in substance should be reviewed under the same standards as any other advertisement.

Collaboration agreements and operating guidelines may define the permitted claims and supporting records, pre-publication review, and procedures for correcting or deleting noncompliant claims. The review should reach claims added in comments and oral explanations in livestreams or short videos. It should also catch inconsistencies among sales pages and labels. The brand should retain reviewed drafts, approval history, correction requests and the final published materials so that the facts can be verified later.

### The Order of Work Before Sale

Taking the steps in this order can reduce the risk of confusing the separate regulatory systems.

1. Decide whether to establish a Taiwan subsidiary or branch or entrust importation and sales to a local importer.
2. Identify who bears the legal responsibilities of the cosmetics manufacturer or importer, and who is responsible for each contractually allocated task.
3. Complete product registration before supplying, selling, giving away or publicly displaying the product, or providing it to consumers for trial use.
4. Prepare a PIF for each product, update it for changes, and retain it for the statutory period at the required location.
5. Review labels, sales pages, advertising and collaboration posts on their overall presentation.
6. Keep procedures ready for inspections and corrective requests, complaints and safety information, and any necessary follow-up measures.
