# Entering the Taiwan Market: Key Differences Between a Subsidiary and a Branch

![Taiwan subsidiary and foreign-company branch comparison](../images/004-taiwan-company-subsidiary-vs-branch/featured-01.jpg)

A foreign business establishing an operating presence in Taiwan commonly compares a locally incorporated Taiwan subsidiary with a Taiwan branch of the foreign company. Both can conduct business, hire personnel, maintain premises, and enter contracts when their registrations and any industry approvals are in place. They differ, however, in legal identity, ownership, liability, financing, profit remittances, and exit procedure. The useful question is therefore not which form is universally simpler, but which form matches the proposed business over its full life cycle.

The comparison should begin with one set of facts. Identify the investors, decision-makers, customers, employees, assets, intellectual property, regulated activities, expected cash flows, and long-term financing plan. Then assign each role, contract, and payment to the proposed entity. This discipline exposes gaps that may be hidden by a short incorporation checklist or a comparison of headline tax rates.

![](../images/004-taiwan-company-subsidiary-vs-branch/img-01.jpg)

## 1. Legal Personality and Ownership

A Taiwan branch is part of the foreign company and has no shareholders of its own. If the business will have third-party co-investors, it may consider a Taiwan subsidiary with an appropriate shareholder structure or another lawful arrangement. Liability, voting rights, financing, licensing, and tax treatment must be evaluated based on the ownership arrangement and business plan.

Article 1 of the Company Act defines a company organized, registered, and incorporated under that Act as a corporate juristic person for profit. A Taiwan subsidiary formed under the Act therefore has a legal identity separate from its foreign parent, and each has its own rights and obligations. The subsidiary can own property, employ staff, contract with customers and suppliers, and bring or defend proceedings in its own name. Control by a single shareholder does not, without more, merge their legal identities.

The ordinary shareholder rule depends on the company form. Under Article 99(1), a shareholder of a limited company is generally liable to the company to the extent of that shareholder’s contribution. A shareholder of a company limited by shares is generally liable to the company only for full payment of the shares for which the shareholder has subscribed. These rules support a real separation of entity and shareholder risk, but they must not be treated as a promise that every exposure stops at the investment amount.

Article 99(2) addresses serious abuse: if a shareholder abuses the company’s status as a juristic person so that the company has difficulty satisfying a specific debt, and the circumstances are serious, the shareholder may bear the debt to the extent necessary. Limited liability also does not eliminate liability arising from a guarantee, a contract signed in another capacity, a tort, director or officer duties, regulatory law, or another applicable cause of action. The documents and actual conduct must be reviewed together.

A branch follows a different legal model. Under Article 371, a foreign company that has not registered a branch may not conduct business in Taiwan in the foreign company’s name. Article 372 requires the foreign company to appropriate funds exclusively for the branch’s business and to designate a representative who serves as the responsible person in Taiwan. Those appropriated branch operating funds remain funds of the foreign company; they are not share capital or equity in a separate entity.

Registering a branch allows the foreign company to operate through it in Taiwan, but does not give the branch a separate legal identity or create a limited-liability silo. A contract made through the branch within the scope of its authorized Taiwan operations is a contract of the foreign company, and every branch obligation is an obligation of the foreign company. Internal limits on authority remain important for governance, but they do not give the branch rights and obligations of its own.

For a Taiwan subsidiary, third-party participation can be addressed through capital contributions, shareholder rights, governance bodies, transfer restrictions, reserved matters, and, where lawful and appropriate, incentive arrangements. The final structure must account for the selected company form, foreign-investment rules, the articles of incorporation, shareholder agreements, licensing requirements, and the commercial facts. For a branch, collaboration with another business must instead rest on a lawful contract or another structure; it cannot be achieved by issuing an ownership interest in the branch.

## 2. Tax and Profit Remittances

A subsidiary and a branch that conduct taxable business in Taiwan generally must address 5% business tax and 20% profit-seeking enterprise income tax, although actual liability depends on taxable income, transaction type, and allowable deductions. Under Taiwan domestic law, a Taiwan subsidiary’s dividend to a foreign parent is subject to 21% withholding; when the Taiwan–Korea Income Tax Agreement and its procedures apply, the source-territory rate is capped at 10%. A Taiwan branch’s remittance of after-tax branch profits to its foreign head office is not a dividend and generally does not trigger separate dividend withholding. A profit-seeking enterprise whose head office is outside Taiwan is exempt from filing the undistributed-earnings return and from the related 5% additional tax.

Taiwan’s general business-tax rate is 5% for taxable sales under the general system. That figure is a starting point, not an estimate of the final liability. Zero-rating, exemptions, special rates, the availability of input-tax credits, and the character and place of a supply can alter the treatment. The invoice flow and actual supply chain should be mapped for the Taiwan operation rather than inferred from its organizational label.

The general profit-seeking enterprise income-tax rate is 20% when taxable income exceeds the statutory threshold. It applies to taxable income, not gross revenue. Revenue recognition, deductible expenses, depreciation, loss utilization, tax incentives, and adjustments may change the taxable base. A Taiwan subsidiary reports its own income and expenses, while a branch must determine the income and expenses attributable to the foreign company’s Taiwan business.

Outbound profits require a second layer of analysis. Under Taiwan domestic law, a dividend paid by a Taiwan subsidiary to a foreign parent is subject to 21% withholding. If the recipient qualifies under the Taiwan–Korea Income Tax Agreement and all applicable procedures are satisfied, the source-territory ceiling for a qualifying dividend is 10%. Before using that ceiling, the parties should confirm residence evidence, beneficial-owner status, payment character, supporting documents, and the correct filing or refund procedure.

After the Taiwan tax on branch profits has been addressed, a remittance of those after-tax branch profits to the foreign head office is an internal movement within the foreign company rather than a dividend. It therefore generally does not attract a separate dividend withholding tax. This conclusion does not convert every outbound transfer from a branch into branch-profit remittance. Interest, royalties, service fees, asset consideration, and third-party payments are different payment types and require their own source, deduction, withholding, treaty, and documentation analysis.

The undistributed-earnings rules also distinguish the structures. A Taiwan subsidiary retaining earnings may need to account for the 5% additional tax under the applicable statutory rules. A profit-seeking enterprise whose head office is outside Taiwan is exempt from filing the undistributed-earnings return and from that related 5% additional tax. The exemption does not remove the branch’s ordinary income-tax obligations or its duty to support profit attribution and remittances.

Headline rates still do not establish the group’s total cost. Transfer pricing may apply to related-party dealings, while branch accounts require defensible allocation of head-office and common costs. Foreign tax credits, the foreign jurisdiction’s tax and accounting treatment, exchange-control reporting, and group reporting should be reviewed separately. A branch is not inherently tax-favored, and a Taiwan subsidiary does not inherently create a higher or lower total burden.

Practical modeling should show at least three stages: taxable operating results in Taiwan, after-tax cash retained for local use, and cash returned abroad. The model should classify each cross-border charge and incorporate the expected timing of losses, investment, dividends, and branch-profit remittances. It should also record the assumptions for treaty eligibility and foreign-jurisdiction relief so that a change in one assumption can be tested without rebuilding the entire comparison.

## 3. Debts and Legal Liability

The clearest structural difference concerns whose debt is created. Because a branch has no independent legal personality, lease, employment, sales, borrowing, and service obligations properly undertaken through it are obligations of the foreign company. The amount appropriated for branch operations does not cap that exposure. Creditors’ rights are determined by the governing law, contract, security, and applicable causes of action, not by an internal budget assigned to the branch.

A Taiwan subsidiary ordinarily incurs its own contractual debts. Shareholders of a limited company are generally liable to the company only to the extent of their contributions, while shareholders of a company limited by shares are generally liable to the company only for full payment of the shares for which they have subscribed, subject in each case to applicable exceptions. This separation can be significant when an operation has long-term leases, employees, product obligations, or regulated risks. It is nevertheless only one part of the liability analysis.

Guarantees and comfort letters can expose a foreign parent according to their wording and governing law. A director, officer, employee, shareholder, or group company may incur separate liability through a contract, breach of duty, or participation in a joint tort. Serious abuse of juristic personality, labor and tax violations, licensing failures, and group contracts may also create exposure outside the ordinary shareholder rule. A Taiwan subsidiary is not a device that isolates every risk.

The operating design should reinforce the chosen legal structure. Contracts should identify the correct party; signatures should reflect actual authority; bank accounts and invoices should belong to the entity earning or paying the relevant amount; and intercompany services, licenses, loans, and guarantees should be documented separately. Informal mixing of personnel, cash, assets, and contractual roles can create disputes even when the registrations themselves are correct.

Risk allocation should also be tested against mandatory law. Contractual limitations may not control regulatory penalties, employee rights, tax obligations, data duties, product rules, or liability for personal misconduct. Insurance may address selected losses, but coverage, exclusions, limits, and insured parties require review. The subsidiary-versus-branch decision should therefore sit within a broader program of contracting, governance, compliance, and insurance.

## 4. Financing and Listing in Taiwan

A branch is not an independent issuer and cannot itself be listed in Taiwan. A Taiwan subsidiary must still satisfy the Company Act and the applicable exchange’s requirements to pursue a listing. Tax incentives do not apply solely because of the chosen organizational form. Eligibility for an investment credit under Article 10-1 of the Statute for Industrial Innovation depends on the taxpayer, qualifying investment, filing deadline, credit method, anti-duplication rules, and applicable tax limits.

A branch cannot issue branch equity to third-party investors because it has no equity interests to issue. It may receive appropriately characterized funds from the foreign head office or obtain lawful financing, subject to any applicable investment-approval, banking, foreign-exchange, tax, and documentation requirements. The parties must distinguish appropriated branch funds, loans, service payments, and other receipts in both records and actual use.

A Taiwan subsidiary can be used to structure local or third-party participation. Its capital, shareholder, governance, incentive, transfer, and financing arrangements remain subject to the Company Act, investment rules, securities law where relevant, contractual restrictions, and the specific facts. New investors will commonly examine decision rights, dilution, information rights, transfer restrictions, exit terms, intellectual-property ownership, and related-party transactions before committing funds.

A branch is not an independent issuer and cannot itself be listed in Taiwan. A Taiwan subsidiary does not qualify for listing merely because it exists or is organized as a company limited by shares. The issuer must satisfy the requirements of the applicable market, including requirements concerning operating history, capital, profitability, share distribution, corporate governance, internal controls, audit, and disclosure.

The detailed standards may differ by market, board, and industry, and they may change over the preparation period. Operational substance also matters: the proposed issuer’s assets, contracts, employees, functions, related-party transactions, financial record, and disclosure readiness must support the listing plan. The possible listing of a foreign company and the possible listing of a Taiwan subsidiary are separate projects and should not be confused with the status of a Taiwan branch.

Long-term plans should be considered before selecting the initial form. A business expecting outside equity, employee incentives, a share sale, or a Taiwan listing may need an ownership platform that a branch cannot provide. A business funded entirely by its foreign head office may still prefer a subsidiary for other reasons, while another may prefer direct branch operation. Neither conclusion follows from financing needs alone.

## 5. Article 10-1 Investment Tax Credits

Article 10-1 of the Statute for Industrial Innovation applies from January 1, 2025, through December 31, 2029. Subject to its statutory conditions and approval process, a company or limited partnership with qualifying expenditures totaling NT$1 million or more but NT$2 billion or less in the same taxable year may elect a credit for qualifying new, own-use smart machinery; 5G systems; cybersecurity products or services; AI products or services; and energy-saving or carbon-reduction hardware, software, technology, or technical services. The taxpayer may elect up to 5% of the expenditure against tax payable for the current year or up to 3% against tax payable in each of three years beginning with the current year. The annual Article 10-1 credit may not exceed 30% of the current year’s profit-seeking enterprise income tax; the combined-credit limit and restrictions on duplicate benefits require separate review.

Eligibility depends on the taxpayer, compliance history, the nature and amount of qualifying expenditure, the new and own-use requirements, timely filing and approval, supporting records, and any later change in use. Contracts, invoices, payment evidence, technical specifications, asset records, implementation plans, and proof of actual use may all be relevant. Labeling a project as involving artificial intelligence, cybersecurity, or energy efficiency does not itself establish statutory eligibility.

Article 10 research and development credits are distinct from Article 10-1 investment credits. The provisions address different activities, expenditure tests, procedures, percentages, and limits. A taxpayer should identify the provision being relied on before contracting and maintain records against that provision rather than attempting to reclassify a completed project later.

Organizational form alone does not answer whether the credit is available. The law’s definition of the applicant, the identity that incurs the expenditure and uses the investment, and every substantive and procedural condition must be checked. It would be equally unsound to assume that a Taiwan subsidiary qualifies automatically or that a Taiwan branch is automatically excluded.

## 6. The Taiwan–Korea Income Tax Agreement and Permanent Establishments

The Taiwan–Korea Income Tax Agreement was signed on November 17, 2021, entered into force on December 27, 2023, and applies from January 1, 2024. Its source-territory ceilings for qualifying dividends, interest, and royalties are each 10%, subject to the Agreement’s residence, beneficial-owner, documentation, and procedural requirements. Business profits are generally exempt in the other territory if the enterprise has no permanent establishment there. A permanent establishment may arise from a fixed place such as a place of management, branch, or office; a construction project lasting more than six months; services performed for more than 183 aggregate days in any 12-month period; or a dependent agent that habitually exercises authority to conclude contracts. A Taiwan branch ordinarily is a fixed-place permanent establishment, so its Taiwan business profits are not automatically exempt.

Permanent establishment (PE) is a treaty concept, and its tests address different factual connections. A services project lasting fewer than 184 aggregate days does not rule out a fixed-place PE, just as a short construction project does not decide whether a dependent-agent PE exists. Each test requires its own facts concerning place, duration, personnel, and authority.

The existence of a Taiwan subsidiary does not by itself establish or prevent a foreign parent’s PE in Taiwan. Separate incorporation and the treaty nexus are different questions. Employee activity, access to premises, contract negotiation and authority, the functions performed, assets used, risks assumed, and the attribution of profit may require separate analysis. The same evidence should be reviewed for both PE status and transfer-pricing consequences.

A treaty claim must also be implemented through the applicable domestic procedures. The parties should prepare the residence certificates, beneficial-owner evidence, transaction documents, and tax filings required for the particular income and follow any applicable advance-approval, withholding, or refund procedure. Treaty ceilings limit qualifying source-territory taxation; they do not replace the need to classify the payment or calculate the income attributable to a Taiwan PE.

## 7. Choosing a Structure and Planning an Exit

The comparison is most reliable when both forms are tested against the same operating plan. A short list of filing differences will not show who controls the business, where risk accumulates, or how cash and assets will move. Before deciding, prepare an organization chart, contract list, funds-flow diagram, and financial forecast, and make the assumptions in each document consistent.

- **Investors and governance:** Identify the initial and future investors, voting arrangements, board or management authority, reserved decisions, deadlock procedures, and reporting lines. A Taiwan subsidiary may accommodate local participation; a branch remains under the foreign company’s governance.
- **Liability allocation:** Allocate contractual obligations and regulatory duties, then identify any guarantees from the foreign parent, the foreign head office’s responsibility for branch obligations, applicable insurance and security, and authority controls. Model both ordinary performance and a significant claim.
- **Operations and assets:** Assign customer and supplier contracts, employees, intellectual property, premises, data, assets, and licenses to the intended legal party. Confirm whether regulated approvals or industry rules impose additional ownership, personnel, capital, or location conditions.
- **Revenue and returns:** Map revenue, costs, cross-border charges, retained earnings, dividends, and branch-profit remittances. Separate each payment type and model Taiwan tax, treaty assumptions, cash timing, and foreign-jurisdiction consequences.
- **Investment and banking:** Plan for investment approval, bank-account opening, inward remittance, any capital increase, the appropriation of branch operating funds, and later outbound remittance. The remitter, recipient, stated purpose, accounting entry, and supporting approval should describe the same transaction.
- **Accounting and reporting:** Determine who will maintain books, obtain any required audit, prepare transfer-pricing support, and complete group reporting. Include foreign tax credits and the foreign jurisdiction’s legal, accounting, tax, and exchange-control consequences.
- **Future transactions:** Test future financing, local participation, employee incentives, restructuring, a share or business sale, and listing. Consider whether contracts and licenses can be transferred, whether investor consent or regulatory approval would be required, and what employee-related steps and tax consequences would follow.
- **Suspension and exit:** Plan for suspension, branch-registration cancellation, subsidiary dissolution and liquidation, creditor claims, employee matters, tax filings, asset disposal, bank closure, and record retention. Assign responsibility and sequence rather than assuming registration cancellation completes the project.

Changing form later is usually more than an amendment of a name. Moving a branch business to a new Taiwan subsidiary, or transferring a subsidiary’s business to another group entity, may involve contract assignments, employee arrangements, intellectual-property permissions, asset transfers, tax, investment procedures, licenses, and customer communications. A transition plan should identify consents and continuity risks before either entity begins operating.

Under Article 378 of the Company Act, a foreign company that no longer intends to conduct business in Taiwan must apply to cancel its branch registration, and cancellation does not discharge liabilities or debts incurred before cancellation. Article 379 separately authorizes official cancellation in specified circumstances and provides that cancellation does not affect creditors’ rights or the foreign company’s obligations. If all of the foreign company’s Taiwan branches have been revoked or canceled, Article 380 requires liquidation of the claims and debts arising from its Taiwan business, and the foreign company remains liable for outstanding debts.

A Taiwan subsidiary ends its business through the applicable dissolution and liquidation procedures in its own legal identity. Corporate approvals, appointment of a liquidator, creditor and employee matters, realization of assets, payment of debts, tax filings, record preservation, and distribution of any lawful remainder belong to that distinct process. Branch-registration cancellation and subsidiary liquidation should not be collapsed into one generic closure checklist.

The preferred structure may change when the assumptions change. A decision memorandum should therefore record the proposed activities, funding, contractual allocation, tax assumptions, licenses, governance, and exit scenario. Periodic review can then determine whether actual operations still match the selected form and whether a planned financing, new regulated activity, or group restructuring calls for a fresh analysis.
