Which company acquired the film rights: the payer, the Taiwan distributor or the affiliate operating the streaming platform? In [Taiwan High Court judgment 113年度重上更三字第14號](https://judgment.judicial.gov.tw/FJUD/printData.aspx?id=TPHV%2C113%2C%E9%87%8D%E4%B8%8A%E6%9B%B4%E4%B8%89%2C14%2C20250325%2C1), later platform availability did not prove that the offshore company had obtained the disputed earlier license.

Decided on March 25, 2025 after a third remand, the case began as a former general manager's claim for repayment of a loan. The Taiwan company raised assigned claims for film-licensing losses as a setoff against that debt. The court therefore had to identify which rights the offshore company had received and what losses remained.

## The signed deal and an earlier admission

A signed US$1.5 million arrangement prevailed. The competing US$800,000 document lacked the relevant party's signature. The court also held the Taiwan company to its earlier admission that rights to eight films had been obtained. A US declaratory judgment addressed an agency relationship. It did not prove that admission wrong. These findings appear in the [High Court's contract and evidence analysis](https://judgment.judicial.gov.tw/FJUD/printData.aspx?id=TPHV%2C113%2C%E9%87%8D%E4%B8%8A%E6%9B%B4%E4%B8%89%2C14%2C20250325%2C1).

Admissions have procedural consequences. Under [Code of Civil Procedure Article 279](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=B0010001&flno=279), a fact admitted in the specified court proceedings generally needs no further proof. Unless otherwise provided, withdrawal requires proof that the admission was contrary to the facts or the opposing party's consent. A document relevant to the commercial relationship still has to establish the particular contradiction being asserted.

Here, the Taiwan court assessed the US decision as evidence. It was not deciding an application to recognize or enforce that judgment. For a company litigating in both jurisdictions, the practical task is to compare the precise factual admission with what the foreign court actually decided, including the parties and rights its decision covered.

## A platform screenshot left the corporate question unanswered

For two other films, the court found that the offshore company had not obtained the rights. Later availability through a different affiliate's platform did not prove that the offshore company had acquired the rights earlier. After the allowed licensing-loss setoffs, the Taiwan company still owed US$21,200 in loan principal, plus the interest specified in the [judgment](https://judgment.judicial.gov.tw/FJUD/printData.aspx?id=TPHV%2C113%2C%E9%87%8D%E4%B8%8A%E6%9B%B4%E4%B8%89%2C14%2C20250325%2C1).

That leaves a concrete documentation problem for a US rights seller or distributor. A screenshot can show availability. A bank record can show payment. Neither, without the relevant agreement and supporting record, completes the account of which company received which rights for which period.

Useful rights files follow the transaction from the signed grant to its title schedule, the named licensee and any authorized sublicensing. Territory, media and term belong beside the title, rather than in a separate email that a later reviewer may never see. Match the payments to that grant. Delivery records belong with it. If affiliates take different roles, the file should explain the connection between them.

## Estimating an amount still required proof of damage

To assess part of the loss, the High Court used [Code of Civil Procedure Article 222(2)](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=B0010001&flno=222). That provision directs the court to assess the amount from all the circumstances when a party has proved damage but cannot prove its amount, or faces serious difficulty doing so.

Proof of loss came first. A licensing team should preserve the package price and any allocation among titles, together with later charges and credits. Those records allow counsel to distinguish a payment for rights actually delivered from a disputed overpayment, and help prevent the same charge from being counted twice. The provision does not turn every missing invoice detail into an assumed loss.

## Before the next rights meeting

The Taiwan Creative Content Agency announced on [September 22, 2026](https://en.taicca.tw/news/detail/222) that TCCF MARKET would focus on international film, television and IP rights transactions. The event is scheduled for November 6–9. For a US business considering Taiwan licensing discussions, it is a timely reason to settle the contracting entity and grant schedule before a commercial summary becomes the only record everyone retains.

Initial ownership of commissioned and employee work is addressed in the [copyright ownership article](/en/columns/taiwan-copyright-employee-freelancer-work-ownership). This dispute concerns a later step: proving what a named company acquired under a particular transaction. A [Taiwan licensing-dispute inquiry](/en/contact) can identify the companies, the disputed titles and any response deadline, with confidential agreements supplied through an agreed channel.

The file should let a reader follow one title from the grant to the licensee and the relevant dates without substituting the group's brand name for a legal entity. In the High Court case, that missing connection remained material even when the film could be watched online.
