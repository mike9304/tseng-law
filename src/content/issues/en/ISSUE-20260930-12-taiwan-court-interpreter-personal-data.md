# No Interpreter in a Taiwan Criminal Case: How to Ask, and When a Post Becomes a Crime

A summons arrives in Chinese. You can order food and talk to your neighbors, and you still cannot follow what the judge is asking. A friend says to paste the paper into a chatbot. Someone else says to put the other side's name and the contract online so they will back down. Those are two different problems, and both of them show up in the same public essay.

On September 29, 2026, JURIST published ["When Court Interpretation Failed in Taiwan, I Turned to a Chatbot,"](https://www.jurist.org/commentary/2026/09/when-court-interpretation-failed-in-taiwan-i-turned-to-a-chatbot/) by Ross Cline. He writes that he was the defendant. The disclosure says he is the subject of the proceedings and has a personal interest in how they are discussed. He writes that a court found he had posted documents containing his landlord's personal data, including the lease, that he was sentenced to six months, and that the conviction became final at the Supreme Court on October 16, 2024. He gives these case numbers: Taichung District Court 111 Su 607, Taiwan High Court Taichung Branch 112 Shang Su 3114, and Supreme Court 113 Tai Shang 4256. He also writes that no document was translated for him and that some hearings went ahead with no interpreter. We have not read those judgments or the court file. This article does not retry his case. The rules below are the ones on the statute database on September 30, 2026.

## What does the right to an interpreter actually say?

[Article 99 of the Code of Criminal Procedure](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=C0010001&flno=99) (刑事訴訟法) says that if the defendant has a hearing or speech disability, or does not understand the language (語言不通), an interpreter shall interpret. When necessary, the questions and the answers may be in writing. The next paragraph applies the same rule to other people who are questioned or interviewed, unless another law provides otherwise. The word is 應, shall. It is not a courtesy the court may skip because a friend is in the room or because you have lived in Taiwan for years.

The statute does not let you choose the interpreter. The court, the prosecutor, or the officer arranges the 通譯. Say which language you actually speak. Everyday Mandarin is not the test. If you cannot follow the questions, say that before you answer.

The first hours at a police station are covered in our column on [police questioning and a foreigner's rights](/en/columns/taiwan-police-questioning-foreigner-rights). One timing rule belongs here as well. [Article 93-1](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=C0010001&flno=93-1) says that if a defendant or suspect needs an interpreter and is not questioned while waiting for that interpreter to arrive, up to six hours of the wait is not counted in the 24-hour period after an arrest. During that excluded time, the person is not to be questioned. Waiting is not an invitation to "just explain it briefly" without the interpreter.

## How do you ask, and what do you do with the transcript?

Say it in short words, and ask for it to be written down: "I do not understand Mandarin. I need an interpreter under Article 99." Repeat it if a hearing starts without one. If you are given a pen, write the same sentence.

[Article 41](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=C0010001&flno=41) requires a record, made at the time, of the questions and the answers. It must be read aloud or given to the person to read, and they must be asked whether it is correct. A lawyer who is present may help a defendant read it and may say whether it is wrong. A request to add, delete, or change something goes into the record. You are asked to sign, seal, or fingerprint at the end. If you refuse, the refusal and the reason must be noted. Do not sign a page you cannot understand. Ask the interpreter to read it first.

He writes that a summons told the defendant to bring his own interpreter. We have not seen that paper. Even if a form says that, Article 99 still says the authority shall provide an interpreter when the defendant does not understand the language. Ask the court or the prosecutor. Do not treat a printed line as a waiver.

A chatbot is not that interpreter. He writes that he put a letter from a Taiwanese oversight body through two systems and that they disagreed on the central point. Use a translation tool to prepare questions for your lawyer if you want. Do not file a paper, sign a record, or publish a claim about what a court decided based only on a machine translation. Ask the interpreter or your lawyer to read the operative part with you.

## What if you still do not understand the proceeding?

Stop. Ask again for an interpreter, and ask for a lawyer before you answer questions about the facts. [Article 27](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=C0010001&flno=27) lets you appoint counsel at any time, and it lets a spouse, a parent, and certain other relatives appoint counsel for you even if you have not asked. If you are writing from abroad, our column on [hiring a Taiwan lawyer from abroad](/en/columns/hire-taiwan-lawyer-from-abroad) sets out the steps. Tell the lawyer the language you need, the next date, and which papers you have not been able to read.

If you are a witness rather than a defendant, paragraph 2 of Article 99 still applies to you. Say that you do not understand before you take the oath or answer. The police-questioning column explains the separate warnings a suspect must receive, including the right to stay silent.

## When can posting someone's data during a dispute be a crime?

The author ties his case to the Personal Data Protection Act (個人資料保護法). On November 11, 2025, the President promulgated an amendment to Article 41. The [Preparatory Office said that day](https://www.pdpc.gov.tw/News_Content/20/1010/) that the Executive Yuan would set the effective date under Article 56. Its [compilation](https://www.pdpc.gov.tw/News_Html/100/) still prints the earlier Article 41 as current and marks the new wording as not yet in force. Both versions max out at five years and an optional fine of NT$1 million. The change is who may restrict a cross-border transfer.

The in-force text punishes a person who, intending an unlawful benefit or to harm another's interests, violates Article 6 paragraph 1, Article 15, Article 16, Article 19, or Article 20 paragraph 1, or a cross-border restriction order of the central industry competent authority under Article 21, in a way sufficient to cause harm. The [database page](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=I0050021&flno=41) currently displays the not-yet-effective sentence, which drops those words about the central industry authority. Do not treat that display as the rule in force. For a post in a dispute, the duties that matter are the ones on sensitive data and on collection and use.

[Article 2](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=I0050021&flno=2) defines personal data to include a natural person's name, date of birth, national ID number, passport number, contact details, financial situation, and other data that can identify the person. A natural person is a non-government agency under the same article. A lease often contains a name, an ID number, and an address.

[Article 19](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=I0050021&flno=19) says a non-government agency may collect or process personal data, other than the sensitive data in Article 6, only for a specific purpose and only on a listed ground. A contract or a similar relationship is one ground, and only if proper security measures are also in place. Other grounds include a clear law, data the person has made public, and consent. The statute lists more. [Article 20](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=I0050021&flno=20) says use must stay within that purpose unless an exception applies, such as consent or preventing a serious harm to someone else's rights. [Article 6](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=I0050021&flno=6) generally prohibits collecting medical records, medical data, genetic data, data about sexual life, health-check data, and criminal-record data, with listed exceptions.

[Article 51](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=I0050021&flno=51) puts the Act to one side when a natural person collects, processes, or uses personal data only for a purely personal or household activity. Publishing another person's identifying details in order to pressure them in a dispute is not something this article can call a household activity for you. Whether a post meets every element of Article 41, including the intent and the harm, is decided on the facts.

[Article 45](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=I0050021&flno=45) says offenses in the penalty chapter require a complaint, except Article 41 and except Article 42 when it is committed against a government agency. A prosecution under Article 41 does not have to wait for the person whose data was posted to file a complaint.

Before you post, leave out the other person's ID number, address, account numbers, medical notes, and criminal-record information. A dispute over rent or a deposit can go in a letter to the other side or to a lawyer. Our police-questioning column is the place to read what happens if that post leads to a call from the police.
