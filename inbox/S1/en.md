# Paid for Something You Sold in Taiwan, Then the Account Froze: The Three-Party Scam

The money that reaches your account after a secondhand sale in Taiwan may not come from the person who bought your item. In Taiwan this is often called a three-party or triangular scam (三方詐騙, 三角詐騙). A fraudster sits in the middle, and somebody else's money pays for your goods.

You list a road bike in Kaohsiung a few weeks before moving home (an invented example). A buyer messages you, asks for your account number, and the transfer arrives within minutes, so you ship the bike to the address you are given. The person who actually sent the money had answered a fake ad posted by the same fraudster and was told to pay into your account. No item reaches that person. They go to the police, and the trail starts with the account the money went into. Yours.

## Why the account stops

[Article 9, paragraph 4, of the Fraud Crime Hazard Prevention Act](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=D0080226&flno=9) lets judicial police investigating a fraud tell a bank to watch-list a suspected account and suspend all of its transactions. Money cannot come in either. Incoming transfers to a watch-listed account (警示帳戶) are sent back to the remitting bank under [Article 5 of the Regulations Governing the Deposit Accounts and Suspicious or Unusual Transactions](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=G0380199&flno=5).

Your other deposit accounts are caught too. [Article 3](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=G0380199&flno=3) defines any other account opened by the holder of a watch-listed account as a derivative watch-listed account (衍生管制帳戶). On those, card, phone and online transfers and other electronic payment functions stop, and incoming transfers bounce. At the counter, the bank may ask for documents showing that a withdrawal or transfer is reasonable and may refuse it if you cannot explain.

## The balance may go back to the victim

From your side, you shipped the bike, so the money is the price. The rules start from the victim's side. For accounts watch-listed on or after April 1, 2025, leftover victim funds are handled under a separate set of anti-fraud regulations ([Article 11, paragraph 7](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=G0380199&flno=11)). Under [Article 52](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=G0380271&flno=52) of those regulations, once the judicial police send the bank a written notice to return the remaining funds to the victim, with a copy to the account holder, the bank may make the return. [Article 53](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=G0380271&flno=53) has the bank count back from the last deposit until the balance reaches zero.

So where does "but it was a sale" get heard? [Article 69](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=G0380271&flno=69) takes a case out of this return procedure and sends it through judicial proceedings when the bank considers it a suspected transaction dispute or a complex case. The bank makes that call.

## Who can lift the flag

Explaining yourself at the branch will not do it. Under Article 10, paragraph 1, of the deposit account regulations, the bank may lift the restrictions only on notification from the original reporting agency or when the watch-list period expires. The exception is an urgent notice: if the agency first reported by phone or fax and the written notice does not arrive within five business days, the bank must contact the agency and then lift the listing ([Article 3, item 3](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=G0380199&flno=3)). The ordinary watch-list period is two years from notification. If the agency needs longer, it must notify the bank again before expiry, and it can extend only once, by up to one year ([Article 9, paragraph 1](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=G0380199&flno=9)). An account holder who disputes the listing deals with the original reporting agency, and the bank assists where necessary (paragraph 2). The wider rules, including the older return procedure for accounts listed on or before March 31, 2025, are in [our column on warning accounts](/en/columns/taiwan-warning-account-foreigners).

That makes the name of the reporting agency the first thing to get from the bank. Your listing, the messages with the "buyer," the courier receipt with the delivery address, and the deposit record are what you can put in front of that agency. Victims sometimes also file a criminal complaint against the seller for aiding fraud, and the same records matter there.

## The victim's refund claim

A victim may also sue the seller for the money. [Article 179 of the Civil Code](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=B0000001&flno=179) requires a person who receives a benefit without legal grounds, to another's loss, to return it.

In a first-instance small-claims judgment of March 3, 2025, the Zhudong Summary Court of the Hsinchu District Court sided with the account holder, who said they ran an online purchasing-agent business ([114年度竹東小字第28號](https://judgment.judicial.gov.tw/FJUD/data.aspx?ty=JD&id=CPEV%2c114%2c%e7%ab%b9%e6%9d%b1%e5%b0%8f%2c28%2c20250303%2c1)). The victim had been deceived by a third party and paid NT$13,000 on that person's instructions. In this kind of directed payment (指示給付), the court said, no performance relationship exists between the victim and the recipient, so the claim lies against whoever gave the instructions. The victim's earlier criminal complaint against the account holder for aiding fraud had ended in a decision not to prosecute for insufficient evidence.

The New Taipei case came out differently at each level. At first instance, on October 26, 2022, the New Taipei District Court heard a claim against a company whose account had received NT$700,176 ([111年度訴字第1496號](https://judgment.judicial.gov.tw/FJUD/data.aspx?ty=JD&id=PCDV%2c111%2c%e8%a8%b4%2c1496%2c20221026%2c1)). The company argued that the son of its representative used the account to sell USDT and that the deposit was the price. The court found nothing proving that the crypto wallet was the son's or where the coins came from, and no messages with the supposed buyer were produced. Nor was there evidence that this buyer was the person who told the victim to pay. With no directed payment established, the court ordered repayment with 5% annual interest.

On appeal, the Taiwan High Court set that judgment aside on May 31, 2023, and dismissed the claim ([112年度上易字第292號](https://judgment.judicial.gov.tw/FJUD/data.aspx?ty=JD&id=TPHV%2c112%2c%e4%b8%8a%e6%98%93%2c292%2c20230531%2c1)). It accepted that the son had sold 23,000 USDT and received this money as the price, relying on the transaction records and on the prosecutors' non-prosecution decision, which had taken the same view. The screenshots filed at first instance left out the last character of the sending wallet address; the company filed complete records on appeal. Because the money was sent on the fraudsters' instructions, the court treated the transfer as a directed payment and held that the claim lies against whoever gave those instructions. Whether they and the USDT buyer belonged to the same group made no difference, it said. The judgment states that it cannot be appealed.

Both the Zhudong judgment and the High Court's judgment in the New Taipei case dismissed the victim's claim and pointed it at whoever gave the payment instructions. Before reaching that point, the High Court checked the transaction records to see whether the USDT sale had really happened.
