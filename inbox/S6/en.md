# "You're on Responsibility System": When Taiwan Law Actually Removes Overtime Pay

In 2014 Taiwan's Constitutional Court called the filing requirement in Article 84-1 of the Labor Standards Act "a mandatory regulation." That phrase decides many arguments about the "responsibility system" (責任制), the label some employers in Taiwan use to explain why a salaried manager gets no overtime.

A foreign manager sent to a Taiwan subsidiary may be told exactly that. Whether it is true depends on three things, and an employer needs all of them.

## Three conditions, not one label

First comes the job. [Article 84-1](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=N0030001&flno=84-1) lets employers and workers set their own working hours, regular days off and leave only for categories the central authority has designated. The Ministry of Labor's list includes system development and maintenance engineers in information services, lawyers employed by law firms, and supervisory staff paid at least NT$150,000 a month.

Supervisory staff are defined, not self-declared. Under [Article 50-1 of the Enforcement Rules](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=N0030002&flno=50-1), they are supervisors who run the business's operations and management and have decision-making power over the hiring, dismissal or working conditions of ordinary workers. A "manager" with no say over hiring or firing is a long way from that definition. The same article describes responsibility-system professionals as workers who use specialized knowledge or skills to complete a defined task and answer for its success or failure.

Next comes a written agreement that takes the Act's standards as a reference and does not harm the worker's health and welfare (Article 84-1(2)). Last is filing that agreement with the local labor authority for approval and record, known as 核備.

## No approval, no exemption

What if the third step was skipped? [Interpretation No. 726](https://cons.judicial.gov.tw/en/docdata.aspx?fid=100&id=310907) answered on November 21, 2014. An agreement that was never approved cannot displace Article 30 (normal hours), Article 32 (overtime), Article 36 (rest days), Article 37 (holidays) or Article 49 (night work by women). A signature on the agreement does not change that. In a civil dispute, the court adjusts the agreement under those articles and calculates pay under Articles 24 and 39.

Before then, the two top courts had split. The Supreme Court, in 102年度台上字第1866號, treated an unapproved agreement as still valid; the Supreme Administrative Court, in 100年度判字第226號, required both the agreement and the approval. Interpretation No. 726 settled it by holding that the approval requirement reaches the civil effect of the agreement too. Its reasoning is practical: long hours affect health, the same kind of job can look very different from one place to another, and workers usually bargain from the weaker side, so a local authority should check each agreement.

The arithmetic then follows the ordinary rules. Hours beyond eight a day or 40 a week are overtime, paid at an extra one-third of the hourly wage for the first two hours and two-thirds for the next two ([Article 24](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=N0030001&flno=24)). Rest-day work earns an extra one and one-third for the first two hours and one and two-thirds after that, and holiday work is paid double ([Article 39](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=N0030001&flno=39)). Overtime itself is capped at 12 hours a day in total and 46 hours a month ([Article 32](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=N0030001&flno=32)).

Records drive the claim. Employers must keep attendance records to the minute for five years and cannot refuse a worker's request for a copy ([Article 30](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=N0030001&flno=30), paragraphs 5 and 6). Breaches of the hours and overtime-pay rules carry fines of NT$20,000 to NT$1,000,000 ([Article 79](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=N0030001&flno=79)).

## Approved, and still paid

Approval buys flexibility, not free hours. The Ministry of Labor's guidance says Article 84-1 does not leave working hours entirely unregulated or allow overtime to go unpaid. Once a worker goes past the normal hours agreed and approved, Article 24 overtime pay applies. The agreement itself must still take the Act's standards as a reference and protect the worker's health, so an approved schedule is bounded by its own terms. The monthly minimum wage, NT$29,500 from January 1, 2026, assumes a 40-hour week. Where the approved normal hours are longer, the minimum rises in proportion.

US managers looking for a salaried-exempt box will not find one; [our column on field engineers](/en/columns/us-equipment-vendor-field-engineers-taiwan-labor-law) covers that gap from the employer's side. For the manager who was simply told "you're on responsibility system," the questions are short: which item on the Ministry's list covers the job, where the written agreement is, and where the approval is.
