# Taiwan Minimum Service Period Clauses: Validity, Training Costs, and Contractual Penalties

![An illustration explaining minimum service periods in employment contracts and the repayment of costs](../images/014-taiwan-mandatory-employment-period/featured-01.jpg)

A signed minimum-service-period clause does not, by its existence alone, establish that it is enforceable. Nor does it establish the amount repayable. In a Taiwan employment contract, the clause sets out a worker's commitment to remain employed for a specified period. It also sets out whether the worker must repay training costs, a signing bonus, or a retention bonus after leaving early, and whether the employer may seek a separate contractual penalty. Whatever label the contract uses, the statutory requirements and the actual circumstances of the payment, training, and termination must be examined step by step.

Four questions need to be kept separate:

1. Does the clause itself satisfy the statutory requirements of Article 15-1?
2. Are the agreed period and the worker's burden within a reasonable scope?
3. To whom is the reason for ending the employment contract attributable?
4. How should the resignation notice and the scope of repayment be determined?

One contract may address all four, yet different statutory provisions and evidence apply to each. Each point should therefore be examined independently: the enforceability of the clause, when the notice of resignation takes effect, whether the worker must repay a prepaid benefit or training costs, and whether any separate loss actually occurred.

## 1. When a Minimum-Service-Period Clause Can Be Enforceable

Is a minimum-service-period clause automatically void? No. Article 15-1 of Taiwan's Labor Standards Act sets the test, and its paragraph 1 provides two alternative statutory grounds. The first is that the employer provides the worker with professional skills training and bears the cost. The second is that the employer provides reasonable compensation in return for the worker's commitment to the minimum service period. With either one, a clause may satisfy the statutory requirements. Both need not be met. The question is which ground actually exists, not what name the contract gives it.

One ground does not end the review. Merely writing a ground into the contract does not automatically make the entire clause enforceable. Once a statutory ground has been established, paragraph 2 requires a separate review of whether the agreed period and the scope of liability are reasonable. The clause must not exceed a reasonable scope in light of all the circumstances, including the duration and cost of the training, the possibility of replacing the worker, and the amount and scope of the compensation.

Paragraph 3 states the consequence. A clause that violates the statutory requirements in paragraph 1 or the reasonableness standard in paragraph 2 is void. These provisions give criteria for reviewing the terms and facts of an individual agreement. They do not make every minimum-service-period clause uniformly valid or invalid from the outset.

A worker's signature may help establish that an agreement exists. It cannot replace the statutory requirements. The length of the agreed period alone should not determine the outcome either. The investment or compensation actually provided, and the reason for selecting that period, must also be examined.

## 2. First Statutory Basis: Employer-Funded Professional Skills Training

To rely on training as the basis for a clause, the employer must actually provide professional skills training to the worker and bear its cost. A training plan that describes a course as professional is not enough. Nor is an estimated cost in the contract. The evidence must connect the subject of the training, the professional or technical skills required for the role, the specific duration, completion, and actual expenditure.

Some costs can be verified directly, such as outside instructor fees, tuition paid to a training provider, and charges for materials or equipment. The review should also cover the basis for calculating any internal costs claimed by the employer. If internal personnel provided the training, examine whose time was devoted to it, how it differed from ordinary supervision or handover, and why the cost can be attributed to the particular worker. Estimated or uniformly allocated amounts alone do not prove the employer's actual expenditure.

Course outlines, training schedules, attendance records, assessment results, completion certificates, invoices, and receipts are basic records for verifying that the training occurred and what it cost. The agreement between the employer and the training provider, payment vouchers, and refund terms can further clarify the amount actually borne. If the worker paid part of the cost directly or a third party provided support, determine who ultimately bore each cost.

Where the training occurred and who delivered it do not, by themselves, draw the line between ordinary workplace familiarization and professional skills training. An in-house course may involve proven professional or technical content and substantial investment. A lengthy course at an outside institution may in substance be general introductory training. Internal training should therefore not be excluded across the board, and a course should not be treated as satisfying the statutory requirements merely because it is expensive or lengthy.

How the service period relates to the investment in training must also be explained. Review individually what skills the training was intended to develop, how those skills relate to the role, and why the proposed service period was necessary in light of the training's duration and cost. Whether the worker actually performed the relevant work after training, and how long the worker had already served, also inform the assessment of the burden's scope.

## 3. Second Statutory Basis: Reasonable Compensation

Reasonable compensation for the service commitment is the second ground. It must have a purpose and structure distinct from ordinary wages or other compensation already due for work performed. Describing a payment on a pay statement as a signing bonus, retention bonus, or prepaid benefit does not by itself determine its legal character.

Start with the payment's purpose. Is it part of the ordinary wage terms offered for recruitment, consideration for a commitment to remain for a particular period, or compensation for achieving performance targets? The contract and disclosure materials should say which. Also examine whether the payment date, amount, vesting date, connection to the service period, grounds for repayment, and repayment formula were presented so that the worker could understand them before entering into the contract.

The Ministry of Labor's June 5, 2026 guidance addresses disclosure. When a retention bonus, signing bonus, or other prepaid benefit is used as reasonable compensation for a minimum-service-period clause, it explains, that role must be clearly disclosed. An employer's later reinterpretation of the payment's purpose, or reclassification of part of the worker's wages as compensation, cannot readily substitute for the disclosure made when the contract was signed.

Reasonableness is not determined by the amount alone. Consider whether the worker received an actual additional benefit, whether the payment conditions were clear, how the portion corresponding to service already completed vests in the worker, and whether the scope of repayment is excessive. The existence of compensation does not permit a service period of any length or repayment liability in any amount without limitation.

Contract terms should also be checked against the payment actually made. Whether payment was delayed, made in installments, or conditional, how much the worker actually received after tax and deductions, and whether a separate written disclosure was provided can all help determine the scope of the compensation and the worker's actual benefit.

## 4. Reasonable Scope and the Four Statutory Factors

Article 15-1, paragraph 2 prescribes no fixed answer based on the label used or the occupation. It requires the specific contract and the structure of the investment or compensation to be reviewed under the following four factors:

1. The duration and cost of the professional skills training
2. The possibility of replacing the worker with someone in the same or a similar role
3. The amount and scope of the compensation
4. Other circumstances affecting reasonableness

The first factor examines how long the training actually lasted and how much the employer paid. A stated total is only a starting point. The review should also consider itemized evidence, the amount attributable to the individual worker, the skills the training provided, and the portion of the investment already recovered.

Replaceability, the second factor, is not determined solely by the employer's assertion that hiring is difficult. Examine whether workers are available for the same or a similar role, what qualifications and level of proficiency are required, the usual time needed to fill the position, and whether objective evidence supports the claimed operational need.

Compensation, the third factor, is a matter of scope as well as amount. It matters when the payment is made, under what conditions it vests irrevocably in the worker, how it corresponds to the entire service period, and whether service already completed is reflected if the employment ends early. Bonuses bearing the same label may be assessed differently depending on their contractual structure and substance.

Other circumstances make up the fourth factor. They may include the circumstances in which the agreement was made, the nature of the work, what was explained to the parties, the actual period served, and the reason employment ended. These are examples, and the relevant circumstances are not limited to them. The importance of each factor may vary by case. All relevant facts shown in the record should therefore be considered.

Ultimately, there must be a reasonable relationship of proportionality among the agreed period, the employer's actual investment, the difficulty of replacement, the compensation received by the worker, and the repayment burden. Enforceability should not be predetermined based on a particular occupation. The outcome of another case should not simply be applied. Both the arrangement when the contract was made and the extent of actual performance when employment ended must be considered.

## 5. Training That Cannot Support the Clause

Under the Ministry of Labor's guidance dated June 5, 2026, some training costs cannot serve as the basis for a minimum-service-period clause or a claim for a penalty or reimbursement. Four kinds of training are covered: routine education conducted regularly, ordinary on-the-job training, training that helps new hires adapt to their work and familiarizes them with the work environment and procedures, and training that employers are required by law to provide. The guidance, Ministry of Labor Letter No. 勞動關2字第1150141814號, addresses each kind separately. Such training accompanies business operations or compliance with statutory duties. Its costs therefore cannot be converted into the basis for a service obligation or a sanction for early termination.

What the training is called is not decisive. Its specific curriculum, professional or technical content, duration, the costs actually borne by the employer, and supporting evidence must be examined. So must the substance of courses that new employees would ordinarily receive, such as explanations of work rules, introductions to the organization and its systems, ordinary handovers, and basic safety procedures. Ordinary recruitment, management, or handover costs that the employer would normally bear cannot be relabeled as a separate investment subject to reimbursement.

Training is not always excluded merely because it was conducted in-house. A single program may combine general familiarization with professional skills components. Each component's subject, duration, cost, and status as legally required training should then be examined separately. A party relying on a professional skills component should use evidence to explain how it differs from ordinary training and who actually bore its cost.

In practice, review the training materials' detailed table of contents and the actual operating records. The cover alone is not enough. Determine whether the program is routinely repeated, designed to develop a specific qualification or equipment-operation skill, required by law, actually attended by the worker, and supported by evidence matching the amount claimed as training costs.

## 6. Bonuses, Repayment, and Early Departure

Must a worker who leaves early repay a signing bonus or retention bonus in full? Not necessarily. If a signing bonus, retention bonus, or other prepaid benefit was provided as reasonable compensation for a minimum-service-period clause, that purpose must have been clearly disclosed to the worker. For a worker who leaves before the period expires, the Ministry of Labor's June 5, 2026 guidance explains that repayment must be calculated in proportion to the unserved period. The employer must not demand full repayment. The actual outcome requires a review of the payment's purpose, the terms of the agreement, the period already served, and the reason employment ended.

Disclosure should not be made for the first time after a dispute arises following payment. When the contract is signed and the payment is made, the worker should be able to understand which payment compensates for the minimum service commitment, the full length of the agreed period, when the payment vests, and the formula used to settle repayment if employment ends early.

To apply proportional repayment based on the unserved period, first establish the agreed start and end dates, the actual dates worked, and the base amount used to calculate repayment. A fixed amount that takes no account of the period already served, for example, should be reviewed for consistency with the guidance's proportionality standard. If payment was made in installments or vests in stages, calculate separately the period to which each installment corresponds.

Any repayment analysis should proceed in order through the enforceability of the clause, the legal character of the payment, the period already served, the reason employment ended, and the repayment formula. The word "penalty" in the contract does not by itself establish the amount owed.

Full-repayment provisions, fixed contractual penalties unrelated to actual loss, and unilateral deductions from wages are three issues. They should not be treated as one. Their respective legal bases, the terms of the agreement, restrictions under labor law, and the lawfulness of any deduction must be examined separately. Neither the amount stated in the employer's demand nor the worker's payment of part of that amount resolves the remaining legal issues.

Reimbursement of training costs must also be distinguished from repayment of a prepaid benefit. The former centers on the actual professional skills training and the employer's expenditure, while the latter centers on the payment's purpose, disclosure, vesting conditions, and proportionality to the unserved period. If both are claimed, compare the supporting evidence for each and check whether any cost has been counted twice.

## 7. When Employment Ends for a Reason Not Attributable to the Worker

Article 15-1, paragraph 4 of Taiwan's Labor Standards Act covers an employment contract that ends before the minimum service period for a reason not attributable to the worker. The worker is then not liable for breaching the minimum-service-period agreement or for reimbursing training costs. The reason employment ended and the attribution of responsibility must be determined from specific evidence, including dismissal notices, resignation communications, and records of alleged violations of working conditions.

Early termination alone does not establish a breach by the worker. Determine who made which statement, the legal ground on which the contract ended, and to which party the circumstances that actually brought about the termination are attributable.

Relevant materials may include a dismissal notice, resignation letter, mutual termination agreement, email and messenger records, materials concerning changes to working conditions, and attendance and work records. A document may mention health or work circumstances. Its wording alone should not determine the outcome. The actual course of events, the legal ground for termination, and the related evidence must be considered together.

Dismissal, mutual termination, and alleged violations of working conditions are examples of circumstances to review, not an exhaustive list of reasons that are not attributable to the worker. Even where the same label is used, the parties' intentions and the circumstances may differ. The label in a document may not match the actual facts.

When paragraph 4 applies, the worker cannot be held liable for breaching the agreement or for reimbursing training costs. The cause of termination therefore directly affects the scope of repayment, and attribution must be addressed before calculating any amount. If a prepaid benefit or another separate claim is also at issue, the legal character and basis of each claim should be reviewed separately.

## 8. Resignation Notice Is a Separate Question

Minimum-service-period clauses do not physically or legally prevent a worker from resigning. A notice of resignation and the notice period concern when the employment relationship ends, while the enforceability of the clause and liability for repayment concern whether termination gives rise to financial liability.

When a worker terminates an indefinite-term employment contract, Article 15 of Taiwan's Labor Standards Act applies the notice periods in Article 16, paragraph 1. Article 16 itself governs termination by an employer. Its notice periods apply to a worker's resignation through Article 15.

Notice periods based on continuous service are:

1. 10 days for at least three months but less than one year of continuous service
2. 20 days for at least one year but less than three years of continuous service
3. 30 days for at least three years of continuous service

Fixed-term employment contracts for a specific task follow a separate rule under Article 15 if they exceed three years. After completing three years of service, the worker may terminate the contract by giving the employer 30 days' notice. This rule must be distinguished from the notice periods based on continuous service that apply to indefinite-term employment contracts.

Where continuous service is less than three months, another type of fixed-term contract is involved, or a statutory ground for immediate termination is asserted, the applicable provisions and facts must be examined individually. The legal conclusion cannot be predetermined merely because the contract states a longer notice period or the employer demands an immediate handover.

In practice, preserve the content and delivery date of the resignation notice, the date the employer actually received it, and the parties' communications about the final day of work. Keep four matters apart: when the resignation takes effect, whether the minimum-service-period clause is enforceable, whether training costs or prepaid benefits must be repaid, and whether any separate loss is claimed. That reduces the risk of treating one contractual phrase as resolving every issue.

## 9. Records Each Side Needs Beyond the Contract

Whether the aim is to prevent a dispute or to assess a claim already made, review the contract together with the chronological records of training, payments, service, and termination. A table can help keep the issues separate. It would show how much of the agreed period was served and remains unserved, and which records correspond to each claimed cost and item of compensation.

### The Employer's File

Start by identifying the statutory ground: professional skills training provided at the employer's cost, or reasonable compensation for the service commitment. Next, distinguish general education, routine education, legally required training, and professional skills training based on each course's actual content, duration, and purpose. Preserve course outlines, schedules, completion records, invoices, receipts, and records showing who bore the cost. Record the grounds for external and internal costs separately. On the compensation side, clearly link in writing the purpose of the compensation, the payment date, amount, vesting conditions, disclosures made to the worker, and the formula for repayment based on the unserved period.

Document the basis for setting the service period, the possibility of replacing the worker with someone in the same or a similar role, and the relationship between the employer's operational needs and its actual investment. Review whether the agreed period and repayment amount are proportionate to the scope of the training costs or compensation, and account for the period already served. Determine the cause of termination and the attribution of responsibility individually. Only then calculate the actual termination date and the served and unserved periods. Before making a wage deduction or demanding repayment, compare the contract, payment records, pay statements, the parties' communications, demand letters, and deduction records to review the legal basis and procedure.

Even with a standard-form contract, do not mechanically apply the same period and amount to every role and worker. Design the clause to reflect the actual investment in training, the compensation, and replaceability, and clearly explain the payment's purpose and the proportional repayment formula before the contract is signed.

### The Worker's File

Gather the original signed employment contract and amendments, recruitment materials, training materials, course outlines, schedules, and completion records. Determine the training's professional or technical content, whether it was general familiarization or legally required training, the amounts shown on invoices and receipts, and who actually bore each cost. For prepaid benefits such as signing and retention bonuses, obtain payment records, disclosures about their purpose as compensation, payment dates, vesting conditions, and repayment formulas.

Record separately the basis for the service period, the period already served, the remaining period, and the employer's claimed ability to replace the worker. Preserve resignation notices, dismissal notices, or mutual termination agreements, as well as proof of delivery such as email and messenger records. Arrange the actual reason and circumstances of termination chronologically. Then review them together with the employer's repayment demand, pay statements, the parties' communications, and deduction records.

Review separately the clause's enforceability, the notice and timing of resignation, repayment of training costs and prepaid benefits, and any separately alleged loss. Do not admit liability solely because the contract was signed or the employer demanded a particular amount. Examine the evidence corresponding to Article 15-1's statutory requirements, reasonableness, attribution of termination, and proportional repayment formula.

Dates worth marking in the chronology are the date the contract was signed, the start and end dates of training, each payment date, the first and last days of work, and the date each notice was delivered. If some records are held only by the employer, first organize the materials available to the worker and the basis for calculating the amount demanded. Then seek additional materials through the appropriate procedures as needed.
