# Postponing or Cancelling a Wedding in Taiwan: How Much of the Banquet and Photo Deposit Comes Back?

Five months before the banquet, you cancel, and the hotel says deposits are never refunded. Is that true? In Taiwan, a government notice sets refund rates by the date your cancellation notice arrives.

A Korean groom and a Taiwanese bride (an invented example) book 30 tables at a Taipei hotel, at NT$20,000 a table, for NT$600,000. They sign eight months ahead and pay a NT$120,000 deposit.

## Two notices, two kinds of contract

Banquets fall under the [mandatory and prohibited terms for banquet and catering service contracts](https://www.ey.gov.tw/Page/DFB720D019CCCB0A/1c8baa64-e66a-4276-a5c3-e94d0bb05308), issued by the Ministry of Health and Welfare on April 13, 2021 (Wei-Shou-Shih No. 1109900867) and effective that day. They cover meals for weddings, funerals and other occasions, served at an agreed time and place by table count. Contract terms more favourable to the consumer prevail (preamble).

The Ministry of Economic Affairs issued the [mandatory and prohibited terms for wedding photography service contracts](https://www.ey.gov.tw/Page/DFB720D019CCCB0A/2b4cea55-d3bb-4695-8510-e84a16fa9e7a) on July 15, 2019 (Ching-Shang No. 10802414910), effective January 1, 2020. They apply to photo shoots combined with gown rental, not to gown rental alone (preamble).

Under [Article 17 of the Consumer Protection Act](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=J0170001&flno=17), mandatory terms announced with Executive Yuan approval form part of a standard-form contract even if left out (paragraph 5), and clauses violating them are void (paragraph 4). A standard-form contract is one built from terms a business drafted in advance for many consumers ([Article 2](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=J0170001&flno=2)(7), (9)). Two 2024 Taiwan FDA letters, cited in a [Ministry of Culture letter of March 22, 2024](https://www.ey.gov.tw/Page/24C4B877E850ED4E/5841fa4d-0969-4d42-af54-708365120393), say bundled goods or services do not take a banquet outside these rules.

## The banquet deposit and the refund bands

Unless the parties agree on none, the deposit may not exceed 20% of the estimated total (banquet terms, Point 7). The couple's NT$120,000 sits exactly at that cap.

Point 10 then uses two dates: when the contract was signed, and when the cancellation notice reached the venue.

| Booked ahead | Notice arrival (days before): refund |
|---|---|
| 180+ days | 180+: 90%; 120–179: 75%; 60–119: 50%; 15–59: 25%; within 14, or no notice: venue may refuse |
| 90–179 days | 135–179: 90%; 90–134: 75%; 45–89: 50%; 15–44: 25%; within 14, or no notice: venue may refuse |
| 30–89 days | 60–89: 90%; 30–59: 75%; 10–29: 50%; within 9, or no notice: venue may refuse |
| 29 days or less | 20–29: 90%; 10–19: 50%; within 9, or no notice: venue may refuse |

One proviso applies to the 90% band: anything the venue keeps above NT$10,000 must still be returned.

In the first row, notice arriving 190 days out gives 90%, or NT$108,000. Of the NT$12,000 retained, the NT$2,000 above NT$10,000 also comes back, so they recover NT$110,000. At 150 days, it is 75%: NT$90,000. At 100 days, half.

Notice may go by letter, text, email or app. If it fails to arrive for reasons neither side caused, the date the sender proves the venue actually knew is presumed to be the arrival date (Point 4). Read receipts matter.

## Postponing instead of cancelling

Point 10(2) covers sudden serious illness, injury or death of the customer or a relative within the second degree, or another legitimate and specific reason that prevents the banquet going ahead. The customer may then cancel. Point 10(3) says the venue "may" substitute a 100% refund for the bands. Read on its own wording, the clause is hard to treat as a duty to refund in full.

Under Point 15, that customer may ask to change the contract, and the venue cannot refuse without a legitimate reason; the deposit carries over. A plain postponement without such a reason is negotiable; if talks fail and the couple cancels, the bands apply.

## Typhoons, power cuts, government orders

What if a typhoon hits on the day? Point 12 of the banquet terms lets either side cancel when force majeure, such as natural disaster, war or government orders, or another cause neither party is responsible for, makes performance impossible or manifestly difficult. The venue returns the deposit without interest, and the cancelling party owes no damages. A power or water cut known in advance that makes performance impossible or manifestly difficult lets either side cancel or agree otherwise (Point 13).

Wedding photography terms contain no general clause of this kind, only an outdoor-weather rule: the parties may agree on another date or method, and re-styling for a new date is free unless otherwise agreed (Point 6). Otherwise the Civil Code applies: if performance becomes wholly impossible for reasons neither side caused, the other side need not pay, partial impossibility reduces payment proportionally, and money paid is recoverable as unjust enrichment ([Article 266](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=B0000001&flno=266)). A deposit is returned in the same situation ([Article 249](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=B0000001&flno=249)(4)).

## When the venue does not deliver

What if the promised ballroom becomes a side room? If the venue is responsible for failing to perform fully, it must notify the customer at once (Point 11). The customer may cancel and claim double the deposit, plus any agreed penalty, or keep the contract and reduce the price in proportion. Damages are available either way.

Advertisements, oral or email promises and annexes are part of the contract (Point 3), and an advertisement more favourable than the contract controls (Point 5). Clauses letting the venue change terms unilaterally, or raise the price in disguise or by extra charges, are prohibited (prohibited terms 4 and 5). With fewer guests than guaranteed, the venue may bill the guarantee, but the customer may hold unused tables for later or take goods or services worth the balance after the venue's necessary costs (Point 14).

## Wedding photos have no refund table

Here the customer may terminate at any time (photography terms, Point 12). The studio may claim payment for services already provided and, if it suffered other loss, damages. No forfeiture percentage appears.

Point 4 limits advance payments. The deposit may not exceed 20% of the total; together with the gown-selection or shoot-date payment, 50%; through the shoot, 60%; through photo selection, 80%. A gown security deposit is capped separately at 10%. A designated photographer or stylist cannot be swapped at will; if that person cannot serve, the studio must notify the customer to choose again, and a customer who paid a designation fee and declines may terminate, recover double the fee and get back fees for services not yet provided (Point 13). Defects after photo selection entitle the customer to repair or a remake within the agreed time, then rescission or a price cut if that fails or is impossible, plus damages if the studio is at fault (Point 8). Delayed selection or pickup triggers a demand giving at least seven days; afterwards the studio is liable only for intent or gross negligence, and six months later may no longer be liable for custody (Point 7). No extra charges beyond the total are allowed unless agreed (Point 3).

## "No refunds under any circumstances"

In a banquet contract, such a clause conflicts with the Point 10 bands and is void under Article 17(4). Clauses excluding the customer's right to cancel or terminate are prohibited (prohibited term 3). A photo contract forfeiting everything on termination can be tested against Point 12 and prohibited term 2, which bars clauses reducing the studio's obligations. Any standard term that breaches good faith and is manifestly unfair to the consumer is void ([Article 12](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=J0170001&flno=12)).

Review time counts too. Businesses must allow a reasonable review period of up to 30 days before signing; otherwise the terms are not part of the contract, though the consumer may elect to keep them ([Article 11-1](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=J0170001&flno=11-1)(1), (3)). Both notices set at least three days, with immediate or reasonable review for banquets booked within three days of the event (banquet terms, Point 2).

If an amount survives as a penalty, it is deemed total compensation for non-performance unless otherwise agreed ([Civil Code Article 250](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=B0000001&flno=250)(2)). Courts may reduce it for partial performance ([Article 251](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=B0000001&flno=251)) or when excessive ([Article 252](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=B0000001&flno=252)); see [how courts cut penalty clauses](/en/columns/taiwan-contract-penalty-clause-reduction-civil-code-252-2026-judgments).

Complaints go to the business, a consumer group or a service centre; the business must handle it appropriately within 15 days ([Article 43](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=J0170001&flno=43)). Then come the local consumer ombudsman and mediation (Article 43(3), [Article 44](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=J0170001&flno=44)). A business that ignores an order to correct a non-compliant contract by the deadline faces a fine of NT$30,000 to NT$300,000; if it misses a second deadline, NT$50,000 to NT$500,000, imposable per violation ([Article 56-1](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=J0170001&flno=56-1)).
