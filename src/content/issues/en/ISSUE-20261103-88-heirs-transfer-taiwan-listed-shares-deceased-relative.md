# A relative died holding Taiwan-listed shares. How do the heirs get them out?

Shares in a Taiwan securities account stay registered to the decedent until the heirs complete a transfer. The broker does not move them to a family member on request; the issuer has to approve an inheritance transfer first.

The Taiwan Stock Exchange set out the route in a [February 26, 2026 explainer](https://www.twse.com.tw/market_insights/zh/detail/8a8216d69c37647f019c98cf280c00ac) on inherited securities. It runs in four steps: find the holdings, settle the estate tax, assemble the transfer file, and have the issuer approve it.

## Step one: find out what was held

Who can see what the decedent held? Families often know the broker but not the full list of holdings, especially when the relative traded through more than one firm over the years. According to the exchange, an estate administrator, executor or heir can apply to Taiwan Depository & Clearing Corporation (TDCC), at the counter, online or by mail, for the decedent's securities movement and balance records once they provide supporting documents.

That record also matters for the estate tax return. Listed shares are generally valued at the closing price on the date of death; if there was no trade that day, the last earlier closing price applies, and if prices swung sharply, the average closing price over the month before death. Our column on [valuing listed shares for estate tax](/en/columns/taiwan-estate-tax-listed-shares-closing-price-valuation) explains the rule.

## Step two: the estate tax certificate comes first

[Article 8 of the Estate and Gift Tax Act](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=G0340072&flno=8) says that until the estate tax is paid, the estate cannot be divided or transfers registered. The exception is a certificate under [Article 41](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=G0340072&flno=41): a clearance certificate once the tax, any fines, late-payment surcharges and interest are paid; an exemption certificate when no tax is assessed; a certificate for property excluded from the estate under Article 16; or, for special reasons, a consent-to-transfer certificate against a firm tax guarantee or a mortgage over taxable real estate in favor of the tax office.

[Article 42](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=G0340072&flno=42) puts the duty on the other side too. Government agencies and private businesses handling a transfer of inherited property must ask for one of these certificates and may not register the transfer without it.

For a decedent who was not an ROC national, generally only property located in Taiwan is subject to Taiwan estate tax, and the return goes to the National Taxation Bureau of Taipei ([Articles 1 and 23](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=G0340072&flno=23)). Taiwan-listed shares of a Taiwan company fall on the Taiwan side. Our column on [estate tax for foreign decedents](/en/columns/taiwan-estate-tax-foreign-decedent) covers scope and deadlines.

## Step three: the transfer file

Paperwork next. The list comes from [Article 24 of the Regulations Governing the Administration of Shareholder Services of Public Companies](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=G0400006&flno=24). For an inheritance transfer, the heir fills in a transfer application and attaches:

- a succession chart drawn up by the applying heir under Articles 1138 to 1140 of the Civil Code, with the heir responsible for any omission or error;
- a household register copy or equivalent document showing the heirs;
- identity documents, which for a foreign heir means a residence permit, a passport, or an identity document authenticated by a Taiwan mission abroad, issued by a local court or government body, or notarized in that country;
- where there are several heirs sharing by statutory portions, a share allocation consent signed by all of them, or the court judgment if a court decided;
- the estate tax clearance or exemption certificate under Article 41.

An heir who appoints someone else to do this must appoint a Taiwan national, who shows a national ID card and the power of attorney. Heirs from mainland China follow a separate rule in the same article: inheritance documents authenticated by the Straits Exchange Foundation and notarized locally, with a person in Taiwan acting under a valid authorization if they cannot come in person. The exchange's explainer adds that a will or court ruling, where the distribution rests on one, goes into the file.

## Step four: review and transfer

Applications go to the decedent's broker. The broker passes the file through TDCC to the issuer or its stock agent, or the heirs can deliver it themselves. Once the issuer or agent approves, TDCC moves the shares into each heir's own book-entry account.

On this route each heir needs a Taiwan securities account to receive the shares; the exchange's explainer also describes other arrangements, for example through an account in an estate administrator's name. A foreign heir who lives abroad opens one under the rules for offshore investors, which require a local agent or representative and a custodian. After the transfer, the exchange notes, heirs can sell in lots or as a package through their own accounts. Stock dividends declared after the death but not yet registered to the heirs also belong to them, and can be claimed after the transfer.

## Where foreign families get stuck

Two practical points deserve attention. Identity papers do not all follow one authentication rule: a residence permit or passport is accepted as it is, while other foreign identity documents must be authenticated, issued by a local court or government body, or notarized. Confirm with the broker which form each heir will use before collecting papers abroad. And because the appointed agent must be a Taiwan national, a family living overseas will usually need a person in Taiwan to handle the counter work.

When several heirs splitting by statutory portions disagree, the all-heir consent cannot be signed. Nothing moves on that route until there is an agreement or a court judgment. A distribution under a valid will takes a different path, with the will attached instead.
