# Your Taiwan employer wants to pay part of your salary in USDT. Can you insist on NT dollars?

Taiwan's Labor Standards Act (勞動基準法) says wages must be paid in legal tender. That means NT dollars. The only statutory exception lets an employer pay part of the wage in kind, and three conditions come attached: the practice must rest on custom or the nature of the business, the labor contract must spell it out, and the item must be valued fairly and suit the worker and the worker's family. A USDT clause has to fit inside that exception. If it does not, you can ask for the agreed wage in NT dollars, and the employer that keeps paying in tokens risks a fine.

Tax works on a separate track. Pay for work remains salary income whatever form it takes, and the employer is still the one that has to withhold; how a token payment is valued when it arrives is a narrower question the statute does not answer specifically.

## What Article 22 actually allows

[Article 22](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=N0030001&flno=22) is short. Its first sentence sets the rule: wages are paid in legal tender (法定通用貨幣). Its second sentence opens a narrow door: "based on custom or the nature of the business," the labor contract may provide for part of the wage to be paid in kind (實物). When that happens, the in-kind item must be priced fairly and must suit the needs of the worker and the worker's family.

Start with the definition. [Article 2, item 3](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=N0030001&flno=2) treats as wages whatever a worker obtains as compensation for work, including regular payments made in cash or in kind, so calling a payment USDT does not take it outside the wage rules. Nothing in the Act mentions stablecoins or digital assets. The Ministry of Labor's published Q&A on Article 22 discusses meals, lodging and utilities provided to workers, and says those may be agreed as part of the wage so long as the total, including the cash portion, is at least the statutory minimum. It does not address tokens. Neither Article 22 nor that Q&A settles whether USDT can count as payment in kind at all. If it can, the other statutory conditions still have to be met.

Take them in turn. Is paying part of the wage this way genuinely based on custom or on the nature of the business? The company's label alone does not establish that condition. Does your signed contract, not just an internal policy email, say which part of the wage is paid this way? And if USDT could count as payment in kind at all, is it valued fairly and suited to the needs of you and your family, as Article 22 requires? A contract may also state the conversion source, time and fee allocation, but those terms do not by themselves settle whether the exception applies. An offer letter that fixes the wage at NT$120,000 and then delivers "the equivalent in USDT" at a rate the company chooses, minus network fees, invites dispute on valuation (an invented example).

Note the word "part." Article 22 never authorizes paying the whole salary in kind.

## Fees, shortfalls and the payslip

Pay can shrink in other ways than a falling token price. Exchange spreads, withdrawal charges and network fees can also come out of the amount you receive.

[Article 22, paragraph 2](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=N0030001&flno=22) requires wages to be paid in full and directly to the worker. The same paragraph allows exceptions where a law provides otherwise or where employer and worker have agreed otherwise, so a fee clause you actually agreed to is treated differently from one the company imposed later. Read the contract before you argue the point.

Your payslip should show the gap. Under [Article 23](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=N0030001&flno=23), unless the parties specially agreed otherwise or wages are paid monthly in advance, wages must be paid regularly at least twice a month. Separately, the employer must provide a breakdown of how each wage item was calculated. It must also keep a wage register for five years. If your payslip says NT$120,000 and your wallet received tokens worth less on the day they arrived, the breakdown is where the employer has to explain the conversion.

Whatever form the pay takes, the agreed wage may not fall below the statutory floor in [Article 21](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=N0030001&flno=21).

## What you can ask for, and what the employer risks

Can you simply refuse tokens and ask for NT dollars? Where the USDT arrangement does not meet Article 22's conditions, the contractual wage is still owed in legal tender, and a written request for payment in NT dollars puts the employer on notice. Keep it factual: the contract clause, the pay dates, the NT dollar amount promised, and what actually arrived.

Local labor authorities have their own tools. Under [Article 27](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=N0030001&flno=27), when an employer does not pay wages on time, the authority may order payment within a deadline. [Article 79](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=N0030001&flno=79) sets a fine of NT$20,000 to NT$1,000,000 for violating, among others, Article 21, paragraph 1 and Articles 22 through 25, and the same range for ignoring an Article 27 payment order. The authority may raise the fine by up to half of the statutory maximum depending on the size of the business, the number of workers affected or the circumstances.

Unpaid contractual wages are a rights dispute under the Labor Dispute Act (勞資爭議處理法). Either side may apply for mediation, and the application goes to the city or county authority where the worker provides services ([Article 5](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=N0020007&flno=5), [Article 9](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=N0020007&flno=9)).

## Tax: salary first, the token later

Wages paid by an employer are salary income (薪資所得), the third category in [Article 14 of the Income Tax Act](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=G0340003&flno=14) (所得稅法). Paying in a token does not change that category. The same article's second paragraph says income received in kind, in securities or in foreign currency is converted at the government-set price or approved exchange rate at the time it is received, and at the local market price where no government price exists. USDT is not named there, and the cited provisions give no USDT-specific valuation method. Treat the valuation as an open point and keep the exchange's NT dollar price and timestamp for each payment.

Withholding is the employer's job. [Article 88](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=G0340003&flno=88) requires a business that pays salary to withhold tax at the time of payment, at rates and under rules drafted by the Ministry of Finance and approved by the Executive Yuan. [Article 89](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=G0340003&flno=89) names the paying business as the withholding agent for salary and the person receiving it as the taxpayer. If it fails to withhold, [Article 114](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=G0340003&flno=114) puts the penalty on the withholding agent, together with an order to make up the tax. This applies whether or not you are a tax resident under Article 7. For a nonresident, [Article 2](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=G0340003&flno=2) adds that tax on Taiwan-source income is, unless the Act provides otherwise, collected by withholding at source.

Selling the USDT later is a separate event with its own records. That question, and how a tax bureau may ask about exchange location and cost, is covered in [the column on crypto sales and Taiwan tax bureau letters](/en/columns/taiwan-crypto-tax-audit-letter-exchange-location-cost-basis).

## Before you sign, or after the first short payment

If the clause is still in a draft offer, ask for the NT dollar wage to be stated as the amount owed, with any token payment described as optional, revocable and converted at a stated source and time, with fees borne by the employer. If you have already been paid short, collect the contract, every payslip, the wallet or exchange deposit records with timestamps, and the messages announcing the USDT policy.

A Taiwan lawyer can check whether the clause fits Article 22 and calculate the NT dollar shortfall for each pay period. Counsel can also send the demand to the employer and represent you in mediation before the labor authority or in a wage claim. The same review can catch withholding statements that do not match what you were actually paid.
