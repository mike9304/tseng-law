# Micron's union application and Taiwan's corporate-group rules

Taoyuan City was examining a proposed Micron group-enterprise union whose stated scope covered several companies and crossed county boundaries, according to a [United Daily News report published on November 12](https://money.udn.com/money/story/5612/9874637). The city said it had received the application on October 27. It was checking possible overlapping applications, consulting the Ministry of Economic Affairs about the companies' relationship and reviewing other documents.

For international employers with several Taiwan entities, the registration question reaches beyond the name on a factory gate. Workers may be employed by different companies within the same group. Their employers' legal relationship and the union's proposed membership scope matter. The reported examination was the city's position on November 12; the article did not establish a later registration outcome.

## A corporate union can span qualifying related companies

[Labor Union Act Article 6](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=N0020001&flno=6) includes workers of enterprises with a controlling and subordinate relationship under the Company Act in the corporate-union category. That category also covers workers of the same factory or workplace, or the same business entity. A union across related companies therefore fits an existing statutory category.

Common branding alone does not establish the relationship. [Company Act Article 369-2](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=J0080001&flno=369-2) identifies a controlling company where its holding exceeds half of another company's outstanding voting shares or total capital. Beyond that ownership route, direct or indirect control over another company's personnel, financial affairs or business operations can also establish a controlling and subordinate relationship.

Ownership is not the only route. This matters where an overseas group chart describes reporting lines but leaves the actual voting interests or operational control unclear. Those underlying facts are needed to assess the statutory relationship; the news report alone does not resolve them for the Micron companies.

The union's charter supplies another part of the scope. Among the matters required by [Article 12](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=N0020001&flno=12) are its district, office, organization, membership entry and exit rules, and members' rights and duties. Those provisions help identify whom the proposed organization seeks to include. The examples are only part of the statutory charter requirements.

## Whose 30-day period is it?

Does the registration provision give the authority 30 days to approve an application? [Article 11](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=N0020001&flno=11) places that filing period on the preparatory committee. It starts with the inaugural meeting.

At least 30 workers must initiate formation and establish a preparatory committee. Its tasks include publicly recruiting members, drafting the charter and convening the inaugural meeting. Within 30 days after that meeting, the committee must submit the charter and lists of members, directors and supervisors to the authority where the union's office is located to seek a registration certificate.

That is the applicant's filing obligation. The sentence does not establish a 30-day agency decision deadline or automatic approval when that period expires. The date an authority receives an application, standing alone, therefore cannot show that the particular filing period was met or that a certificate must already have been issued.

For a Taiwan HR or legal team responding to a group-union application, the relevant file connects the companies named in the charter with evidence of their relationship. The inaugural-meeting record answers the filing-date question. Membership terms answer a different one: which workers the proposed organization intends to cover. Keeping those records connected gives the registration discussion a concrete basis beyond a shared corporate name.
