# Closing a Taiwan Company: What Happens to Capital and Company Assets?

![Closing a Taiwan company and accounting for its capital, liabilities, and residual assets](../images/002-withdraw-capital-taiwan-company/featured-01.png)

Closing a Taiwan company is not simply a matter of emptying its bank account and stopping sales. A company has its own property, obligations, records, and legal status. Its assets belong to the company, not to its shareholders. A shareholder who contributed capital received an ownership interest, but that contribution does not give the shareholder a continuing right to withdraw company cash, equipment, receivables, or other assets at will.

The correct route depends first on what the payment is and whether the company will continue. Company property includes cash and everything else the company owns or may collect. Paid-in capital or capital contributions describe amounts contributed in exchange for ownership interests. Lawful business expenses pay company obligations incurred for company purposes. Dividends distribute earnings under the applicable corporate and tax rules. Repayment of genuine company debt satisfies an actual obligation supported by evidence. A capital reduction changes the registered capital structure. Liquidation, by contrast, winds up the company and may end with a distribution of residual assets after liabilities have been handled.

These categories should not be collapsed into the informal idea of “getting the investment back.” A bank balance may include customer deposits, borrowed funds, unpaid taxes, employee entitlements, or money needed to perform existing contracts. The accounting label, legal authority, transaction documents, tax consequences, and effect on creditors all matter. Before any payment is made, the company should identify the nature of the payment and the legal process that supports it.

![](../images/002-withdraw-capital-taiwan-company/img-01.png)

## 1. Company Assets, Paid-In Capital, and Shareholder Funds Are Different

A company that will permanently cease operations generally should register its dissolution, complete liquidation, discharge its debts and tax obligations, and only then distribute its remaining assets to shareholders. If the company will continue, it may consider a lawful capital reduction appropriate to its company form. Ordinary business expenses, lawful dividends, and repayment of genuine company debt are separate legal and tax categories and require their own authority, documentation, accounting, and tax treatment.

The distinction begins with ownership. Once money or property has been contributed to the company and the company has acquired it, the asset is not held in a personal wallet for the contributing shareholder. Directors, responsible persons, managers, and shareholders must deal with company property for an authorized company purpose and through the appropriate decision-making and accounting process. The same principle applies even when one person owns all of the equity and controls the bank account.

Paid-in capital is also different from the particular cash that remains in a bank account months or years later. Capital may have been lawfully spent on rent, payroll, inventory, professional fees, equipment, taxes, or other operating needs. Conversely, a substantial cash balance does not prove that the company has distributable earnings or that the cash may be returned as capital. The company’s books, financial statements, resolutions, contracts, tax records, and outstanding obligations must be considered together.

Article 9 of Taiwan’s Company Act applies when share subscription payments or capital contributions owed to a company were not actually paid but were represented as fully paid in the incorporation filing, or when paid-in amounts were returned to shareholders or shareholders were allowed to withdraw them after incorporation. The responsible person may be punished by imprisonment for up to five years or detention and may also be fined from NT$500,000 to NT$2.5 million. Article 9 is not a general criminal prohibition on every lawful use of company funds.

That limited statutory rule is important. A properly supported payment of rent, wages, a supplier invoice, or taxes—or repayment of a genuine loan—does not become unlawful merely because it is made from company funds. At the same time, labeling a transfer an “expense” or “loan repayment” does not determine its legal character. The company should be able to show the commercial purpose, contractual or corporate authority, payment trail, recipient, accounting entry, withholding treatment, and tax reporting.

Under Article 90 of the Company Act, a liquidator may not distribute company assets to shareholders before all company liabilities have been discharged. A liquidator who violates that rule may be sentenced to imprisonment for up to one year or detention and may also be fined up to NT$60,000.

The Article 90 sequence protects creditors during liquidation. It also explains why a proposed shareholder distribution cannot be assessed by looking only at current cash. Claims may still need to be collected or disputed, assets may need to be sold, and liabilities may include employee claims, taxes, professional fees, secured debt, contingent claims, or the cost of completing the liquidation itself.

Any other civil, criminal, accounting, or tax consequence—and any issue involving a director’s duties—depends on the payment’s purpose, authority, documentation, accounting treatment, and particular facts. A transfer to a shareholder or related person should not automatically be treated as a breach of trust. Instead, it should be classified and reviewed under the rules that govern the transaction.

## 2. Permanently Closing a Taiwan Company

Dissolution of a limited company requires approval by shareholders holding at least two-thirds of all voting rights. Dissolution of a company limited by shares ordinarily requires a meeting attended by shareholders representing at least two-thirds of all outstanding shares and approval by a majority of the voting rights represented at the meeting. If a company with publicly issued shares does not meet that attendance threshold, it may instead act at a meeting attended by shareholders representing a majority of all outstanding shares if at least two-thirds of the voting rights represented at the meeting approve. Any higher quorum or voting threshold in the articles of incorporation controls. The dissolution change registration generally must be filed within 15 days after dissolution.

Dissolution and liquidation perform different functions. Dissolution ends the company’s ordinary operating phase and places it on the path to winding up. Liquidation addresses unfinished business, determines and realizes company property, collects claims, handles liabilities and taxes, conducts creditor procedures, and ultimately determines whether any residual assets may be distributed. Simply ceasing sales, abandoning an office, or closing a bank account does not complete either process.

The resolution rules depend on the statutory company form. Company Act Article 113 requires a limited company to obtain approval from shareholders holding at least two-thirds of all voting rights. Under Company Act Article 316, a company limited by shares ordinarily acts at a meeting attended by shareholders representing at least two-thirds of all outstanding shares, with approval by a majority of the voting rights represented at that meeting. If a company with publicly issued shares cannot meet that ordinary attendance threshold, the alternative is attendance representing a majority of outstanding shares and approval by at least two-thirds of the voting rights represented. The articles of incorporation may impose a higher quorum or voting requirement, which controls.

The following sequence is a qualified educational outline. The necessary decisions, forms, notices, tax treatment, and order may differ with the company form, cause of dissolution, articles of incorporation, financial condition, regulated activities, workforce, and foreign-investment structure.

### 1. Review the company before adopting the closing plan

Review contracts, employees, permits, company assets, debts, taxes, litigation, bank accounts, foreign-investment approvals, and remittance records. The review should extend to leases, customer advances, warranties, intellectual property, insurance, payroll, retirement or severance obligations, security interests, guarantees, related-party accounts, electronic records, and access to tax systems. Identify the people who will preserve the books, company seals, credentials, and channels for receiving notices.

This review is not merely administrative. It helps determine whether the company can perform or terminate contracts, whether licenses require separate action, whether assets are encumbered, and whether a proposed payment could prejudice a creditor. It also provides the starting information for the liquidator’s inventory and balance sheet.

### 2. Adopt the dissolution resolution

Adopt the dissolution resolution under Company Act Article 113 or 316, applying the rule for a limited company or company limited by shares and checking the articles of incorporation for higher requirements. The minutes and supporting records should accurately identify the proposal, meeting or consent process, votes, effective timing, and related appointments or authorizations.

### 3. Apply for dissolution change registration

Under Article 4 of the Company Registration Regulations (公司登記辦法), the company generally must apply for dissolution change registration within 15 days after dissolution. The filing should be prepared based on the company’s actual registration record and the applicable ground for dissolution. Dissolution change registration does not, by itself, automatically cancel every tax registration, permit, license, contract, account, or other obligation; separate measures may be required.

### 4. Address the current-period final income tax return

For profit-seeking enterprise income tax, the company must file its current-period final income tax return within 45 days after the competent authority approves the dissolution. Official guidance explains that the period uses the approval document’s issuance date and begins counting from the following day. The company should nevertheless confirm the actual approval document, its issuance date, and the applicable dissolution basis before calculating the deadline.

The current-period final return should be coordinated with the accounting cutoff, supporting schedules, withholding matters, unpaid taxes, and records that the liquidator will need. It is distinct from the liquidation-income return filed after liquidation ends.

### 5. Conduct the liquidation

Select or confirm the liquidator under the applicable law, articles of incorporation, and resolutions, and make the required court filing. The liquidator should take control of the company’s seals, books and records, supporting evidence, accounts, and relevant systems and should prepare the inventory of property and balance sheet. The liquidator’s responsibilities include concluding pending business, collecting claims, realizing property when necessary, discharging debts and taxes, and completing the applicable notices, public announcements, claim-presentation periods, or other creditor procedures.

The exact creditor process and supporting filings depend on the company and the governing provisions. Employee wages and retirement or severance obligations, secured claims, taxes, disputed claims, pending litigation, guarantees, and liquidation expenses may require separate analysis. Records should remain internally consistent from the collection or sale of an asset through the accounting entry and tax filing.

### 6. Distribute only residual assets

Only residual assets remaining after liabilities and taxes have been handled may be distributed, under the applicable rules and ownership interests. Before that point, the liquidator should not treat the registered capital amount, historic investment amount, or bank balance as a sum automatically owed back to shareholders. The amount and character of a final distribution depend on the realized assets, allowed claims, liquidation costs, taxes, and legally applicable allocation rules.

### 7. Complete the closing reports

File the liquidation-income return within 30 days after liquidation ends and make the required liquidation-completion report to the court. The tax filing and court report are distinct parts of closing the matter, and supporting books and documents remain important even after active operations have stopped.

Company Act Article 24 provides an important qualification: dissolution caused by consolidation or merger, split-up, or bankruptcy may not follow ordinary liquidation. The cause of dissolution must therefore be identified before applying an ordinary outline. No fixed liquidation duration can be promised because the timeline depends on assets, claims, creditor procedures, disputes, taxes, records, and court or agency requirements.

## 3. When Liabilities Exceed Assets or the Company Cannot Pay Its Debts

Liquidation following dissolution is not limited to companies whose assets exceed their liabilities. Under Article 89 of the Company Act, if the aggregate value of the company’s assets is insufficient to satisfy its liabilities, the liquidator must file an application for a declaration of bankruptcy. Whether ordinary liquidation may continue requires a fact-specific review of the company’s assets, liabilities, ability to pay debts as they mature, collateral, tax liabilities, and creditor body.

Several financial concepts must be separated. Balance-sheet insolvency compares the value of assets with total liabilities. Inability to pay debts as they mature concerns whether the company has sufficient funds available when its obligations become due. Liquidity asks whether assets can be converted into usable funds at the needed time. A company may show assets exceeding liabilities on paper but still be unable to meet payroll or a tax payment because its receivables are uncollectible or its property cannot be sold promptly. A temporary cash shortage, however, does not by itself determine the legal conclusion in a particular case.

Book value is not necessarily realizable value. Inventory may be obsolete, accounts receivable may be disputed, intellectual property may have no ready purchaser, and real estate or equipment may be pledged as collateral or subject to attachment, co-ownership, or contractual restrictions. Security interests and priority claims can affect which assets are available and how proceeds must be applied. Employee claims, tax liabilities, litigation, arbitration, guarantees, and contingent obligations may change the analysis as information develops.

The company and liquidator should therefore review current books, bank records, claims, liabilities, security interests, taxes, creditor treatment, and reliable asset valuations before any shareholder distribution. Payments should not be made simply in the order demands arrive without considering collateral, priority, avoidance risk, and the applicable process. New shareholder funding also requires care: the company should assess whether it restores a realistic ability to pay, merely delays an unavoidable problem, or changes creditors’ position unfairly.

Article 89 should be applied to the company’s actual financial circumstances, not reduced to a supposed rule that bankruptcy requires both property available for liquidation and multiple creditors. The number and identity of creditors may be relevant to the overall facts, but they do not replace the statutory inquiry into solvency, debts coming due, security, priority, and realizable value.

## 4. Returning Capital While the Company Continues: Capital Reduction

If the company will continue operating but wishes to return part of its capital, a lawful capital reduction may be considered. A capital reduction is not an informal shareholder withdrawal, and it is not always available. It changes the company’s capital structure through a formal process and must be evaluated against the company form, governing law, articles of incorporation, financial position, and purpose of the reduction.

The company should complete the steps required for its company form: adopt the proper resolution, implement creditor-protection measures, verify and account for the capital change, make any foreign-investment filings, analyze tax and withholding, review remittance requirements, and apply for change registration. Depending on the facts, creditor protection may involve notice, announcement, an objection period, payment, or security. A foreign-invested company should also confirm how the reduction interacts with prior investment approvals, investment-amount records, bank documentation, foreign-exchange handling, and the characterization of any outbound payment.

Approval of a capital reduction does not answer every payment question. The records should connect the resolution, verified capital change, shareholder interests, accounting entries, payment evidence, withholding position, tax filing, and registration. The company should also assess whether it will retain enough resources to continue its business, perform contracts, pay employees and taxes, and discharge debts as they mature after the reduction.

Other payments remain separate from capital reduction. Lawful business expenses require a genuine company purpose, an underlying obligation, supporting invoices or other evidence, proper approval, accounting, and applicable tax treatment. A shareholder who pays a genuine company obligation may have a reimbursement claim, but both the expense and the reimbursement should be documented rather than inferred from common ownership.

Dividends must be supported by distributable earnings and the required corporate action. Cash in a bank account alone does not establish distributable earnings. The company must consider losses, reserves, financial statements, ownership interests, resolution requirements, withholding, and income-tax consequences. Describing a transfer as a dividend cannot cure the absence of earnings or required approval.

Repayment of a genuine shareholder loan or other company debt requires an actual debtor-creditor relationship. Relevant evidence may include the loan or supply contract, original transfer, commercial terms, approvals, maturity, interest, invoices, repayment records, and accounting history. Related-party terms, withholding, tax reporting, thin-capitalization or transfer-pricing issues, and remittance documentation may also require review. Each expense, dividend, debt repayment, and capital-reduction payment must stand on its own authority and evidence.

## 5. Temporarily Suspending Business Instead of Closing

A company suspending business for at least one month must apply for business-suspension registration before the suspension begins or within 15 days after it begins, and each suspension period may not exceed one year. Suspension does not eliminate the company or automatically eliminate its tax-filing obligations; a profit-seeking enterprise that suspends business during the year still must file its annual income tax return. Other obligations depend on the company’s assets, employees, contracts, licenses, and tax profile.

Business suspension preserves the company’s legal existence. It may be appropriate when operations are genuinely paused and a later resumption remains possible, but it is not a substitute for dissolution and liquidation when the company will permanently close. The company should not assume that suspension turns off every filing, payment, notice, or preservation duty.

During suspension, changes to the registered address, responsible person, articles of incorporation, or capital may still require change registration. The company should maintain a current address and responsible contact at which agencies, courts, creditors, contracting parties, and other stakeholders can deliver notices. Company seals, accounting records, tax records, digital credentials, and material correspondence should be retained and kept accessible.

Vehicles and real property may continue to incur taxes and carrying costs even when the company has no revenue. Leases, insurance, financing, maintenance, subscriptions, and other contracts may continue unless amended or terminated. Employee status and labor obligations must be addressed. Industry licenses may have separate suspension, renewal, reporting, or surrender rules. Bank accounts should be managed deliberately, with authorized persons and reliable record retention rather than simply left unattended.

Before the registered suspension period ends, the company should reassess whether it will resume business, seek another lawful suspension period where available, or move to permanent closure. The review should be completed early enough to make any required filing and to update information that has changed. Repeated delay can make a later liquidation harder if records are lost, responsible persons become unavailable, addresses are outdated, or claims and tax matters remain unresolved.
