# False Financial Reports in Taiwan: Who May Claim Civil Damages

An investor who still holds shares can seek damages for a false financial report under [Article 20-1, paragraph 1 of Taiwan's Securities and Exchange Act](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=G0400001&flno=20-1). The provision covers good-faith acquirers, sellers, and holders of the issuer's securities who suffer loss caused by misrepresentation or concealment in the report's essential content. A price decline alone does not establish every element of the claim or the recoverable amount. An issuer's admission of falsity does not, by itself, establish all those matters either.

## Shares bought after the report and held through the correction

An American living in Taipei buys shares of a Taiwan issuer through a securities broker. The purchase takes place the day after the company publicly announces its annual financial report. The buyer still holds the shares when the issuer later announces that a revenue figure in that report was false. This is an invented example.

Does buying the next day establish good faith? The date alone does not. [Article 20-1, paragraph 1](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=G0400001&flno=20-1) requires good faith, misrepresentation or concealment in essential content, and loss caused by that defect. The example identifies a report and a holding period. It leaves those legal requirements to be established.

Covered documents include the issuer's financial reports and financial and business documents filed or publicly disclosed under the Act, as well as financial reports filed or publicly disclosed under Article 36, paragraph 1 ([Article 20, paragraph 2](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=G0400001&flno=20); [Article 20-1, paragraph 1](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=G0400001&flno=20-1)). Here, the document is the annual financial report described in [Article 36, paragraph 1, subparagraph 1](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=G0400001&flno=36).

Selling before the later announcement does not by itself rule out a claim. [Article 20-1, paragraph 1](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=G0400001&flno=20-1) also covers good-faith sellers who suffer loss from misrepresentation or concealment in the report's essential content. Someone who acquired shares in their own name can consider a claim as an acquirer; someone who kept the shares can consider one as a holder. Each still has to establish the statutory requirements.

Brokerage arrangements also matter. If a customer engages a securities broker to buy or sell as a commission agent in the broker's own name, [Article 20, paragraph 4](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=G0400001&flno=20) treats the customer as the acquirer or seller for paragraph 3. [Article 20-1, paragraph 6](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=G0400001&flno=20-1) extends that rule to report-based claims under paragraph 1.

## Liability depends on the person's role

[Article 20-1, paragraph 1](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=G0400001&flno=20-1) names two groups of defendants: the issuer and its responsible persons, and the issuer's employees who signed or sealed the report or document in question. Either a signature or a seal suffices for the employee requirement. Helping prepare the report, or having a name printed on it, does not satisfy that requirement without a signature or seal. Responsible-person status is a separate legal classification; an accounting officer's signature alone does not establish that status. The report defect and resulting loss must still be established.

For the people listed in paragraph 1 other than the issuer, [Article 20-1, paragraph 2](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=G0400001&flno=20-1) provides a defense. They must prove both that they exercised all due diligence and that they had good cause to reasonably believe the content contained no misrepresentation or concealment. Both parts are required. The issuer cannot use this defense.

## What the attesting CPA did

Attestation alone is insufficient. A CPA who attests the covered reports or documents is liable if misconduct, or a violation or neglect of professional duties, causes the loss described in paragraph 1 ([Article 20-1, paragraph 3](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=G0400001&flno=20-1)). The claim therefore concerns both the work the CPA performed and its connection to the investor's loss.

Working papers can be sought through the court. To pursue that CPA liability, a good-faith acquirer, seller, or holder may ask the court to obtain the CPA's working papers and request to inspect or copy them. The CPA and accounting firm may not refuse ([Article 20-1, paragraph 4](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=G0400001&flno=20-1)).

Negligent defendants under paragraphs 1 and 3, except the issuer, pay in proportion to their degree of responsibility when their negligence causes the paragraph 1 loss. That proportional-liability rule does not apply to the issuer ([Article 20-1, paragraph 5](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=G0400001&flno=20-1)). A claim against the issuer still requires the essential-content defect and resulting loss specified in paragraph 1.

## Trading misconduct and false reports have distinct provisions

Misrepresentation, fraud, and other acts sufficient to mislead others are prohibited during the public offering, issuance, private placement, or trading of securities. A person who breaks that rule owes compensation for the resulting loss of good-faith acquirers or sellers ([Article 20, paragraphs 1 and 3](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=G0400001&flno=20)). Paragraph 3 does not list holders.

For reports and documents, [Article 20, paragraph 2](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=G0400001&flno=20) prohibits misrepresentation or concealment in the issuer's financial reports and financial and business documents filed or publicly disclosed under the Act. [Article 20-1, paragraph 1](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=G0400001&flno=20-1) then identifies the claimants, including holders, and the defendants for the report-based claim. That distinction matters to the investor who has kept the shares.

## Audit, attestation, and quarterly review

The example concerns an annual financial report. Under [Article 36, paragraph 1, subparagraph 1](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=G0400001&flno=36), a company that has issued securities under the Act must announce and file it with the competent authority within three months after each fiscal year ends, unless that authority provides otherwise for special circumstances. The chairperson, managerial officer, and accounting officer must sign or seal it. A CPA audits and attests it; the board approves it, and the supervisors recognize it. A company with an audit committee is exempt from that supervisor-recognition requirement ([Article 14-5, paragraph 4](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=G0400001&flno=14-5)).

First-, second-, and third-quarter reports normally must be announced and filed within 45 days after the relevant quarter ends, subject to the same special-circumstances exception. The same three officers sign or seal them, a CPA reviews them, and they are reported to the board ([Article 36, paragraph 1, subparagraph 2](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=G0400001&flno=36)). Review is distinct from audit and attestation.

That distinction affects which CPA work is being discussed: [Article 20-1, paragraph 3](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=G0400001&flno=20-1) concerns attestation. [Article 14-5, paragraph 1, subparagraph 10](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=G0400001&flno=14-5) also lists second-quarter reports requiring a CPA audit and attestation among audit-committee matters. Articles 36 and 14-5 do not, on their own, identify every second-quarter report subject to that requirement.
