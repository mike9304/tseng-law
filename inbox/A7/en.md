# Leaving a Dormant Taiwan Subsidiary Alone: Ordered Dissolution, Revoked Registration and the Liquidation That Remains

Six months. That is how long a Taiwan company can stop trading on its own, without registering a suspension, before the authority may order it dissolved. Staff gone, no sales, a modest bank balance: a subsidiary in that state still exists under Taiwan's Company Act.

## The six-month trigger

[Article 10 of the Company Act](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=J0080001&flno=10) lists when the competent authority may order dissolution, on its own motion or at an interested party's request. The main cases are a company that has not started business six months after incorporation, and one that has stopped business on its own for six months or more after starting. A registered postponement or a registered suspension takes a company out of those two cases. Two more grounds sit in the same article. One is a company name barred by a final court judgment, where the company has not registered a new name within six months after the judgment became final and still has not done so by a deadline the authority then sets. The other is failing to submit the CPA-audited capital documents within the period set by [Article 7(1)](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=J0080001&flno=7), unless they reach the authority before it orders dissolution.

Suspension filings and the tax returns still due while a company is suspended are covered in [our column on closing a Taiwan company](/en/columns/withdraw-capital-taiwan-company).

## Dissolution is the start of liquidation

A dissolved company must be liquidated unless the dissolution came from a merger, division or bankruptcy ([Article 24](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=J0080001&flno=24)). For liquidation purposes it is treated as not yet dissolved ([Article 25](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=J0080001&flno=25)), and it may carry on business temporarily to wind up pending matters ([Article 26](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=J0080001&flno=26)).

Who does the work? That depends on the company type. In a company limited by shares, the directors are the liquidators, unless the Act or the articles say otherwise or the shareholders' meeting elects someone else; failing that, the court may appoint one on an interested party's application ([Article 322](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=J0080001&flno=322)). A limited company follows the unlimited-company rules through Article 113, so all shareholders are the liquidators by default, subject to similar exceptions in the Act, the articles or a shareholders' resolution ([Article 79](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=J0080001&flno=79)); if no liquidator can be determined, the court may appoint one on an interested party's application ([Article 81](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=J0080001&flno=81)). Where the subsidiary is a company limited by shares and the director default applies, a head-office executive who sits on the Taiwan board becomes a liquidator once the company is dissolved.

Liquidators are responsible persons of the company within the scope of that role ([Article 8(2)](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=J0080001&flno=8)). In a company limited by shares, liquidators must inspect the company's assets on taking office, prepare financial statements and an inventory, have them reviewed by the supervisors and approved by the shareholders' meeting, and then report to the court; obstructing or refusing the inspection carries a fine of NT$20,000 to NT$100,000 ([Article 326](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=J0080001&flno=326)). In a limited company, Article 87(1)–(2), as applied through Article 113, requires a balance sheet and inventory for the shareholders. Directing from abroad is not a shield either. A person who is not a director but in substance performs a director's functions, or controls the company's personnel, finances or business and directs the directors, bears the same civil, criminal and administrative liability as a director ([Article 8(3)](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=J0080001&flno=8)). The rule does not reach directions the government gives, in order to develop the economy, promote social stability or otherwise serve the public interest, to directors it has appointed.

The deadline is six months. A liquidator who cannot finish in time may apply to the court for an extension, giving reasons; otherwise each liquidator faces a fine of NT$10,000 to NT$50,000 ([Article 87(3)–(4)](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=J0080001&flno=87)). These rules apply to companies limited by shares through [Article 334](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=J0080001&flno=334), and limited companies follow the unlimited-company rules on dissolution and liquidation ([Article 113](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=J0080001&flno=113)).

What if the money is not there? When the company's assets cannot pay its debts, the liquidator must immediately petition for bankruptcy, and the liquidator's role ends on handing matters to the bankruptcy trustee. A liquidator who fails to petition faces a fine of NT$20,000 to NT$100,000 ([Article 89](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=J0080001&flno=89), applied to companies limited by shares by Article 334). For a dormant subsidiary with unpaid suppliers, tax or wages, that question can come up early, which is why the liquidator's first balance sheet matters.

## When nobody files the dissolution

If a dissolved company never registers its dissolution, the authority may revoke its registration, on its own motion or on an interested party's application ([Article 397](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=J0080001&flno=397)). Except for ordered or court-ruled dissolutions, the company's responsible person is first given 30 days to object. Revocation follows if no objection comes in within that time or the grounds offered are insufficient.

Revocation does not finish the liquidation. [Article 26-2](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=J0080001&flno=26-2) lets others apply to use the name of a company whose liquidation is still unfinished ten years after its dissolution, annulment or revocation was registered, which presupposes that an unfinished liquidation can outlast revocation. With good reason, the name can be protected by approval from the central authority sought within the six months before that period ends.

The formal route, with its tax deadlines and the steps before cash can leave Taiwan, is set out in [our column on liquidation tax filings](/en/columns/closing-taiwan-subsidiary-branch-liquidation-tax-filings) and [the one on when remaining cash can leave](/en/columns/taiwan-company-dissolution-liquidation). For a dormant subsidiary, three things are the starting points for working out which route is still open: the date trading stopped, the company type and who now sits on the board or holds the shares, and records of its assets and debts.
