# Filming in a Taiwan National Park: Fines of Up to NT$3,000, Who Receives Them, and How to Appeal

Cooking inside a parked vehicle at Wuling, the high pass on Hehuanshan, breaks Taroko National Park's rules even with a flameless electric appliance. The park headquarters said so on October 25, according to the [Central News Agency](https://www.cna.com.tw/news/asoc/202610250103.aspx): the parking lot is not a designated camping and cooking area, so cooking or staying overnight there does not comply, inside a vehicle or not.

The occasion was a complaint about a car-camping video that a production company shot at Wuling featuring a Korean cheerleader. After reviewing the footage, the headquarters said rock climbing shown in the video violated the National Park Act and would be penalized. Police on patrol had already asked the crew to stop cooking on October 4, and the crew complied. The report does not say whether the performer or the company will receive the decision.

For anyone shooting vanlife or travel content in Taiwan's mountains, the episode raises practical questions. How large can the fine be? Is there a permit to get? And what happens if the notice arrives after you've flown home?

## What the Act forbids, and the NT$3,000 ceiling

[Article 13 of the National Park Act](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=D0070105&flno=13) lists eight prohibited acts inside a park, from burning vegetation, hunting and polluting water or air to dumping refuse and driving outside permitted areas. Item 8 is a catch-all: other conduct prohibited by the national park authority, which is the Ministry of the Interior ([Article 3](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=D0070105&flno=3)). For people who leave open trails to get a shot, the headquarters said rangers and police first ask them to stop; repeated refusal, or actual pollution, leads straight to a citation under that item.

The fine sounds odd on paper. [Article 26](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=D0070105&flno=26) sets "not more than 1,000 yuan" for violations of items 4 through 8, an amount stated in an old currency unit. [Article 2 of the statute converting those units](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=G0380048&flno=2) multiplies it by three. So the cap is NT$3,000. Damage also has to be repaired, or paid for if repair is impossible or extremely difficult ([Article 27](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=D0070105&flno=27)).

## No filming clause, but permits still matter

Search the Act and its [Enforcement Rules](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawAll.aspx?pcode=D0070106) and you will not find the word for filming. The working rules sit in the Ministry's park-by-park prohibitions under Article 13(8). Point 6 of Taroko's list bars rock climbing, camping, cooking and operating remote-controlled devices outside designated areas without approval, so both scenes the headquarters objected to are on it. Rock climbing, camping and remote-controlled devices appear both in the June 2019 text reproduced in the headquarters' [drone application notes](https://ws.www.gov.tw/Download.ashx?u=LzAwMS9VcGxvYWQvMjk1L3JlbGZpbGUvNzcyMS8yMzIxMjYvMTk4NTlkZWMtYzgzYS00ZTY5LWI2YzAtNzg4MzFiNjFlZTQ0LnBkZg%3D%3D&n=55Sz6KuL5rWB56iLLnBkZg%3D%3D&ico+=..pdf) and in the [current text](https://www.nps.gov.tw/uploads/files/6eb768a982c10cb515e776baf881470a1b928a4796b0a4d1b190d980f963718b.pdf), amended by the Ministry of the Interior on September 5, 2025 and in force from that day. Cooking has a history of its own. The 2019 text said 野炊, cooking over an open fire on the ground, and an amendment of June 28, 2022 changed it to 炊煮, which the [2025 text](https://www.nps.gov.tw/uploads/files/719950e66f08894b99e70eca7c8a19c8bd5a6a28c6d70b4795f8ce86be48ef75.pdf) keeps. The [explanatory note](https://www.nps.gov.tw/uploads/files/05d566d7f402ca296a403c5ae2db3b9f.pdf) for that amendment says 炊煮 covers any heat source, from firewood to camping stoves.

Drones have their own procedure. Taroko National Park Headquarters takes advance applications for aerial filming, and the [government service page](https://www.gov.tw/News_Content_2_371377) asks for them at least 7 days before the shoot, with 7 days to process. The same page gives the earliest filing date both as 60 days and as three months ahead. Approval from the headquarters does not replace aviation rules: point 4 of the application notes says drone operators must also comply with the Civil Aviation Act (民用航空法) and other applicable law, and point 7 requires a separate park entry permit for controlled areas such as ecological protection areas, plus a mountain entry permit inside mountain restricted zones. Entering an ecological protection area requires the headquarters' approval under [Article 19](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=D0070105&flno=19), and [Article 14(10)](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=D0070105&flno=14) leaves room for other matters that need the authority's permission.

## Contesting a fine from overseas

Before imposing a fine, the agency should give the person a chance to state their side ([Administrative Penalty Act, Article 42](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=A0030210&flno=42)). It may skip that step when the facts are objectively clear. Footage of the act itself may be argued to meet that test.

A decision sent abroad is served through the foreign country's authorities or a Taiwan mission or similar body; failing that, by registered mail ([Administrative Procedure Act, Article 86](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=A0030055&flno=86)). The decision itself must state how to challenge it, the deadline and the receiving authority ([Article 96](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=A0030055&flno=96)).

You then have 30 days from the day after service to file an administrative appeal ([Administrative Appeal Act, Article 14](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=A0030020&flno=14)). Living in the United States counts as not residing where the appeal authority sits, so travel time is deducted ([Article 16](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=A0030020&flno=16)). For residents of the Americas, the Executive Yuan's [deduction rules](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=A0030119&flno=5) set it at 44 days. Appointing an appeal representative who lives there and can act within the period removes the deduction. The appeal goes through the agency that issued the fine, which, if it finds the appeal well founded, may cancel or change its own decision at that stage ([Article 58](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=A0030020&flno=58)).

After an unsuccessful appeal, a suit to set the decision aside goes to the administrative courts ([Administrative Litigation Act, Article 4](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=A0030154&flno=4)). Fines of NT$500,000 or less follow the summary procedure, with a district administrative court as the first instance ([Article 229](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=A0030154&flno=229)).

## Brand, agency or creator: who pays

Foreign nationals who breach an administrative duty inside Taiwan are subject to Taiwan's administrative penalties ([Administrative Penalty Act, Article 6](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=A0030210&flno=6)), and a penalty requires intent or negligence ([Article 7(1)](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=A0030210&flno=7)). Where the company itself is the violator, Article 7(2) presumes that the fault of its representative or of the staff who actually acted is the company's own. [Article 14](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=A0030210&flno=14) penalizes each person who intentionally takes part in a joint violation, according to their role.

Who bears the cost is a contract question for the brand, the agency and the creator. A clause can say the production company reimburses the fine and any restoration cost. It cannot change whose name appears on the decision. The amount itself reflects blameworthiness, impact and any gain from the violation ([Article 18(1)](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=A0030210&flno=18)); where the gain exceeds the statutory maximum, the fine may go above it up to that gain (Article 18(2)). Whether sponsorship or ad revenue from a video counts as gain from the violation is open to argument.
