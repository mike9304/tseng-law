# An overseas stablecoin on a Taiwan platform: the trading-approval route

[CNA reported on November 3](https://www.cna.com.tw/news/afe/202611030293.aspx) that Taiwan's proposed stablecoin issuer rules would initially focus on banks and financial-sector joint ventures. For a Taiwan platform considering an existing overseas stablecoin, a different question arises: which approval would allow its service to handle that token?

The answer remains part of a future framework. As of November 9, Taiwan's new virtual-asset services law has been promulgated but has not taken effect. Its [legislative history](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawHistory.aspx?pcode=G0400163) records promulgation in July, and [Article 56](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=G0400163&flno=56) leaves commencement to the Executive Yuan.

## The token's alternative approval route

Once operative, [Article 35](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=G0400163&flno=35) would generally require a stablecoin used in covered virtual-asset services to have issuance permission under Article 34 or regulatory approval for trading. The authority may provide otherwise. Trading approval is an alternative within this token condition, not proof that every aspect of the service is authorized.

Who would seek that trading approval? Article 35 provides for an application by the virtual-asset service provider. It delegates the procedure, conditions, withdrawal of approval and related matters to rules made by the regulator in consultation with the Central Bank. Those details matter to an overseas token issuer discussing access through a Taiwan operator.

By comparison, [Article 34](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=G0400163&flno=34) concerns permission to issue stablecoins in Taiwan. Under that provision, the issuer would apply, and the regulator would need Central Bank agreement before granting permission. An existing token's use abroad does not establish either Taiwan issuance permission or trading approval under the future framework.

## The operator still has its own licensing question

[Article 6](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=G0400163&flno=6) describes covered businesses as providing specified services for others in Taiwan. These include exchanges between virtual assets and currency, transfers, and custody or management of virtual assets or the tools controlling them. The actual service matters. Calling a project a “stablecoin partnership” leaves the operator's activities unclear.

Future [Article 7](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=G0400163&flno=7) would generally require service-provider permission and a license for the relevant business category before operations, subject to regulatory exceptions. A token's trading approval would not replace that authorization. Conversely, identifying a licensed operator would not, by itself, answer Article 35's separate question about the stablecoin it proposes to handle.

For an overseas issuer and a Taiwan service provider, the documents can identify the token, the entity providing the customer service and the activity that entity will perform. That allows a legal review to connect each proposed operation with the relevant authorization, instead of treating permission attached to one participant as covering the whole arrangement.

## The issuer consultation does not decide a token's status

The Financial Supervisory Commission's [November 6 notice](https://law.banking.gov.tw/chi/NewsContent.aspx?msgid=2949) concerns draft stablecoin issuance and management regulations. Consultation runs from November 7 through December 16. It does not establish trading approval for any overseas token, and the notice is not a commencement order for the law.

An overseas issuer's description of its token and the Taiwan operator's account of its proposed service need to be read together, even where a proposed partnership presents both under a single product name. The name is not an approval. Any claimed approval can then be matched to its holder, the token and its permitted scope. Until the operative rules and the relevant approvals are established, the November issuer proposal cannot settle whether that operator may offer that token through the proposed Taiwan service.
