# Your Face in Someone Else's Post or Ad in Taiwan: The Legal Grounds for Removal and Damages

"When a personality right is infringed, the injured person may ask the court to remove the infringement." That is Article 18(1) of Taiwan's [Civil Code](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=B0000001&flno=18), which also allows a request to prevent a threatened infringement. The term "portrait right" (肖像權) appears nowhere in the Code. Taking your face off a post or flyer starts here.

Paragraph 2 of the same article narrows things. Damages and compensation for emotional harm are available only where a statute specifically provides for them.

## Removal does not need fault; damages do

No intent or negligence is mentioned in Article 18(1). A poster who "didn't know permission was needed" still faces removal if infringement is established.

Money is harder. Article [184](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=B0000001&flno=184)(1) requires an intentional or negligent, unlawful infringement. For non-pecuniary loss, Article [195](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=B0000001&flno=195)(1) covers unlawful infringement of body, health, reputation, liberty, credit, privacy or chastity, or of "other personality interests" where "the circumstances are serious"; that condition attaches only to the second branch. Framing changes what must be proved.

Harm to reputation supports appropriate measures to restore it. In [Judgment 111-Hsien-Pan-2](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingleRela.aspx?PCODE=B0000001&FLNO=195&ty=CJ) of February 25, 2022, the Constitutional Court held that these exclude a judgment ordering an apology.

## Is a recognizable face personal data?

The Personal Data Protection Act, in Article [2](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=I0050021&flno=2)(1), lists name, birth date, ID number, physical characteristics and more, ending with "other data that can directly or indirectly identify the individual." A clear face can fall within that. A blurred figure in a crowd raises the identification question first.

"Non-government agencies" under the Act include natural persons, legal persons and other groups (Article 2(8)). Collection or processing needs a specific purpose and one of eight grounds in Article [19](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=I0050021&flno=19)(1), such as consent, a contract or self-published data. Use must stay within that purpose under Article [20](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=I0050021&flno=20)(1) unless an exception such as consent applies.

Shops often reply that you posted it publicly yourself. Article 19(1) does list self-published data (item 3) and generally available sources (item 7). Item 7 has a proviso for a person with a clearly more worthy, significant interest in prohibiting processing or use, and once notified of such a prohibition the collector must delete or stop (Article 19(2)). An ad is "use," so Article 20(1) still applies.

Breaches support deletion under Article [11](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=I0050021&flno=11)(4) and damages under Article [29](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=I0050021&flno=29)(1), which holds a non-government agency liable unless it proves no intent or negligence. That flips the burden in Civil Code Article 184. An individual's influencer or personal-shopping page is covered too, unless Article 51's personal exception applies.

How much? Article 29(2) applies Article [28](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=I0050021&flno=28)(2)–(6): non-pecuniary loss is compensable, and if actual damage is hard or impossible to prove, the court may set NT$500 to NT$20,000 per person per incident. Article [3](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=I0050021&flno=3) also bars waiving deletion and stop-use rights in advance or limiting them by contract.

## A friend's account versus a shop's ad

Two situations are excluded by Article [51](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=I0050021&flno=51)(1): a natural person acting purely for personal or household activities, and audio-visual data from public places or events not combined with other personal data.

Your friend's holiday photo on a personal account raises the first, sending you back to Civil Code Articles 18 and 195. A company is not a natural person, and a sole trader's promotion is hardly "purely" personal. For a street or event photo, the second turns on whether a name, employer or account link was posted with it. Judicial Yuan [Interpretation No. 689](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingleRela.aspx?PCODE=B0000001&FLNO=18&ty=C), on the Social Order Maintenance Act's stalking rule, recognized a reasonable expectation of freedom from intrusion in public spaces and control over personal data.

Picture a Taipei wedding studio posting a British couple's shoot on Facebook as an advert without asking (an invented example). It took their images to perform a photography contract. Whether advertising fits that purpose, and whether the contract has a consent clause, are Article 20(1) questions. The studio may own the copyright. The questions remain.

Their first message can be short: which post (URL and date), what they want (removal and no further use elsewhere), and by when. Damages can go in the same letter or follow a reply.

## Copyright usually belongs to whoever took the picture

Under the Copyright Act, the author is the person who creates the work (Article [3](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=J0070017&flno=3)(1)(2)); photographic works are listed in Article [5](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=J0070017&flno=5)(1)(5), and copyright arises on completion (Article [10](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=J0070017&flno=10)). Paying for a shoot does not change that: Article [12](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=J0070017&flno=12) makes the commissioned party the author by default, keeps unallocated economic rights with it, and lets the commissioning party use the work. Employee photos follow Article [11](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=J0070017&flno=11); see [Who Owns Code and Designs Made in Taiwan by Employees and Freelancers?](/en/columns/taiwan-copyright-employee-freelancer-work-ownership)

If someone takes a photo you shot, selfies included, you can assert reproduction (Article [22](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=J0070017&flno=22)) and public transmission (Article [26-1](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=J0070017&flno=26-1)) rights, seek removal (Article [84](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=J0070017&flno=84)) and damages (Article [88](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=J0070017&flno=88)). Joint infringers are jointly liable, so a shop and its ad agency can both be pursued. Hard-to-prove loss may be set at NT$10,000 to NT$1,000,000, up to NT$5,000,000 if intentional and serious. If someone else photographed you, the copyright is theirs and you rely on the Civil Code and data protection law.

## Where the criminal provisions reach

Criminal Code Article [315-1](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=C0000001&flno=315-1) punishes peeping at, or secretly recording, another's "non-public activities, speech, conversations or private body parts" without justification: up to three years, detention or a fine of up to NT$300,000. Distributing such secret recordings falls under Article [315-2](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=C0000001&flno=315-2)(3), punished as in paragraph 1: up to five years, detention, or a fine of up to NT$500,000, alone or combined.

Complaint deadlines differ. Article 315-1 is complaint-only; Article 315-2 is not on the list in Article [319](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=C0000001&flno=319). Complaints must be filed within six months of learning who the offender is ([Code of Criminal Procedure Article 237](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=C0010001&flno=237)(1)). A false caption may be defamation under Article [310](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=C0000001&flno=310): up to two years, detention or a fine of up to NT$30,000 when in writing or pictures, also complaint-only (Article [314](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=C0000001&flno=314)).

Personal Data Protection Act Article [41](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=I0050021&flno=41) punishes violating Article 19 or 20(1) with intent to gain unlawfully or harm another, where damage is likely: up to five years, plus an optional fine of up to NT$1,000,000. No complaint is needed (Article [45](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=I0050021&flno=45)). Article 41 was amended on November 11, 2025, effective on a date the Executive Yuan sets. As of November 3, 2026, the elements here read the same before and after.

Sexual images form a separate regime (Articles [319-1](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=C0000001&flno=319-1) and [319-3](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=C0000001&flno=319-3)).

## Evidence first, takedown second

Deleting a post deletes the evidence. Capture the URL, date and account name together; for an ad, the shop and product. Save the image file and the poster's profile page. For a LINE group, capture its name and member count. If evidence risks being lost, preservation can be sought before suing ([Code of Civil Procedure Article 368](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=B0010001&flno=368)(1); see [Preserving Evidence Before a Civil Suit in Taiwan](/en/columns/taiwan-civil-evidence-preservation-before-lawsuit)).

Platform reports follow platform rules and leave damages open. Direct demands commonly go by post-office certified letter (存證信函); an identical copy stays at the post office for three years ([Regulations Governing the Handling of Mail Article 34](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=K0050021&flno=34)).

Tort claims expire two years after learning of the damage and the liable person, or ten years after the act (Civil Code Article [197](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=B0000001&flno=197)(1)); data protection claims, two years from knowledge or five from the damage (Article [30](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=I0050021&flno=30)). A demand interrupts prescription (Civil Code Article [129](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=B0000001&flno=129)(1)(1)), but lapses without suit within six months (Article [130](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=B0000001&flno=130)).

Township and city mediation committees handle civil disputes and complaint-only criminal cases ([Township and County-Administered City Mediation Act Article 1](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=I0020003&flno=1)), free apart from inspection costs ([Article 23](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=I0020003&flno=23)). [Article 35](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=I0020003&flno=35)(2) applies the Act to district committees in cities such as Taipei, so "township or city" below reads as "district." If the parties live in different townships or cities, a civil case goes to the committee at the other side's domicile, residence, place of business, or office ([Article 13](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=I0020003&flno=13)). Court-approved civil settlements have the effect of a final judgment ([Article 27](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=I0020003&flno=27)(2)), and applying interrupts prescription like a lawsuit (Civil Code Article 129(2)(2)). If the application is withdrawn or dismissed, or mediation fails, prescription is treated as never interrupted ([Civil Code Article 133](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=B0000001&flno=133)).

Too urgent to wait? Code of Civil Procedure Article [538](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=B0010001&flno=538) allows an interim order to prevent serious harm or imminent danger in a disputed relationship a main action can settle; the court generally hears both sides first.
