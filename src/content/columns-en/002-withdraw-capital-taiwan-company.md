# Closing a Taiwan Company: What Happens to Capital and Company Assets?

![Closing a Taiwan company and identifying its company property and residual assets](../images/002-withdraw-capital-taiwan-company/featured-01.png)

Can the capital originally paid into a Taiwan company go straight back to a shareholder’s account when the owners decide to end the business? It is one of the first questions they ask. The answer starts with whose money it is. Once contributed funds enter the company’s account, they are company property, and company property belongs to the company, not to the shareholders personally. That holds even where one shareholder owns the entire company or is its sole director.

A shareholder therefore cannot freely withdraw company deposits or assets merely because that shareholder once contributed capital. Deposits held in the company’s name, accounts receivable, equipment, vehicles, real estate, security deposits, and intellectual property must all be handled within the company’s rights and obligations.

Stopping operations does not end the company. Its legal personality and filing obligations remain. For a permanent closure, dissolution registration and liquidation should be linked so that the company’s contracts, claims, debts, taxes, and residual assets can be settled.

![](../images/002-withdraw-capital-taiwan-company/img-01.png)

## 1. Company Assets and Shareholder Contributions

Capital is an equity item. It represents the amount shareholders paid when the company was incorporated or increased its capital. That figure does not always equal the current balance in the company’s account, and the single term “capital” cannot describe all the assets acquired and liabilities incurred in the course of business. At closing, the amount of capital recorded in the books is too narrow a focus. Actual property and liabilities, receivables and payables, taxes, contingent liabilities, and liquidation costs must be considered together.

Whether a shareholder’s contribution can be returned depends first on the legal nature of the transaction. A payment the company owes for goods or services, a dividend already lawfully declared, repayment of money a shareholder lent to the company, a capital reduction, and a distribution of residual assets after liquidation each have their own requirements. Renaming a transaction, or assigning it an arbitrary account in the books, does not change its character.

Article 9 of Taiwan’s Company Act carries a criminal penalty. It applies when share capital payable to a company was represented as fully paid although it had not actually been paid, or when, after registration, share capital was returned to shareholders or shareholders were permitted to recover it. The penalty falls on the company’s responsible persons (公司負責人): imprisonment for up to five years, detention, or a fine of between NT$500,000 and NT$2.5 million. That fine may also be imposed together with imprisonment or detention. The provision does not generally punish ordinary, lawful uses of company funds. Payments of rent, wages, amounts owed to suppliers, or taxes for actual business operations must be distinguished from falsely representing unpaid share capital as paid or returning share capital after registration. Still, even a transfer labeled a business expense may raise separate issues under company law, tax law, and accounting standards if its actual use, counterparty, consideration, or decision-making authority is unclear.

Creditors and taxes come first in liquidation. A shareholder’s recovery of an investment ranks after them. A liquidator who distributes company property to shareholders before paying the company’s debts faces imprisonment for up to one year, detention, or a fine of up to NT$60,000, which may likewise be imposed together with imprisonment or detention (Company Act, Article 90). Even when a shareholder asserts a loan claim against the company, the actual loan agreement, flow of funds, interest terms, accounting treatment, and order of repayment must be checked.

Other civil, criminal, and tax liabilities depend on the specific facts: the purpose of the transfer and the authority for it, the supporting evidence, its accounting treatment, and the relationship between the parties. For each transaction, the resolutions, contracts, tax calculations and filings, bank transaction records, and accounting books should be checked for consistency with one another.

In practice, two lists are a useful start. One separates assets held in the company’s name from assets held personally by shareholders. The other is a separate schedule of claims and debts between the company and its shareholders.

## 2. Procedure for Permanently Closing a Company

Dissolution is the legal procedure that ends a company’s ordinary business operations and moves it into the liquidation stage. Liquidation then settles the company’s remaining affairs and property relationships. Dissolution registration alone does not extinguish every debt. Nor does it automatically turn company property into shareholder property.

The steps below follow the general order. The actual filing authorities, documents, announcements or notices, tax treatment, and court reports should be confirmed based on the company type, cause of dissolution, and specific facts.

1. Review the company’s status before closing. Obtain the articles of incorporation, shareholder register, recent registration records, accounting books, financial statements, and tax returns. List the ongoing contracts, employees, leases, permits and licenses, assets, debts, guarantees, taxes, litigation and enforcement proceedings, and bank accounts. A company established with foreign-investment approval should also review its investment structure, the shareholders’ remittance routes, and the bank and foreign-exchange documents required to transfer funds abroad. Contract termination costs, the steps needed to settle employment relationships, restrictions on disposing of assets, and the possibility of enforcing security interests should be identified before the closing resolution. Without them, the resources available for liquidation cannot be calculated realistically.

2. Adopt the dissolution resolution that fits the company form. A limited company requires the approval of shareholders holding at least two-thirds of the voting rights (Company Act, Article 113). For a company limited by shares, Article 316 sets the general rule: shareholders representing at least two-thirds of all issued shares must attend, and the resolution must be approved by a majority of the voting rights represented by the attending shareholders. A company that has made a public offering of shares and does not meet that attendance requirement may adopt the resolution with shareholders representing a majority of all issued shares in attendance and at least two-thirds of the voting rights represented by the attending shareholders in favor. Higher requirements in the articles of incorporation, for the number of shares represented or for voting rights, must also be followed. The meeting notice, exercise of voting rights, preparation of minutes, and conflicts of interest should be checked separately.

3. Apply for dissolution change registration within the deadline. As a general rule, Article 4 of the Company Registration Regulations (公司登記辦法) requires a company to apply for change registration within 15 days after a registered matter changes. The dissolution change registration appropriate to the company form and cause of dissolution should therefore be prepared within 15 days after dissolution. Dissolution registration, closure of tax registration, business-tax procedures, and cancellation or surrender of permits and licenses may involve different responsible authorities and legal effects. One filing should not be treated as completing them all.

4. File the current-period final return as of dissolution. For profit-seeking enterprise income tax, this return must be filed within 45 days after the competent authority approves the dissolution. Official tax guidance counts the period from the day after the competent authority sends the approval document, that is, the day after its date of issuance. The filing period, the meaning of the approval date, and the method of calculating the deadline should be checked against the particular approval document and applicable rules. Before filing, the books should reflect revenue and expenses through the date of dissolution, gains or losses on asset disposals, accrued expenses, withholding, and taxes paid.

5. Determine the liquidator and complete the court and creditor procedures. After appointing a liquidator under the articles of incorporation or a shareholder resolution, or confirming the statutory liquidator, report the required matters to the court. The liquidator prepares an inventory of property and a balance sheet, concludes the company’s existing affairs, collects outstanding claims, and determines how assets will be preserved and realized. At the same time, the liquidator pays debts and taxes and completes the necessary notices, announcements, and creditor-protection procedures. Whether employment-related debts such as wages and severance pay, secured debts, tax debts, and general debts exist, and in what order they are treated, should be confirmed under the relevant laws and facts.

6. Determine the residual assets available for distribution. Cash recorded in the books is only part of the picture. The collectability of outstanding claims, asset-disposal costs, taxes, litigation risk, and liquidation costs must also be accounted for. Only residual assets remaining after all debts and taxes have been settled may be distributed to shareholders, under the applicable rules, the articles of incorporation, and ownership interests. Residual assets and paid-in capital are separate concepts. Shareholders should not assume that the amount they originally contributed will come back unchanged. Before distribution, also confirm the tax character of the distribution, allocation among shareholders, remittance documents for foreign shareholders, and foreign-exchange procedures.

7. Conclude the liquidation and make the final filings. File the liquidation-income return within 30 days after the liquidation ends, and make the required liquidation-completion report to the court. Revenue and expenses during the liquidation period, the results of asset disposals, taxes paid, payment of debts, and distribution of residual assets must be consistent with the books and supporting evidence. Also check the closing of bank accounts, retention of company seals and documents, statutory retention of tax and accounting books, and the final status of permits, licenses, and contracts.

Dissolution resulting from a merger, split-up, or bankruptcy may be exempt from ordinary liquidation procedures. Even a voluntary dissolution, conversely, may require additional procedures if there is unresolved litigation, long-term receivables, real estate, security, employees, unpaid taxes, or a complex foreign-investment structure. How long liquidation takes depends on these facts. Article 87(3) of the Company Act nonetheless requires the liquidator to complete it within six months. Article 334 applies that rule to companies limited by shares, and Article 113(2) applies it to limited companies. If that is not possible, the liquidator may state the reasons and apply to the court for an extension.

Whenever company assets are disposed of, before or after dissolution, the company should check the counterparty, the appropriateness of the price, any interests of directors or shareholders, the required internal approvals, and the tax consequences. A transfer of assets to a related party, or a waiver of claims, requires a separate review of its effect on the company and its creditors.

How the liquidator, creditor announcements, and court reports are handled after dissolution registration is covered in [Closing a Taiwan subsidiary or branch: when can the remaining cash leave?](/en/columns/taiwan-company-dissolution-liquidation).

## 3. When Liabilities Exceed Assets or the Company Cannot Pay Its Debts

Liquidation after dissolution is not reserved for companies whose assets exceed their liabilities. Under Article 89 of the Company Act, if the company’s property is insufficient to pay its debts, the liquidator must immediately apply for a declaration of bankruptcy. Whether ordinary liquidation can continue should be determined individually, after reviewing excess liabilities, inability to pay, security, tax debts, and the number of creditors. The central questions are whether company property is insufficient to pay the debts, what the payment dates and cash flow are, what security and priority claims exist, and when the liquidator learned of these circumstances. The actual records have to answer them.

If the company’s financial condition is unclear, the amount that shareholders may receive should not be calculated first. The latest financial statements are not enough. The review should also reflect debts incurred after the financial-statement date, guarantee obligations, litigation claims, employee-related amounts, the possibility of a tax audit, and the actual disposal value of assets.

Excess liabilities generally concern the company’s financial position, determined by comparing assets with liabilities. Inability to pay concerns whether debts can be paid when they become due. Even if the books show substantial assets, the company’s ability to pay may be assessed differently if those assets cannot be converted immediately into cash or are subject to security. Conversely, a temporary cash shortage alone does not necessarily mean that the same procedure applies in every case.

Once a liquidator learns that company property is insufficient to pay the company’s debts, the liquidator must consider the duties the Company Act imposes. Paying only a particular creditor or shareholder first at this stage may prejudice other creditors’ interests and procedural fairness.

Shareholders or management may intend to provide additional funds to the company or to restructure its debts. The method and effects must also be documented. A new loan, a capital increase, a debt waiver, and an agreement with creditors can each produce different accounting and tax consequences.

## 4. Capital Reduction While the Company Continues

Sometimes the business itself will continue, but its required capital has decreased or its capital structure needs to be adjusted. A capital reduction may then be considered as a lawful way to return part of the shareholders’ contributions while continuing the company. It is not always available. Nor is it an informal means for shareholders to take company deposits whenever they wish.

Under company law, a capital reduction is a procedure that changes the amount of the company’s capital. A bank transfer and a reduced capital account in the books do not complete it. The resolution appropriate to the company form, creditor protection, capital verification and accounting treatment, foreign-investment matters, taxes, remittance, and change registration must all be checked.

Where will the returned funds come from? That source should also be examined. Even a company with cash must be able to continue operating after paying wages, taxes, supplier invoices, loans, guarantees, and anticipated operating expenses. If the reduction would weaken creditor protection or make it difficult for the company to meet its obligations, the procedure and the directors’ responsibility for their judgment require closer review.

Companies with foreign shareholders should keep the investment approval or reported information, shareholder register, change in capital, foreign-exchange records, and bank remittance documents consistent. Remitting capital-reduction proceeds abroad does not end with the resolution. The company may need to prepare registration records, investment documents, tax materials, and an explanation of the nature of the funds, as the bank requires.

How shareholders are taxed on amounts received in a capital reduction is not determined merely by labeling them a return of contributed principal. The company’s capital composition, the method of capital reduction, the shareholder’s acquisition cost, the character of the amount distributed, and taxation in the shareholder’s country of residence should all be checked. Withholding, tax filings, foreign taxes paid, and the applicability of relevant agreements should also be reviewed according to the individual shareholder and transaction structure.

Ordinary business expenses, dividends, and repayment of a loan the company actually owes are each a separate transaction from a capital reduction.

An expense is supported by the contract, transaction details, tax documentation, and payment approval when the company actually receives necessary goods or services and pays reasonable consideration. The supplier may be a shareholder or director. In that case the need for the transaction, its price, approval of the conflict of interest, and the requirements for recognizing the expense should be checked additionally.

Dividends must also be distinguished from a post-liquidation distribution. A dividend requires distributable profits, financial information, and a resolution appropriate to the company form, and may be followed by payments to individual shareholders, withholding, and filings. Cash in the company’s account does not by itself establish distributable profits. Accumulated losses, statutory reserves, and unappropriated retained earnings should be checked.

For a loan a shareholder made to the company, the points to check are the date of the agreement, transfer of principal, interest, maturity, entries in the books, and actual use of the funds. Interest withholding and related-party transaction issues should be reviewed at the repayment stage. Retroactively relabeling a capital contribution as a loan should be avoided.

Funds may be paid to the same shareholder on the same day. Even then, the legal character and tax treatment of each transaction should be recorded separately. If the company is to continue, the board’s or shareholders’ decision-making records should show that the company will remain able to operate normally and pay its debts after the payment.

## 5. Business Suspension When the Company Will Not Close Immediately

Business suspension allows a company to stop operating for a period while retaining its legal personality. It may be used when the possibility of resuming business is under consideration, or when time is needed to settle contracts and assets. The company is not extinguished. Its existing rights and obligations are not all settled either.

A company suspending business for at least one month must apply for business-suspension registration before the suspension or within 15 days after the suspension begins. The exception, found in the proviso to Article 3, paragraph 1 of the Regulations Governing Company Registration, is a suspension that has already been reported to and recorded by the tax authority under the Business Tax Act. Each suspension period may not exceed one year. The suspension start date and planned end date should be set, and the company’s current status should be checked to determine whether separate company-registration and business-tax filings are required.

During suspension, changes to registered matters such as the business address, responsible person, articles of incorporation, or capital still require the necessary change registration. The company should maintain an address and a responsible person who can receive mail and agency notices, and follow the applicable procedures if its shareholders or officers change or its capital is altered.

Vehicles, buildings, or other assets held by the company may continue to generate separate expenses such as local taxes, management fees, and insurance premiums.

Before suspension, the company should decide whether to terminate or maintain its commercial contracts, handle employment relationships lawfully, and review the conditions and renewal deadlines for industry-specific permits and licenses. It should also designate a person to manage bank accounts and electronic tax records, and establish a system to retain accounting books and supporting evidence for the statutory period.

Suspension is no blanket exemption from tax filings. The company must file an annual income tax return for the year in which it suspends business. Other filing or payment obligations may remain, based on transactions before and after suspension, the holding or disposal of assets, withholding, employees, and the industry involved. Having no actual sales does not establish that the company has no filing obligation for a particular tax. Its registration status and required filings should be checked separately with the competent tax authority.

Before the suspension period ends, the company should decide whether to resume business, consider whether it qualifies for a further suspension, or move to permanent closure. A company that resumes should check the resumption registration and its tax, permit, and licensing status; one that closes should prepare for dissolution and liquidation based on its assets and liabilities at that time. When the intent is to close the company permanently, suspension is no substitute for dissolution and liquidation.
