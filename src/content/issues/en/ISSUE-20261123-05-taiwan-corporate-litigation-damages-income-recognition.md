# Taiwan corporate damages awards: which year's tax return includes the income?

An unnamed company omitted a civil damages award from its Taiwan tax return and was assessed additional tax and a penalty, [CNA reported on November 18](https://www.cna.com.tw/news/afe/202611180312.aspx). The Taipei National Taxation Bureau said the income belonged in the year the judgment establishing the damages claim became final.

During the annual close, the controller of a US-owned Taiwan subsidiary may receive a litigation update from local counsel while the payment record sits with treasury. A bank statement establishes a transfer date; the finality of the judgment helps determine the Taiwan reporting year under the bureau's explanation.

## A Taiwan company uses accrual accounting

[Article 22 of the Income Tax Act](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=G0340003&flno=22) requires company-form businesses to use accrual accounting. Its cash-basis option applies to non-company businesses under stated conditions involving existing practice or a small scope of operations. A foreign parent cannot create a cash-basis election for its Taiwan subsidiary simply by adopting that approach in an internal reporting policy.

Year-end recognition is addressed by [Article 27 of the profit-seeking-enterprise income-tax assessment rules](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=G0340051&flno=27). Except where cash accounting has been approved, income belonging to the year must be recorded at closing as accrued income, using estimated figures. Receipt is not the sole trigger.

Applying these provisions, the bureau's [November 18 release](https://www.mof.gov.tw/singlehtml/384fb3077bb349ea973e7fc6f13b6974?cntId=9461a282ac0345649b2cc722c0a383a1) says the income accrues when the judgment establishing the civil damages claim becomes final. Its warning covers damages sought for infringement of rights or breach of contract. It does not introduce a new November tax or a special rule for foreign shareholders.

## The bureau's example concerns an omitted reporting year

In the example, the company brought proceedings in 2019 and the judgment became final in 2023. Damages and interest exceeded NT$10 million. According to the bureau, the company should have included that income in its 2023 return but failed to do so. Additional tax exceeded NT$2 million. The release does not disclose the payment date.

An omission can affect more than the underlying tax. For a taxpayer that files a required return but omits or understates taxable income, [Article 110, paragraph 1](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=G0340003&flno=110) provides for a fine of up to twice the omitted tax. That is a ceiling. The rounded tax and penalty figures reported in this example do not establish a fixed penalty rate for other companies.

## An exception requires more than a later transfer

Article 27 also addresses income that cannot be ascertained at annual closing because of special circumstances. It permits that income to be treated as prior-period income in the year it becomes ascertainable. The condition matters.

Does a debtor's slow payment automatically satisfy it? The provision does not say so: its test concerns inability to ascertain the income at closing. A company proposing later recognition would need to explain the relevant circumstances, rather than rely only on the date money reached its account.

For a multinational finance team, the useful handoff is a record connecting the judgment's finality, the damages and interest it establishes, and the receivable recorded for that year. Payment information then shows how the receivable was collected. Where a proposed tax treatment depends on Article 27's exception, the remaining question is what could not be ascertained at closing and why.
