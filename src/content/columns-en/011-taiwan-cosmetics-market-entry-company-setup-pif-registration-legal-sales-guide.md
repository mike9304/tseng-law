# Taiwan Cosmetics Market Entry: From Company Setup to PIF Registration — A Legal Sales Guide

![Featured image](../images/011-taiwan-cosmetics-market-entry-company-setup-pif-registration-legal-sales-guide/featured-01.jpg)

﻿

Sophisticated Korean beauty brands highly value the spending power of the Taiwan market and are actively exploring entry into Taiwan.

I often receive questions from clients such as: “Counsel, if we just find a distributor or import the goods, can we start selling right away?”

My answer is usually: “It is not that simple!”

Below, I will explain in detail how to sell cosmetics lawfully in Taiwan.

![](../images/011-taiwan-cosmetics-market-entry-company-setup-pif-registration-legal-sales-guide/img-01.jpg)

**Step 1: Choose your Taiwan market-entry model — agent or direct operation**

First, the brand must decide how to “enter” Taiwan with its products. Common business models fall into the following two types.

**1. Appointing an agent/distributor (lower entry barrier; faster market entry)**

Advantages: No need to establish a company in Taiwan. Import customs clearance, product registration, warehousing/logistics, and local marketing are typically handled by the agent.

Disadvantages: Brand control is relatively weak. Pricing, brand image, and even sales strategy may be influenced by the partner.

※ Notes: Be sure to execute a comprehensive agency agreement that clearly defines the scope of trademark authorization, termination mechanisms, and related terms.

**2. Establishing a Taiwan subsidiary or branch (high control; long-term positioning)**

Advantages: Headquarters can directly control brand image and operating strategy, which is favorable for building brand value.

Responsibilities: A Taiwan subsidiary becomes the legal “product registrant” and “domestic responsible person.”

※ Notes: The process includes review by the Investment Commission of the Ministry of Economic Affairs and company formation procedures, which generally take about three months.

**Step 2: Product registration and PIF (Product Information File)**

Under the current Cosmetic Hygiene and Safety Act, before cosmetics are supplied, sold, publicly displayed, or provided to consumers as free samples/gifts, you must complete “**product registration**” in the Taiwan authorities’ system and prepare a PIF (Product Information File).

※ What is a PIF (Product Information File)?

A PIF is a set of technical and regulatory documents that prove a cosmetic product’s safety, regulatory compliance, and eligibility for market sale. Think of it as the product’s “ID card plus health checkup report.” It includes the full ingredient list, safety reports, manufacturer information, stability testing, and more.

Q1. Who registers?

It must be a local Taiwan “product registrant”—that is, your Taiwan agent/distributor, or the Taiwan subsidiary or branch you establish.

Q2. Where do you register?

Registration is done through the “Cosmetics Product Registration Platform” established by Taiwan’s Food and Drug Administration under the Ministry of Health and Welfare (TFDA).

Q3. How do you register?

TFDA has set up a dedicated “Cosmetic Product Information File (PIF) section” that centrally provides regulations, preparation guidelines, briefing materials, and more, which you can download and use directly.

Alternatively, Taiwan has specialized cosmetics testing firms that prepare and register PIFs on your behalf—feel free to consult a Taiwan lawyer.

※ Caution: A PIF is not finished once uploaded. You must properly retain it in-house for at least five years to prepare for random inspections by the authorities. If an inspection finds that materials are inaccurate or incomplete, the consequences can be severe—not only administrative fines, but also product delisting (recall) and similar measures.

**Step 3: Avoid marketing landmines — the red lines of advertising law**

The area where foreign brands most easily “pay tuition” is advertising copy.

In particular, cosmetics brands often collaborate with influencers to post product reviews.

If you do not carefully monitor the language influencers use, the company may be penalized—so extra caution is required.

Two major violation traps:

1. **Claims of medical efficacy (most serious):** Cosmetics functions are limited to moisturizing and beautifying. Anything related to “treatment” is regulated as a pharmaceutical. Therefore, you must not promote medical efficacy such as “treats acne,” “repairs cellular DNA,” or “anti-inflammatory and sterilizing.”
2. **False and exaggerated advertising:** Absolute claims lacking scientific support, such as “results in 7 days,” “strongest in history,” or “the only one.”

※ Caution: If exaggerated advertising is found, fines range from TWD 40,000 to TWD 200,000. Where medical efficacy is involved, fines may reach up to TWD 5,000,000, and continuous penalties may be imposed based on the number of violations.

The legal bases for this article include the Cosmetic Hygiene and Safety Act, the Regulations Governing Cosmetic Product Information Files, and the Criteria for Determining False, Exaggerated, or Medical-Efficacy Claims in Cosmetic Labeling, Promotion, and Advertising.
