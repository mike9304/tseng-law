# Asked to campaign in Taiwan? The line for foreign residents

[CNA reported on October 29, 2026](https://www.cna.com.tw/news/asoc/202610290025.aspx) that prosecutors questioned people in an alleged election-related dinner case. The allegations have not been proved. With [local elections set for November 28](https://www.cec.gov.tw/central/article/61722), a different question may land in an American resident's inbox: can a candidate ask you now to take the stage, post a campaign video, or help canvass in November?

Taiwan's election law names particular promotional acts and restricts the person who invites a foreign national to perform them. Residence and work rules ask further questions about what the foreign resident actually does.

## The rule is aimed at the invitation

[Article 56(4) of the Public Officials Election and Recall Act](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=D0020010&flno=56) bars a political party or anyone else from inviting a foreign national to do any of the acts in [Article 45](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=D0020010&flno=45). The clause itself contains no words limiting it to the official campaign period. Those acts include speaking publicly or signing an endorsement for a candidate, appearing on stage, promoting a candidate in a media interview or online, distributing campaign material, and taking part in a campaign march, canvass, or fundraiser. A social post written for a campaign can therefore be different from watching a rally.

Article 56(4) places this prohibition on the inviter, as the [Central Election Commission clarified in 2011](https://law.cec.gov.tw/LawContent.aspx?id=GL000143&media=print). It does not say a foreign resident is fined under that clause merely for receiving an invitation. [Article 110](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=D0020010&flno=110) sets the current administrative fine for an Article 56 violation according to the inviter's role: NT$200,000–2 million for a party, candidate, or specified connected person; NT$100,000–1 million for others. Who made the request, in what capacity, and what was requested are therefore material facts. A foreign resident may face separate immigration or work issues arising from participation.

Official campaign periods differ by office. [Article 40](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=D0020010&flno=40) sets 15 days for a special municipality mayor, 10 days for specified other offices, and five days for village or borough chiefs and specified representatives, counting back from the day before polling. The [Central Election Commission's timetable](https://www.cec.gov.tw/central/article/61722) fixes the polling day. In a [May 15, 2012 meeting decision](https://web.cec.gov.tw/api/file/544fb7ab-ee17-435c-9461-7f6b4c745381.pdf), the commission declined to penalize a candidate for inviting foreign nationals to appear at an event held before that office's official campaign period. The same meeting fined a candidate NT$500,000 under the penalty then in force after a Japanese legislator gave a short greeting from a campaign vehicle the day before polling. The 2012 amount is not the current penalty range. Together, the two decisions show why the office being contested, the event date, and the guest’s proposed role matter. Neither decision settles a request made now for an appearance during the coming campaign period.

## A spouse exception has edges

A narrow exception in Article 56(4) applies when the invited foreign national is the spouse of a candidate or person facing recall. It covers standing or appearing at a rally and joining a march or canvass without giving a supporting campaign speech. The exception does not say that the spouse may deliver a campaign speech, post promotional media at the campaign's invitation, or join a fundraiser. The relationship and the act both matter.

No spouse exception applies simply because a foreign resident has lived in Taiwan for years or has an ARC. A campaign cannot treat the card as permission to place that resident in a candidate video. A crowd photo proves little. It does not, by itself, show an invitation to campaign. Messages arranging the appearance, the planned role, and what happened on the day answer different parts of the question.

## Attendance, advocacy, and paid work

[Immigration Act Article 29](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=D0080132&flno=29) generally prohibits a foreign national from engaging in activity inconsistent with the permitted reason for stay or residence. For a legally resident person, it expressly excepts petitions and lawful assemblies. Attending a lawful rally as part of its audience therefore raises a different Article 29 question from taking an assigned promotional role. The election law still governs a campaign invitation.

A paid assignment adds employment law. [Employment Service Act Article 43](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=N0090001&flno=43) generally requires an employer-applied permit before a foreign national works in Taiwan, unless the Act provides otherwise. [Article 68](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=N0090001&flno=68) states the consequences of working in violation, including an order to leave. Residents may have different work authorization; the permit or exemption held by this resident determines whether the paid task is covered. Paying for a video or a canvassing shift neither removes Article 56(4) nor answers whether the worker is authorized.

At a lawful rally, a resident sits in the audience; later a campaign asks that resident to appear in an online ad (an invented example). The same resident has moved from attendance to a proposed promotional act. Whether the campaign actually invited and used the appearance still has to be established.

There is no single rule for every personal social-media post. Article 56(4) turns on an invitation and on an Article 45 act; Article 29 looks at the resident's permitted purpose and its lawful-assembly exception. Whether a post was requested by the campaign, made in an employment role, or simply expressed a personal view changes the analysis. The request and proposed content show whether “online support” involved an invitation to promote a candidate.

## The request before an event

An invitation message, a script, and a payment offer can show what the campaign wanted from a foreign resident. The office being contested and the scheduled date matter as well. If a complaint has already been made, the actual notice identifies the alleged conduct and the agency dealing with it; the October 29 dinner investigation is no finding about any foreign participant.
