# "You're Covered by the Responsibility System": Taiwan's Article 84-1 and Overtime Pay

In 2014 Taiwan's Constitutional Court called the filing requirement in Article 84-1 of the Labor Standards Act "a mandatory regulation." That phrase decides many arguments about the "responsibility system" (責任制), the label some employers in Taiwan use to explain why a salaried manager gets no overtime.

A foreign manager sent to a Taiwan subsidiary may be told exactly that. Article 84-1 does let some workers agree different hours, but only for a designated job whose actual duties fit the statutory definition, under a written agreement approved by the local authority. Even then, hours beyond the approved normal hours are paid as overtime.

## Which jobs and agreements Article 84-1 reaches

First comes the job. [Article 84-1](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=N0030001&flno=84-1) lets employers and workers set their own working hours, regular days off and leave only for categories the central authority has designated. The Ministry of Labor's list includes system development and maintenance engineers in information services who meet the Enforcement Rules' definition of responsibility-system professionals, lawyers employed by law firms, and supervisory staff paid at least NT$150,000 a month who meet the definition of supervisors. The job title on the list is not enough; the actual duties have to fit.

Supervisory staff are defined, not self-declared. Under [Article 50-1 of the Enforcement Rules](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=N0030002&flno=50-1), they are supervisors who run the business's operations and management and have decision-making power over the hiring, dismissal or working conditions of ordinary workers. A "manager" who does not run part of the business, or who has no decision-making power over hiring, dismissal or working conditions, is a long way from that definition. The same article describes responsibility-system professionals as workers who use specialized knowledge or skills to complete a defined task and answer for its success or failure.

Next comes a written agreement that takes the Act's standards as a reference and does not harm the worker's health and welfare (Article 84-1(2)). Last is filing that agreement with the local labor authority for approval and record, known as 核備.

## When the agreement was never approved

What if the third step was skipped? [Interpretation No. 726](https://cons.judicial.gov.tw/en/docdata.aspx?fid=100&id=310907) answered on November 21, 2014. An agreement that was never approved cannot displace Article 30 (normal hours), Article 32 (overtime), Article 36 (rest days), Article 37 (holidays) or Article 49 (night work by women). A signature on the agreement does not change that. In a civil dispute, the court adjusts the agreement under those articles and calculates pay under Articles 24 and 39. Article 49(1), the restriction on women's night work, has since lost effect: [Interpretation No. 807](https://cons.judicial.gov.tw/en/docdata.aspx?fid=100&id=310988) struck it down from August 20, 2021.

Before then, the two top courts had split. The Supreme Court, in 102年度台上字第1866號, treated an unapproved agreement as still valid; the Supreme Administrative Court, in 100年度判字第226號, required both the agreement and the approval. Interpretation No. 726 settled it by holding that the approval requirement reaches the civil effect of the agreement too. Its reasoning is practical: long hours affect health, the same kind of job can look very different from one place to another, and workers usually bargain from the weaker side, so a local authority should check each agreement.

The arithmetic then follows the ordinary rules. On an ordinary schedule, hours beyond eight a day or 40 a week are overtime. Where a designated industry has lawfully redistributed normal hours with union consent, or labor-management conference consent if there is no union (Articles 30(2)–(3) and 30-1), overtime starts after the redistributed hours instead ([Enforcement Rules Article 20-1](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=N0030002&flno=20-1)). Overtime is paid at an extra one-third of the hourly wage for the first two hours and two-thirds for the next two ([Article 24](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=N0030001&flno=24)). Rest-day work earns an extra one and one-third for the first two hours and one and two-thirds after that, and holiday work is paid double ([Article 39](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=N0030001&flno=39)). Overtime itself is capped: 12 hours a day in total and 46 overtime hours a month, or up to 54 a month (138 per three months) with union consent, or labor-management conference consent where there is no union, and an employer with 30 or more workers must report that extension to the local authority; with separate rules for natural disasters and sudden events ([Article 32](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=N0030001&flno=32)).

Records drive the claim. Employers must keep attendance records to the minute for five years and cannot refuse a worker's request for a copy ([Article 30](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=N0030001&flno=30), paragraphs 5 and 6). Breaches of the hours and overtime-pay rules carry fines of NT$20,000 to NT$1,000,000 ([Article 79](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=N0030001&flno=79)).

## Approved, and still paid

Approval buys flexibility, not free hours. The Ministry of Labor's guidance says Article 84-1 does not leave working hours entirely unregulated or allow overtime to go unpaid. Once a worker goes past the normal hours agreed and approved, Article 24 overtime pay applies. The agreement itself must still take the Act's standards as a reference and protect the worker's health, so an approved schedule is bounded by its own terms. The monthly minimum wage, NT$29,500 from January 1, 2026, assumes a 40-hour week. Where the approved normal hours are longer, the minimum rises in proportion.

US managers looking for a salaried-exempt box will not find one; [our column on field engineers](/en/columns/us-equipment-vendor-field-engineers-taiwan-labor-law) covers that gap from the employer's side. For the manager who was simply told "you're covered by the responsibility system," the open points are which item on the Ministry's list covers the job, whether the actual duties fit the Article 50-1 definition, and where the written agreement and the approval are. Even if all of them exist, hours past the approved normal hours are paid as overtime.
