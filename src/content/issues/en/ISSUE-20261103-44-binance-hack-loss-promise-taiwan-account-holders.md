# Binance says users will not bear the hack losses. What does that promise cover?

"Binance will absorb the financial impact of these stolen funds, not our users." The sentence comes from the exchange's [Recovery Update #3](https://www.binance.com/en/support/announcement/detail/1fdf1f61559c401da0435895ffcccb62), published on October 31. Attackers had drained its hot wallets the day before, in the early hours of October 30 Taiwan time.

Binance's [October 30 announcement](https://www.binance.com/en/support/announcement/detail/c5a8729a97ac486d963784d96c5642d7) put the total impact at about US$6.3 billion. Around US$3 billion of the affected assets, it said, had already been frozen on-chain. Binance promised to cover all losses through its Secure Asset Fund for Users (SAFU) and company funds, and paused withdrawals while it checked its systems.

For a foreigner living in Taiwan with assets on the platform, the promise settles one question. Others stay open. What, precisely, did the exchange commit to?

## Stolen funds, in the exchange's own words

The commitment is tied to "these stolen funds." In the same update Binance said it had replenished about 90 percent of the stolen assets, drawing on part of SAFU, part of its futures insurance fund and its corporate treasury. A running timeline kept by the Taiwanese outlet [BlockTempo](https://www.blocktempo.com/binance-hack-explainer-timeline-frozen-funds-compensation-deposits-withdrawals-resume/) records a November 1 notice raising that to about 95 percent; the remainder was mainly less liquid tokens.

Withdrawals came back in stages. The same timeline, last updated at 13:16 Taipei time on November 1, says a first phase covering 15 coins was scheduled for that afternoon. At 13:09 the public API still showed withdrawals closed. [CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/10/31/binance-hack-asset-coverage-withdrawals.html) then reported that bitcoin withdrawals resumed on November 1, followed by the Ethereum network and BNB Smart Chain.

What you see on screen is the platform's record of the account. Whether it represents a debt owed to the customer or a custody obligation depends on the contracting entity and the terms in force at the time. The theft hit the exchange's wallets. Whether it could reduce the customer's position would be a question for those terms; Binance's public position is that the exchange carries the cost.

Trading losses are a separate matter. The risk warning in the footer of Binance's website says that "Binance is not liable for any trading losses you may incur." How far liability is limited in a given case turns on the liability clauses of the account terms that applied at the time. Either way, a position closed out in the October 30 price drop is not a stolen fund as Binance described the promise.

## No claims form has been published

No public filing procedure exists. BlockTempo's timeline, last updated on November 1, said Binance had not announced any procedure for users to declare or register claims. It advised readers to treat any "claim registration" link as a scam.

A request for a seed phrase, a login code or an upfront fee to release a balance or speed up a withdrawal has no basis in anything Binance has published.

## The FBI finding and the frozen assets

Late on October 31, US time, the FBI said it had determined North Korea was responsible, CNBC reported. In Taiwan it was already the morning of November 1. According to [Anue](https://news.cnyes.com/news/id/6643217), the bureau said some of the stolen assets were being converted to ether and that further laundering was expected.

Recovering the stolen assets now involves Binance, law enforcement and the stablecoin issuers that froze tokens: Tether said it had frozen more than 1.5 billion USDT and Circle more than 800 million USDC, [CNBC reported on October 29](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/10/29/circle-tether-freeze-funds-impacted-by-potential-binance-security-issue.html). Under Binance's stated approach, customers do not have to wait for that recovery: the exchange says it has already replaced most of the assets from its own funds.

## A delayed withdrawal and the question of governing law

Say a delay did cost you money. Before any Taiwanese rule comes into play, the account terms usually name a governing law. [Article 20](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=B0000007&flno=20) of the Act Governing the Choice of Law in Civil Matters Involving Foreign Elements applies the law the parties chose. Only if there is no express choice, or the choice is invalid under the chosen law, does it turn to the law most closely connected with the contract. That law is presumed to be the law of the domicile, at the time of the act, of the party whose performance characterizes the contract. A choice made to evade Taiwan's mandatory or prohibitive rules does not displace them ([Article 7](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=B0000007&flno=7)), and a foreign law is not applied where the result would offend Taiwan's public order or good morals ([Article 8](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=B0000007&flno=8)). Whether either provision reaches these terms is not settled. Where to sue or arbitrate is a further question, covered in our [column on offshore exchanges that suspend withdrawals](/en/columns/taiwan-offshore-crypto-exchange-withdrawal-suspension-arbitration-insolvency).

If Taiwan's Civil Code governs, several provisions frame the analysis. For an obligation with no fixed time of performance, [Article 229](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=B0000001&flno=229) puts the debtor in delay only after the creditor, already entitled to demand performance, makes a demand that goes unmet. Delay runs from receipt of the demand, or from the end of any period the demand sets. If the terms allowed a suspension, a request sent during the pause may not yet have been an enforceable demand. [Article 231](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=B0000001&flno=231) allows damages caused by delay. A debtor in delay also bears losses from force majeure, unless it proves they would have happened even with timely performance. Yet [Article 230](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=B0000001&flno=230) removes liability for delay that results from circumstances not attributable to the debtor.

That is where the cause of the breach becomes important. Binance's first announcement said its initial investigation suggested a compromised laptop belonging to a DevOps engineer. Later updates, as BlockTempo recorded them, mentioned other points of entry, and the co-CEO referred to EIP-7702. No final cause has been published before the promised post-mortem. Under [Article 220](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=B0000001&flno=220), a debtor answers for its intentional or negligent acts; the degree of negligence varies with the nature of the matter, and is assessed more lightly where the matter brings the debtor no benefit. Unless the law or the contract provides otherwise, damages are limited to the loss suffered and the profits lost. Lost profits are those that could be expected in the ordinary course, or from plans, equipment or other special circumstances already in place ([Article 216](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=B0000001&flno=216)).

Exclusion clauses meet their own limits. [Article 222](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=B0000001&flno=222) bars excluding liability for intent or gross negligence in advance. [Article 247-1](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=B0000001&flno=247-1) voids terms in pre-drafted contracts that exempt or reduce the drafting party's liability when, in the circumstances, they are obviously unfair. The [Consumer Protection Act](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=J0170001&flno=12) voids standard terms that violate good faith and are obviously unfair to consumers, but it protects "consumers," defined in [Article 2](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=J0170001&flno=2) as people who transact, use goods or receive services for the purpose of consumption. Does trading crypto for investment fit that definition? The statute does not say.

## Records to keep this week

Start with balances from before October 30: a screenshot or export gives a fixed point. Binance said in Recovery Update #3 that it posted a reserve snapshot taken at 00:00 UTC on October 31, with a full zero-knowledge proof expected by November 3 (UTC). That proof shows whether your balance is counted among the liabilities in the snapshot. The pre-October 30 record is a separate reference.

Withdrawal requests made during the pause, with their timestamps, are a record of when you asked; whether each counted as a demand under Article 229 is a separate question. Note whether a time is shown in Taiwan time or UTC. A saved copy of the terms that applied when you opened the account, and of the current version, shows which company you contracted with and what it said about suspensions.
