# The Bank Approved Less Than Your Taiwan Home Contract Assumed: Cash, Cancellation or Forfeit?

A week after the bank's approval letter comes in below the planned loan, the seller's agent emails an American buyer with a request. It is for a promissory note covering the part of the handover payment the loan will not reach, made out to the seller and marked as barred from transfer by endorsement (an invented scenario). The buyer has not yet decided whether to find the cash or try to leave the deal.

Three sets of terms bear on that choice, and each answers a different question. The note comes from one clause of the Ministry of the Interior's terms for resale home (成屋) contracts. Whether the gap is owed in cash at all comes from another clause, and what the bank actually pays out depends on the papers signed at the bank.

## Where the note comes from

Point 7 of the Ministry's mandatory terms for standard-form contracts to buy an existing home (成屋買賣定型化契約應記載及不得記載事項, announced October 31, 2019, in force May 1, 2020) deals with the loan money itself. Before paying the tax-stage instalment (完稅款), the buyer goes to the bank on the notified date to open the loan account, confirm the loan terms in person (對保) and affix seals. The contract then picks one payment route.

On the first route the buyer signs a disbursement authorization (撥款委託書). It tells the bank to pay the approved loan into the seller's account, or first into a repayment account that clears the seller's existing mortgage, with the rest going to the seller once that mortgage is released. The second route has both sides collect the money together after notice. There, when paying the second instalment (備證款, paid once the seller's transfer documents are ready), the buyer gives the seller a promissory note (本票) equal to the tax-stage and handover payments, payable to the seller and marked as barred from transfer by endorsement, or equivalent security. The seller returns the note, or releases the security, on receiving the money.

If the contract follows the Ministry's wording, a request like the agent's rests on point 7(3). Where the amount in the authorization does not cover the handover payment (交屋款), the joint-collection rule applies to the difference with the necessary changes. On the authorization route, then, a buyer whose loan came in short may be asked for a note or other security for that difference.

## The note and the gap

Such a note backs the unpaid price. Under point 7(2), the seller may enforce it if the buyer does not pay that price as agreed and still refuses after a demand. What "as agreed" means once the loan has come in short is answered by point 6, and by whichever box the parties ticked under it.

Point 6 starts earlier in the deal. The buyer writes in a planned loan amount, and when paying the second instalment hands over the documents the loan needs, such as an authorization to pay off the seller's existing mortgage, and names the lending bank. Without a choice from the buyer, the seller may name one. A shortfall is then sorted by cause: attributable to neither party, to the seller, or to the buyer.

## Whose shortfall?

Lending is a deal between the buyer and the bank. Its size turns on the bank's valuation of the unit and its view of the borrower's income and debts, and nothing in the Ministry's terms tells a bank how much to lend. Nor does point 6 say which causes land in which group.

Is a valuation below the price the buyer's problem, the seller's, or nobody's? The terms leave it open. The buyer's own credit, or a loan the seller had promised to arrange, would pull toward other rows, and the bank's written reasons are where that argument starts.

One line in the model contract's signing notes leans the seller's way. Buyers are told to weigh their own credit or to arrange a loan limit with a bank in advance (signing notes, item 6(1)). A seller arguing that the gap belongs to the buyer can point to it.

## The boxes in point 6

In the Ministry's [model resale contract](https://www-ws.land.ntpc.gov.tw/001/Upload/403/relfile/9020/8257/7ec633f3-6f40-4384-8736-5cfccb987f42.pdf) (June 2023), point 6 reappears word for word as article 5, tick boxes included.

For a shortfall attributable to neither side, the boxes are: cash from the buyer, in one lump sum, when the loan is disbursed; a right for either party to cancel; or another arrangement. With the first box ticked, the gap is due in cash on disbursement day. With the second, either side may walk away instead.

A seller at fault faces boxes that favor the buyer. The buyer may cancel, and the seller then returns the price received, with late interest, within the number of days stated. Or the seller lets the buyer repay the gap in instalments on the same term and conditions as the loan the seller had promised. Or the gap carries the promised interest rate but is repaid monthly over a shorter term of at least seven years. The last box, as in the no-fault row, is a blank for another arrangement.

Buyer-caused shortfalls get one rule. Unless the seller agrees to instalments, the buyer pays the gap within the number of days written in, counted from the day the buyer receives notice and never fewer than ten bank business days. If the buyer misses that payment deadline, the seller may cancel.

Only the no-fault and seller-fault categories are menus to be marked in the contract. If no box in one of those categories is ticked, the printed wording does not name a default option. The buyer-fault category has no boxes; it sets a payment duty, subject to the seller agreeing to instalments.

## Once the authorization reaches the bank

Handing a signed authorization to the bank locks the loan. From then on the buyer may not revoke, cancel or change the loan agreement or the authorization, or ask the bank to delay or stop the payout. One exception survives: a material defect because what was confirmed in items 5 to 7 of the property condition statement is untrue. Those items cover radiation testing, chloride testing of the concrete, and whether an unnatural death occurred in the unit. The statement (annex 1) was amended by a [Ministry announcement of December 19, 2025](https://gazette.nat.gov.tw/egFront/detail.do?metaid=162390&log=detailLog), in force April 1, 2026, and the three items kept their numbers.

That lock reaches the loan, not the purchase. Whether the purchase can still be cancelled is decided by point 6 and the contract's own terms, before or after signing. A notice cancelling the purchase does not by itself halt the bank's payout; that turns on the exception above and on the loan and disbursement terms signed with the bank. Whether a foreign buyer may hold Taiwan real estate at all is covered in [our guide for foreign buyers](/en/columns/foreigners-buying-property-in-taiwan).

## Before a seller keeps paid money

Missing the point 6 deadline opens the way to cancellation. Keeping what has been paid takes more. Point 12 of the mandatory terms, article 11 of the model contract, adds late interest of 0.02% a day, simple, counted from the date a payment was due, once that payment is five days overdue.

Forfeiture starts at the one-month mark. If a payment or the interest on it is a month overdue, the seller must send a written demand, such as a certified letter (存證信函). Counting from the day after the demand arrives, more than seven days must pass without payment before the seller may cancel and keep the price paid as liquidated damages.

What the seller keeps is capped at 15% of the total price for the land and building (房地總價款). Beyond that the seller may claim no further damages. A court may also cut an excessive penalty to a reasonable amount ([Civil Code art. 252](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=B0000001&flno=252)); how Taiwan courts have used that power is covered in [our piece on penalty clause reductions](/en/columns/taiwan-contract-penalty-clause-reduction-civil-code-252-2026-judgments).

## Money that comes back

Cancellation unwinds the deal. Each side returns what it received, and money comes back with interest from the date it was received, unless a statute or the contract says otherwise ([Civil Code art. 259](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=B0000001&flno=259)(1)–(2)).

A deposit (定金, dingjin) is part of that sum. Article 3 of the model contract counts any deposit already received as part of the signing payment, and the signing notes give the reason (item 5(2)) by quoting [Civil Code art. 249](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=B0000001&flno=249)(1): when the contract is performed, the deposit is returned or treated as part of the payment. If performance becomes impossible, the same article sets defaults the parties can change by agreement. A payer at fault cannot reclaim it. A recipient at fault returns double. Where neither is at fault, it is returned.

Escrow is a third pocket. Some deals park the price in a third-party account under a price guarantee arrangement (履約保證), and neither the mandatory terms nor the model contract has a clause on it. Release after a cancellation follows the escrow agreement's own terms.

## When point 6 is not part of your deal

The mandatory terms bind through a particular route. They rest on [Consumer Protection Act art. 17](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=J0170001&flno=17), and paragraph 5 of that article makes announced mandatory terms part of a standard-form contract even when the business left them out. A standard-form contract is built from terms a business prepared in advance to sign the same kind of contract with many consumers ([art. 2](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=J0170001&flno=2)(7), (9)). Point 14 asks for the seller's company or trade name and registration number, which assumes a business seller.

Buying from a private owner through a broker is another matter. The signing notes describe the model contract as a reference for consumers, businesses and the general public (item 1). Its loan clause does not slip into a private contract on its own.

With no loan clause at all, the Civil Code fills in, and no article in it makes bank approval a condition of paying the price. A buyer who pays late can be given a reasonable deadline, and the seller may cancel once it passes ([Civil Code art. 254](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=B0000001&flno=254)). Any agreed penalty counts as the total compensation for non-performance unless the contract says otherwise ([art. 250](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=B0000001&flno=250)(2)).
