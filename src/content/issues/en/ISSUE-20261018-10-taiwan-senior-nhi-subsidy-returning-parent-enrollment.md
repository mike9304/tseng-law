# Taiwan's senior NHI subsidy vote: a returning parent's enrollment date

Taiwan's legislature passed an amendment concerning National Health Insurance (NHI) premiums for income-qualified seniors on October 16, 2026, [CNA reported](https://www.cna.com.tw/news/aipl/202610160144.aspx). A family arranging an older parent's move back from the United States still needs to know when that parent's NHI enrollment will begin.

For a parent who retains Republic of China nationality, household registration and insurance history determine which enrollment rule applies. An old NHI card does not show whether that parent's household registration or insurance enrollment remained in place during the years abroad. The legislative report does not establish any particular returnee's enrollment or entitlement to a premium subsidy.

## The two-year record and the six-month period

[Article 8 of the National Health Insurance Act](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=L0060001&flno=8) requires an ROC national to enroll if the person has Taiwan household registration and a record of NHI participation within the previous two years. It also provides a route for someone who has maintained Taiwan household registration continuously for the six months immediately before enrollment.

The conditions measure different things. The two-year test concerns insurance participation; it is not simply the time since the parent's last visit to Taiwan. The six-month test concerns continuous household registration, rather than six months counted from an airline ticket. A family reconstructing the dates from travel memories alone may therefore be working with the wrong timeline.

Article 8 also provides separate enrollment categories. These include a person who has Taiwan household registration and is employed by a business or institution, or otherwise has a regular employer. A nonworking retired parent's position cannot be inferred from the experience of a relative who returned to take a job. The article does not impose an identical six-month wait on every returning national.

The National Health Insurance Administration's [guidance on returning after an extended stay abroad](https://www.nhi.gov.tw/en/cp-50-f11a9-27-2.html) applies the same distinction. It explains that a person who reestablishes household registration and has the relevant recent insurance history can resume enrollment immediately. Where the recent insurance record is absent, the household-registration period becomes relevant. The guidance's starting point is the person's registration and insurance history, not their age or the reason for returning.

## A card kept abroad cannot settle current enrollment

Under [Article 13](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=L0060001&flno=13), a person who does not meet the qualifications in Article 8 or Article 9 is outside NHI coverage; if already enrolled, that person must be withdrawn. Keeping the physical card does not override that rule. This does not mean that every parent living in the United States has lost coverage. It means that possession of a card cannot answer whether a particular enrollment remains valid.

For an adult child arranging the move from abroad, an NHI enrollment history and the household-registration record can explain why the parent's situation differs from another returnee's. A withdrawal date, a later period of participation or the date registration was reestablished may change which part of the returnee guidance applies. The National Health Insurance Administration can address the enrollment date using those records, without the family having to infer coverage from a broad subsidy headline.

That date is the useful answer when a family is considering medical appointments around the return. Who pays a qualifying person's premiums is a different question from whether the parent is enrolled on the proposed appointment date. Neither question is resolved by the October 16 legislative report alone.
