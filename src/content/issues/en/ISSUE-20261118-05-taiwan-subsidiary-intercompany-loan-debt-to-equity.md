# Can a loan to your Taiwan subsidiary become share capital without a new wire?

US$9.449 billion. That is the investment Taiwan's Ministry of Economic Affairs approved for Micron Technology in its Taiwanese subsidiary. The ministry attributed much of the rise in approved foreign investment, a record US$20.899 billion for January to October, to that approval, [Focus Taiwan reported on November 16](https://focustaiwan.tw/business/202611160021). For a US group whose regional holding company has lent to a Taiwan subsidiary, the approval raises a practical question: can that loan become share capital without fresh cash?

[Reporting on the ministry's January–September approval figures](https://uanalyze.com.tw/articles/7526057183) described Micron Semiconductor Asia, a Singapore company, using about US$9.45 billion in monetary claims for a capital increase in Micron Memory Taiwan.

## The Company Act allows it, by board resolution

Cash is only one way to pay for shares. For a subsidiary organized as a company limited by shares, [Article 156 of the Company Act](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=J0080001&flno=156) lets a shareholder contribute monetary claims it holds against the company, as well as property or technical know-how the company needs. The board of directors resolves the amount credited.

The shares themselves come from a separate decision. [Article 266](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=J0080001&flno=266) requires new shares to be issued by a board resolution adopted with at least two-thirds of the directors present and a majority of those present. Two things are therefore decided at board level: the issue of new shares, and how much of the claim is offset against them.

## Foreign investment approval does not disappear

Holding shares in a Taiwan company is an investment under [Article 4 of the Act for Investment by Foreign Nationals](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=J0040002&flno=4). A foreign investor applies to the competent authority for approval with an investment plan, and applies again when that plan changes ([Article 8](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=J0040002&flno=8)). [Article 6](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=J0040002&flno=6) lists the accepted forms of contribution: cash; machinery, equipment or raw materials for own use; intellectual property and know-how; and other property the authority recognizes. Monetary claims are not named in that list; the verification rules discussed below deal with them expressly.

The January–September report identifies the investor as the Singapore company rather than the US parent. Under [Article 3](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=J0040002&flno=3), a foreign company's nationality follows the law under which it was formed.

Loans can also be investments in their own right. The same Article 4 treats a loan of one year or more to an invested company as investment, and the January–September reporting described Orsted's item, about US$1.25 billion, as an investment made by loan. Where an intercompany loan was itself approved as an investment, converting it into shares changes that investment, and Article 8 covers changes to an investment plan.

Once approved, the contribution must be made within the period set in the approval and reported to the authority ([Article 9](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=J0040002&flno=9)). Approval for any unimplemented portion is revoked when the approved period expires. An investor with a legitimate reason for more time must apply for an extension before that deadline, and the authority decides whether to approve it.

## Which number gets verified

After the contribution, the investor, or the Taiwan company or an agent, applies to have the investment amount verified. The Regulations for Verification of Investment by Overseas Compatriots and Foreign Nationals set a two-month window after the contribution is made ([Article 2](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=J0040014&flno=2)).

For cash, the verified figure depends on bank documents. Foreign currency sold for Taiwan dollars counts at the net amount after fees and other related costs; unconverted foreign currency is translated at the receiving bank's buying rate when it issues the transaction voucher. Claims are different. [Article 3](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=J0040014&flno=3), paragraph 1, item 8, counts a claim used as share capital at the amount as approved, the figure at the time the application was approved. The supporting documents under [Article 11](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=J0040014&flno=11) are brief: the application form and the invested company's certificate that the investment has been completed. The authority may still ask for a CPA capital verification report or other documents when it considers them necessary ([Article 13](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=J0040014&flno=13)).

No wire means no bank record to anchor the figure. The approved claim amount is the number that comes back at verification, alongside the board's offset resolution and the shares issued against it.
