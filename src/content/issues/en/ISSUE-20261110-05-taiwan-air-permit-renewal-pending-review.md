# Keeping a Taiwan plant operating while its air permit renewal is pending

Does a pending air-permit renewal let a Taiwan supplier continue production after the permit expires? [CNA reported on November 9](https://www.cna.com.tw/news/afe/202611090283.aspx) that proposed changes to the Air Pollution Control Act were scheduled for legislative negotiations the following day. For an overseas buyer relying on that supplier, the answer affects the production schedule as well as the permit file.

In its [November 9 release](https://www.ida.gov.tw/ctlr?PRO=information.rwdInformationNewsView&id=43431), the Ministry of Economic Affairs described a proposal that would limit continued operation during delayed agency review to two months after expiry. That was a proposed change. As of the morning of November 10, the law in force retains a conditional continuation rule. No two-month limit applies.

## The filing window and the reason for delay

[Article 30](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=O0020001&flno=30) requires a renewal application three to six months before the permit expires. Where a covered stationary source applies within that window but agency review prevents a decision before expiry, it may continue operating under the original permit's terms until the review is completed. Both conditions matter.

For a Taiwan subsidiary reporting to an overseas parent, an application receipt establishes a filing date. Correspondence about the review helps explain why the decision remains outstanding. A status update saying only “renewal pending” leaves those two questions unanswered. It also gives procurement staff little basis for deciding whether a supplier's anticipated output rests on an existing permission or an unresolved assumption.

The continuation provision preserves the original permitted scope. [Article 24](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=O0020001&flno=24) requires operating permits for stationary sources designated by the central authority and requires operation according to the issued permit. It does not place every Taiwan business in that category. For a covered production line, a plan to change equipment or expand activity needs its own permit analysis; continued review of the existing permit does not authorize whatever production the company proposes next.

## A late filing changes the position

Outside the renewal window, Article 30 requires the covered activity to stop from the expiry date if the authority has not yet made its decision. Filing before the final day therefore does not, by itself, preserve the same continuation protection as filing within the statutory window.

Where no renewal application is made before expiry at all, the consequence goes further: the permit loses effect from the expiry date, and continued activity requires a new permit application. Article 30 also directs the authority to reject a renewal before expiry when the documents fail the requirements or the deficiencies have not been corrected. Timely filing is not a guarantee of approval.

An interruption can affect more than next week's shipments. If a covered activity must stop, a customer's inventory plan may need to account for interrupted production even while the supplier is pursuing renewal. That commercial exposure depends on the supply arrangement; Article 30 itself does not decide delivery deadlines or remedies under the customer's contract.

For a customer reviewing its supply arrangements, the useful documents are the original permit, the dated application record and the authority's correspondence, including any request for corrections. Read together, they show which production activities the permit covers and whether the claimed continuation rests on the conditions in Article 30. A new delivery commitment still needs to fit the activity that the Taiwan site may lawfully perform.
