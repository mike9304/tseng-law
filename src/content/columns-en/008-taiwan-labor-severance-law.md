faq:
  - q: "In Taiwan, does an employee who resigns voluntarily receive severance pay?"
    a: "No. Unlike Korea, Taiwan imposes a severance-pay obligation only when the company dismisses (lays off) the employee. If the employee resigns voluntarily, the company does not need to pay severance."
  - q: "Does the company still have to pay severance in a disciplinary dismissal?"
    a: "No. If the employee commits an unlawful act, violates company rules, or is absent without justified reason for three or more consecutive days (Labor Standards Act Article 12), the company may terminate employment without prior notice and need not pay severance. By contrast, economic dismissal under Article 11 requires prior notice and payment of severance."
  - q: "How is Taiwan severance pay calculated?"
    a: "For each full year of service, the employer must pay 0.5 months of the employee’s average wage as severance, up to a maximum of six months’ wages."

# Taiwan Labor Law: Is Severance Pay Hard to Get in Taiwan??

![Featured image](../images/008-taiwan-labor-severance-law/featured-01.jpg)

Hello, I am Attorney Wei Tseng, a Taiwan lawyer.

Today I want to talk with you about severance pay in Taiwan.

![](../images/008-taiwan-labor-severance-law/img-01.jpg)

Many of you may already know this.

In Korea, when an employee leaves the company, the company must pay a retirement/severance allowance.

But Taiwan is different from Korea:

the company becomes obligated to pay severance only when the company side dismisses the employee.

If the employee resigns voluntarily, the company does not need to pay severance.

However, if the employee commits an unlawful act,

**violates company rules,**

**or fails to report to work without reason for three or more consecutive days,**

the company may dismiss the employee without paying severance.

I will summarize this in a simple table.

|  |  |  |  |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Type** | **Economic dismissal**  **資遣員工(經濟解僱)** | **Disciplinary dismissal**  **解僱員工(懲戒解僱)** | **Employee voluntary resignation**  **員工自請離職** |
| Meaning | When the employer needs personnel adjustments based on business conditions, the reason arises in the employer’s business sphere and is not the worker’s responsibility. Therefore the employer must observe the advance-notice period, pay severance, and otherwise balance the disadvantage to the worker. | When the worker commits an unlawful or improper act, the employer may terminate the labor contract immediately without prior notice and need not pay severance. This is one of the employer’s disciplinary powers. | The worker is free to end the contract at any time, but must observe the notice period based on length of service so the employer can arrange handover and find a replacement. |
| Conditions | Yes  (Taiwan Labor Standards Act Article 11) | Yes  (Taiwan Labor Standards Act Article 12) | None |
| Prior notice | Required | Not required | Required |
| Difficulty | Simple | Difficult | Simple |
| Whether the company must pay severance (資遣費) | Required | Not required | Not required |
|  | Taiwan Labor Standards Act Article 11 (勞動基準法第11條): Unless one of the following situations arises, an employer may not terminate a labor contract even with prior notice to the worker.  1. Where the employer’s business is suspended or transferred  2. Where the employer’s business suffers operating losses or business contraction  3. Where force majeure requires suspension of business for one month or more  4. Where the nature of the business changes such that workforce reduction is necessary and the terminated employee cannot be reassigned to another suitable position  5. Where a particular worker is clearly unable to perform satisfactorily the work required of the position | Taiwan Labor Standards Act Article 12 (勞動基準法第12條): An employer may dismiss a worker without prior notice if the worker falls under any of the following.  1. Misrepresenting facts at the time of concluding the labor contract so as to mislead the employer and create a risk of harm to the business  2. Committing violence or serious insult against the employer, the employer’s family members, the employer’s agent, or other coworkers  3. Being sentenced to imprisonment or a more severe penalty that has become final, without a suspended sentence or without being permitted to commute the sentence to a fine  4. Seriously violating the labor contract or work rules  5. Intentionally wasting machinery, tools, raw materials, products, or other items owned by the employer, or intentionally leaking the employer’s technical or business secrets, thereby causing damage to the employer  6. Being absent without justified reason for three consecutive days, or being absent without justified reason for six or more days in one month |  |

> In Taiwan, for each full year of an employee’s service,
> the employer must pay 0.5 months of average wages as severance.
> (Up to a maximum of six months’ wages)

When an employee’s pay is high and the employee has a certain length of service,

severance becomes relatively large as well.

Some unscrupulous Taiwan companies then use various means

to avoid paying high severance

and push the employee to resign voluntarily.

Employees should be careful

and not fall for the company’s scheme.

Let me describe one case I handled.

There is a well-known Company A with branches across Taiwan.

For some reason the operator changed,

and as soon as the new operator took office,

in order to cut personnel costs,

the company began calling in high-salary, long-tenured employees one by one for interviews.

My client, Mr. B, had worked at Company A for more than ten years

and was the top-performing salesperson in central Taiwan.

Because his salary and bonuses were very high,

the company targeted Mr. B.

One day, a senior company executive called B in for an interview

and suddenly began criticizing how unfaithfully B was working.

Saying the company was in difficulty,

that B should sacrifice and set an example,

the executive demanded that B accept a lower salary and rank.

Otherwise B would have to leave the company,

and the executive handed B an A4 sheet

and ordered him to write: “I, OOO, voluntarily accept a lower position and salary.”

The executive also said this agreement would be shown to all other employees

so they would follow suit.

B was the top performer in the central region,

had earned a great deal of money for the company,

and was working diligently every day,

so he was very resentful of this unreasonable demand.

B refused to write that content,

and ultimately the company executive became angry and told B to leave.

Feeling wronged and sad,

B left the company he had worked at for ten years

and did not report to work thereafter.

Using the same method, the company

lowered the pay of many senior employees,

and some employees could not endure this treatment

and resigned voluntarily.

But B had realized early on that after the change of operator

the company was trying to force employees out by improper means,

and because B had discovered that recruitment ads were being posted even before employees left,

on the day B entered the interview

B recorded the entire process on a mobile phone

and could prove that B had not resigned voluntarily.

With a lawyer’s help afterward,

B was able to receive a substantial severance payment,

but other employees who left were not so fortunate.

Because such cases are common,

when the company uses improper methods

you must preserve evidence.

When the company seizes on small mistakes by the employee,

sets unreachable targets,

makes unreasonable demands,

or reassigns the employee to a strange position,

**you must preserve evidence.**

**Your normal attendance records,**

**overtime records, performance records,**

**company rules, email records with colleagues and supervisors,**

**and recordings of conversations with supervisors**

**should be kept as evidence favorable to you.**

If you work in Taiwan,

severance is harder to obtain than you might think.

Do not compromise easily with the company,

and be careful so the company does not wrongfully dismiss you

or force you into voluntary resignation.

Severance is an employee’s legal right,

and the company should of course pay it.

You too should protect your rights in Taiwan.
