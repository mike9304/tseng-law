# Handing Over a Taiwan Bank Account: Where the Crime Starts, and When Scam Victims Can Sue

One bank account, handed over for a fee that was only promised, can be enough to trigger the criminal provision of Taiwan's Money Laundering Control Act. The fee never has to arrive.

Take three illustrative requests, none from a real case. A remote "payroll" job wants the passbook and card. A loan agent says the account needs some transaction history before approval. Someone offers cash for the account you will stop using once you leave Taiwan. Each raises two separate questions under Taiwan law: was handing over the account an offence, and does the holder owe the scam victims money?

## Where the criminal line sits

[Article 22(1) of the Money Laundering Control Act](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=G0380131&flno=22) bars anyone from handing over, or letting others use, a bank account or an account with a virtual-asset or third-party payment provider. There are three exceptions: the arrangement fits ordinary commercial or financial practice, it rests on trust between relatives or friends, or there is some other legitimate reason.

A breach on its own leads to an administrative admonition (告誡) from the police under paragraph 2. Paragraph 3 turns it into a crime in three situations. Payment was agreed or received. The accounts handed over total three or more. Or the person breaches the rule again within five years of an admonition. The penalty is up to three years' imprisonment, short-term detention, or a fine of up to NT$1 million, and a prison term can come with a fine.

Picture an English teacher who agrees to sell an account for NT$5,000 a week before flying home (an invented example). The buyer never pays. The agreement itself can still meet the "payment agreed" condition. One account is enough.

An admonition follows the person to the bank, too. Under [Article 4](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=I0030062&flno=4) and [Article 6](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=I0030062&flno=6) of the regulation implementing Article 22(6), the restrictions run for five years from the admonition date. ATM transfers and ATM withdrawals are each capped at NT$10,000 a day per account. Payments and transfers through online, mobile or telephone banking, or through an account linked to a payment platform, are barred. Paying utility bills, taxes, criminal or administrative fines, and late charges is exempt.

Where the account was actually used in a fraud, prosecutors can also look at aiding fraud and aiding money laundering ([Criminal Code Art. 30](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=C0000001&flno=30), [Art. 339](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=C0000001&flno=339); [Money Laundering Control Act Art. 19](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=G0380131&flno=19)). Intent covers more than certainty. [Criminal Code Article 13(2)](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=C0000001&flno=13) treats foreseeing that a crime may happen, and accepting that it might, as intent.

## A civil claim that outlives a non-prosecution

When the scammer's identity remains unknown, the receiving account may identify a potential defendant: its holder. [Civil Code Article 184(1)](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=B0000001&flno=184) makes people liable for negligent harm as well as intentional harm, and [Article 185](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=B0000001&flno=185) treats anyone who aids a tort as a joint tortfeasor.

Carelessness alone does not decide the case. The holder must have helped intentionally or negligently, and that help must have an adequate causal link to the victim's loss; both judgments below work through that test. Once it is met, joint liability lets the victim claim the whole loss caused by that tort from the account holder alone ([Civil Code Art. 273(1)](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=B0000001&flno=273)).

On September 30, 2026, the Taipei District Court applied that test on appeal ([114 Jian-Shang No. 212](https://judgment.judicial.gov.tw/FJUD/printData.aspx?id=TPDV%2C114%2C%E7%B0%A1%E4%B8%8A%2C212%2C20260930%2C1)). The account holder said they were 21 at the time and had been tricked while trying to get a loan. Prosecutors had declined to charge, and the first-instance court had rejected the victim's claim. The appeal court reversed. In its view, ordinary people owe a duty to keep their account details safe and not hand them out casually; the non-prosecution had settled only whether there was direct or indirect intent, and civil negligence is measured by a different standard.

What made it negligent? The court pointed to the instructions: claim to be the son of a man you have never met, withdraw the money the day it lands, return it in cash rather than by transfer. None of that fits ordinary business. The holder withdrew the money and passed it on. Providing the account and handling the cash, the court found, was negligent assistance with an adequate causal link to the victim's loss.

The award was NT$150,000 plus interest, with no reduction. A victim who failed to see through a deliberate fraud was not contributorily negligent, the court held ([Civil Code Art. 217](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=B0000001&flno=217)), and nothing showed that paying would seriously affect the holder's livelihood ([Art. 218](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=B0000001&flno=218)).

## A romance scam that came out the other way

Two months earlier, on July 29, 2026, the Hualien Branch of the Taiwan High Court dismissed a victim's appeal over NT$620,000 ([115 Shang-Yi No. 12](https://judgment.judicial.gov.tw/FJUD/printData.aspx?id=HLHV%2C115%2C%E4%B8%8A%E6%98%93%2C12%2C20260729%2C1)). Here the holder had met someone online. They called each other husband and wife, and the partner asked to use the account for business. The court doubted that anyone in a relationship weighs every request rationally.

Two more facts mattered. This was an everyday account that paid a car loan and a phone bill. And about NT$17,000 of the holder's own money had already been moved out of it, four days before the victim's first transfer.

Both courts were asking the same thing. At the moment the account changed hands, would a reasonably careful person have seen that it could be used for crime?

## Deadlines, and claims inside a criminal case

A victim's claim lapses two years after learning of the damage and who is liable, and in any event ten years after the act ([Civil Code Art. 197(1)](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=B0000001&flno=197)). [Article 487 of the Code of Criminal Procedure](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=C0010001&flno=487) opens an incidental civil action to a person harmed by the crime, filed inside the criminal case against the defendant and anyone liable under the Civil Code. The holder need not be the one on trial, but the loss must arise from the offence charged in that case. The window is set by [Art. 488](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=C0010001&flno=488). An acquittal ends that incidental action with a dismissal, though on the victim's request it is transferred to the civil division of the competent court ([Art. 503(1)](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=C0010001&flno=503)).

Once a passbook, card, PIN or online-banking login has gone out, the bank can be told exactly what was handed over and when, and asked about stopping the account. Withdrawing money that arrives afterwards and handing it over in cash, as instructed, is the very conduct the Taipei court held against the holder. The messages from the day the account changed hands are what a prosecutor reads for intent and what a civil judge reads for negligence.
