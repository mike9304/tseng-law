# Paying for a Parent's Funeral From Their Taiwan Bank Account: Forgery Risk, Renunciation and the Debt Cap

Can the family simply use a parent's passbook and seal to cover the funeral while the estate gets sorted out? Taiwan's Supreme Court answered that question for one family in May 2023, and the answer was a criminal conviction ([112 Tai-Shang No. 1703](https://judgment.judicial.gov.tw/FJUD/printData.aspx?id=TPSM%2C112%2C%E5%8F%B0%E4%B8%8A%2C1703%2C20230504%2C1)).

The father died in July 2016. His son and the son's wife then stamped the father's seal on post office withdrawal slips and took out NT$245,000 in two withdrawals. The son had kept a tally of medical and funeral expenses. The appeal court still convicted him of using forged private documents and imposed five months in total. The Supreme Court dismissed his appeal on May 4, 2023.

## Authority ends at death

Its reasoning is short. Authority a person gives during their lifetime, including authority over a bank account, ends when they die, and it makes no difference that the person holding it is an heir. After the death, deposits can be withdrawn only with the consent of all the heirs and in all their names. Spending the money on medical or funeral bills bears on whether there was intent to take property unlawfully. It does not undo the forgery. Nor did the other heirs' silence count as consent.

Taiwan's Civil Code points the same way. Heirs take over the deceased's property rights and obligations from the moment of death ([Article 1148](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=B0000001&flno=1148)), and where there are several heirs, they hold the whole estate in common until it is partitioned ([Article 1151](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=B0000001&flno=1151)). The money in the account is part of that common estate.

## A seal means forgery; a card means the ATM offence

Stamping a deceased person's seal on a withdrawal slip engages [Criminal Code Articles 210](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=C0000001&flno=210) and [216](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=C0000001&flno=216). On June 29, 2026, the Taoyuan District Court sentenced a father to five months, suspended, for moving NT$6 million from his late son's account into his own with the son's seal ([114 Su No. 2074](https://judgment.judicial.gov.tw/FJUD/printData.aspx?id=TYDM%2C114%2C%E8%A8%B4%2C2074%2C20260629%2C1)). The heirs were the son's two young boys. The court mentioned a narrow exception for matters of real significance to the deceased, such as funeral costs, but found nothing necessary or urgent about a NT$6 million transfer when the estate could easily cover those bills.

A card is no safer. On July 23, 2026, the Taipei District Court convicted a person who had lived with the deceased of withdrawing the deceased's savings from ATMs with the deceased's card after the death. The offence was [Criminal Code Article 339-2](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=C0000001&flno=339-2), obtaining property from an automated payment machine by improper means. The sentence was five months, with NT$373,608 confiscated ([114 Yi No. 1349](https://judgment.judicial.gov.tw/FJUD/printData.aspx?id=TPDM%2C114%2C%E6%98%93%2C1349%2C20260723%2C1)).

## If renunciation is on the table

Heirs facing debts larger than the estate may consider renouncing. That has to be done in writing to the court within three months of learning that you can inherit ([Civil Code Article 1174](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=B0000001&flno=1174)). It reaches back to the moment succession opened ([Article 1175](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=B0000001&flno=1175)). Someone who renounces is treated as never having been an heir.

That is what happened in a New Taipei District Court case decided on June 3, 2026 ([115 Shen-Jian No. 523](https://judgment.judicial.gov.tw/FJUD/printData.aspx?id=PCDM%2C115%2C%E5%AF%A9%E7%B0%A1%2C523%2C20260603%2C1)). Two of the deceased's children renounced, leaving a daughter as the only heir. She filed the complaint against the deceased's former spouse, who had used the deceased's seal to withdraw money. The sentence was six months in total, suspended.

## Staying an heir, and keeping the cap

An heir who does not renounce is, as a rule, liable for the deceased's debts only up to what they inherit ([Civil Code Article 1148(2)](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=B0000001&flno=1148)). That protection can be lost. [Article 1163](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=B0000001&flno=1163) removes it for serious concealment of estate assets, serious false entries in the estate inventory, or disposing of estate assets with intent to defraud the deceased's creditors.

On May 26, 2026, the Taichung District Court applied the third ground ([115 Jia-Sheng No. 28](https://judgment.judicial.gov.tw/FJUD/printData.aspx?id=TCDU%2C115%2C%E5%AE%B6%E8%81%B2%2C28%2C20260526%2C1)). The heir had been served a payment order for the deceased's loan, sold the inherited property anyway, and did not use the proceeds to pay. The same ruling also said that failing to file an estate inventory with the court does not, on its own, cost an heir the cap.

## The route banks expect

In the Taoyuan judgment's account, heirs claiming a deceased depositor's savings normally present a death certificate, household registration records, the estate tax clearance certificate, a chart of heirs, the heirs' seal certificates and an application. If one heir collects for everyone, a power of attorney signed by all heirs, or renunciation documents, comes too. The list is long.

A family abroad may be tempted to ask a relative in Taiwan to "just take the money out" for the funeral. The people convicted in these cases were not only heirs: they included a partner who lived with the deceased, the deceased's father and a former spouse.
