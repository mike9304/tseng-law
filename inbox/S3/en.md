# Years Apart, Half Still? When a Taiwan Court Adjusts the Split of Marital Property

Three years and four months. That was how long one Taipei marriage lasted before the husband died, and his heirs told the court it was too short to justify giving his widow an equal share of the couple's surplus property. The court disagreed.

Arguments like that are now built into the statute. Since 2021, Taiwan's Civil Code has spelled out what a judge must weigh before cutting or waiving a spouse's half, and one of the listed factors is how long the couple lived apart. Can years spent in different countries reduce a spouse's claim?

## Half is the rule

When the statutory marital property regime ends, each spouse's remaining post-marital property is calculated after deducting debts incurred during the marriage, and the difference is shared equally. Inheritances, gifts and solatium are left out ([Civil Code Article 1030-1(1)](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=B0000001&flno=1030-1)).

Courts could adjust the split before 2021 too. The amendment of January 20, 2021 rewrote the standard in paragraph 2 and added paragraph 3. If a spouse's lack of contribution to or cooperation in married life, or other circumstances, makes equal division unfair, the court may adjust or waive that spouse's share (paragraph 2). Paragraph 3 lists the factors: household labor, raising the children, overall contribution to the family, the length of time living together or apart, when the property was acquired, and each spouse's economic ability.

An American engineer spends a decade working in Texas while his Taiwanese wife raises their children in Hsinchu (an invented example). If his net post-marital surplus turns out larger than hers, she can claim half the difference between the two; the question is whether the decade apart lets a court cut that share. Paragraph 3 lists the length of separation without specifying its reasons, and it also requires the court to weigh overall contribution to the family and the other listed factors. What each spouse carried during those years, and when the assets were bought, can still change the answer. All of this assumes Taiwan's statutory regime governs the couple at all; that question is covered in [our column on dividing marital property with an American spouse](/en/columns/taiwan-marital-property-division-us-assets).

## The Supreme Court sends one back

In [Supreme Court 111年度台上字第2561號](https://judgment.judicial.gov.tw/FJUD/data.aspx?ty=JD&id=TPSV%2c111%2c%e5%8f%b0%e4%b8%8a%2c2561%2c20221125%2c1) (November 25, 2022), the couple had slept in separate rooms of the same home since 2012. The spouse claiming a share admitted to paying no household expenses from that year on. Their three adult children testified that the other parent paid most of the food, cram-school and school-activity costs. The High Court still ordered an equal split, reasoning that nobody had proved idleness or habitual waste.

That was too quick, the Supreme Court held. It described the claim as a way to protect the economically weaker spouse by recognizing that spouse's contribution, and said the amendment was meant to stop courts applying inconsistent standards of unfairness. The divorce became final after the 2021 amendment, so the amended article applied. The lower court had not looked into whether the claimant's spending went to the family, why the claimant's debts arose, or how much the claimant contributed to housework and child care. The money award was set aside and the case sent back.

On remand, the Taiwan High Court gave the claimant 30% of the difference instead of half ([111年度家上更一字第22號](https://judgment.judicial.gov.tw/FJUD/data.aspx?ty=JD&id=TPHV%2c111%2c%e5%ae%b6%e4%b8%8a%e6%9b%b4%e4%b8%80%2c22%2c20230705%2c1), July 5, 2023). After the move into separate rooms in 2012, it found, the claimant had paid only for basics such as cable TV, health insurance premiums and part of the children's student loans, and had taken little part in housework, so the claimant's contribution to the growth in assets over that period was low. The court also weighed how long that period was: about seven of the roughly 25 years from the 1995 wedding to the calculation date, August 15, 2019, when the other spouse filed for a judicial divorce. That is about seven twenty-fifths. The claim of NT$2,275,331 became an award of NT$1,365,199, and the judgment states that neither side may appeal.

## Two cases where half held

Separation did not move the Tainan Branch of the Taiwan High Court ([113年度重家上字第3號](https://judgment.judicial.gov.tw/FJUD/data.aspx?ty=JD&id=TNHV%2c113%2c%e9%87%8d%e5%ae%b6%e4%b8%8a%2c3%2c20241218%2c1), December 18, 2024). The couple married in 1977 and lived apart from 2015. The land in dispute had been bought before the separation. The spouse resisting the claim said the other had contributed nothing, but that spouse had cared for the children and run a shop, and frequent trips abroad after the separation were within the traveler's means. The court kept the equal split, and on March 26, 2025 the Supreme Court rejected a further appeal as inadmissible ([114年度台上字第565號](https://judgment.judicial.gov.tw/FJUD/data.aspx?ty=JD&id=TPSV%2c114%2c%e5%8f%b0%e4%b8%8a%2c565%2c20250326%2c1)).

Taipei went the same way in the case from the opening ([111年度重家財訴字第10號](https://judgment.judicial.gov.tw/FJUD/data.aspx?ty=JD&id=TPDV%2c111%2c%e9%87%8d%e5%ae%b6%e8%b2%a1%e8%a8%b4%2c10%2c20240227%2c1), February 27, 2024). The heirs pointed to the short marriage, the lack of children and gifts the husband had made to his wife. They lost on all three. The court counted her work as a full-time homemaker who also helped care for her bedridden mother-in-law, and said childlessness was no reason to deny her contribution. As for the gifts, they were already excluded from the calculation by paragraph 1; using them again to cut her share would undermine that exclusion. The judgment was appealed, and the case ended in a mediated settlement at the Taiwan High Court on December 3, 2024; the terms are not public.

## Two years, five years

Neither deadline is generous. A claim expires two years after the claimant learns of the difference, and in any event five years after the regime ends (paragraph 5). For a spouse living abroad who discovers the other's assets late, when those two years started can become the real dispute. The claim cannot be assigned or inherited unless acknowledged by contract or already in litigation (paragraph 4).

Each court gave its own grounds. The Supreme Court faulted the lower court for not examining spending, housework and child care, and the court on remand pointed to the claimant's low contribution after the move into separate rooms. The Taipei court counted the widow's homemaking and care for her mother-in-law, and the Tainan Branch relied on the land having been bought before the separation. The 30% figure, too, came from that case's own years in separate rooms and its own record of contribution.
